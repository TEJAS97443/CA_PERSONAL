## In plain words

### Diwali in ninety days

Every year Tapri's Diwali season needs four times the usual stock of tea, sugar, kulhads and gift boxes, for about three months.

> **Kesar:** Simple. We issue shares for the Diwali stock.
> **Ritu:** Shares are forever, Kesar-ji. The stock is gone by Bhai Dooj.
> **Dev:** And half of it is already financed. The tea estate gives us sixty days, the corporate clients pay half when they order their hampers, and the staff are paid on the seventh of next month.
> **Kesar:** So the money is free?
> **Ritu:** Some of it. Per unit, please, before you celebrate.

A short-term need is one that arises for a period **not exceeding one year**: stock, debtors, cash. By the matching rule of [[fm.sources.long|long-term sources]], it should be met from **short-term sources**.

Some of them arise on their own as the business runs, with no negotiation: the supplier's credit (**trade credit**), wages and taxes not yet paid (**accrued expenses**), money received for goods not yet delivered (**deferred income**), and **advances from customers**. Others must be arranged: a large, highly rated company can borrow from investors by selling **commercial paper**; banks give **loans, overdrafts and cash credits**; exporters get **packing credit** before they ship and **post-shipment finance** after; a sister company with spare cash can lend through an **inter-corporate deposit**; and the public can lend through **public deposits**.

::: analogy The sweet shop before Diwali
A sweet shop before Diwali buys khoya and sugar on a month's udhaar from the wholesaler (trade credit), takes advance payment for the big office orders (customer advances), pays its karigars at month end (accrued wages), and draws on a bank limit against the stock in its godown, paying interest only on what it uses (cash credit). By Bhai Dooj the stock is sold, the udhaar paid, the limit back to zero. Nobody sold a share of the shop.
:::

::: key
Short-term needs are met from short-term sources. Trade credit (without any explicit cost), accrued expenses and deferred income arise spontaneously, and customer advances are cost free; the rest (commercial paper, bank advances, export credit, inter-corporate and public deposits) must be arranged and carry interest.
:::

## How it works

### 1. The spontaneous sources

The Study Material lists ten short-term sources (SM 2.24–2.32). The first three arise in the normal course of business.

**(i) Trade credit** (SM 2.24) is credit granted by suppliers of goods, etc., as an incident of sale.

- Its usual duration is **15 to 90 days**.
- It **generates automatically** in the course of business and is common to almost all business operations.
- It can be in the form of an **open account** or **bills payable**.
- It is preferred because it is **without any explicit cost**, and as long as the business is a going concern it keeps on rotating.
- It **enhances automatically with the increase in the volume of business**.

**(ii) Accrued expenses and deferred (unearned) income** (SM 2.24). Accrued expenses are liabilities for services already received, like wages, taxes, interest and dividends; they arise from day-to-day activities and are a **spontaneous source of finance**. Deferred income is money received for goods and services to be provided in the future; it increases liquidity, so it too is a spontaneous source.

**(iii) Advances from customers** (SM 2.25). Manufacturers and contractors producing costly goods with a long manufacturing or construction time usually demand advance money when they accept the order. It is a **cost free source of finance** and really useful.

::: note Spontaneous and negotiated sources
The working capital chapter sorts working capital finance into **spontaneous sources** (which naturally arise in the course of business operations: trade credit, credit from employees, credit from suppliers of services) and **negotiated sources** (which have to be specifically negotiated with lenders: commercial banks, financial institutions, the general public). Among its sources it adds bills payable (spontaneous), and bill discounting and the bill rediscounting scheme (SM 9.102–9.105): see [[fm.wc.banking|financing of working capital]]. The cost of availing trade credit, and of not taking it, is in [[fm.wc.payables|management of payables]].
:::

### 2. The money market instruments

**(iv) Commercial paper (CP)** (SM 2.25):

- An **unsecured** money market instrument issued in the form of a **promissory note**.
- Introduced by the RBI in **1989**, to enable **highly rated corporate borrowers** to diversify their sources of short-term borrowings and to provide an additional instrument to investors. Besides corporates, **primary dealers and all-India financial institutions** may also issue it.
- Issued in denominations of **₹5 lakh or multiples thereof**; the interest rate is generally linked to the **yield on the one-year government bond**.
- Every eligible issuer must get a **credit rating** from CRISIL, ICRA, CARE, FITCH Ratings India or any other agency specified by the RBI.

The working capital chapter adds that CP matures in a minimum of 7 days to less than one year from the date of issue, and gives its advantages and limitations (SM 9.104–9.105; [[fm.wc.banking|financing of working capital]]).

**(v) Treasury bills (T-bills)** (SM 2.25): a class of **Central Government securities**, issued by the Government of India to meet its short-term borrowing requirements, with maturities ranging **between 14 and 364 days**.

**(vi) Certificates of deposit (CD)** (SM 2.25): a document of title similar to a time deposit receipt, **issued by a bank**, with a fixed maturity of **not less than 7 days up to a maximum of one year**, except that there is no prescribed interest rate. Its main advantage: the banker is not required to encash the deposit before maturity, and the investor is assured of liquidity because he can **sell the CD in the secondary market**.

### 3. Bank advances

Banks lend the deposits they receive so that they can be called back when required; lending yields gross revenue out of which interest on deposits and costs are met and a reasonable profit is made. A bank's lending policy is not merely profit motivated but also keeps in mind the **socio-economic development** of the country (SM 2.26). The facilities (SM 2.26–2.28):

1. **Short-term loans.** The entire advance is **disbursed at one time**, in cash or by transfer to the borrower's current account. It is a single advance against securities like shares, government securities, life insurance policies and fixed deposit receipts. Except for interest and charges, no further adjustments are made; repayment is in full or by an agreed schedule.
2. **Overdraft.** The customer may **withdraw in excess of the credit balance in the current account**, up to a fixed limit. Though **repayable on demand**, overdrafts generally continue for long periods through annual renewals of the limits. Interest is charged on **daily balances**; cheque books are provided.
3. **Clean overdrafts.** Granted only to parties which are financially sound and reputed for integrity: the bank relies on the **personal security of the borrower**, with no tangible security. Banks look at the turnover in the account, satisfactory dealings over a considerable period and market reputation, take guarantees from other creditworthy persons, and grant it for a short period only.
4. **Cash credits.** An advance up to a certain limit; the customer **draws only to the extent of his requirements** and deposits surplus funds in the account, and **interest is charged only on the amount actually availed**. Limits are generally sanctioned against tradable goods by **pledge or hypothecation**; though repayable on demand, banks usually do not recall them unless compelled by adverse factors.
5. **Advances against goods.** A reliable source of repayment: goods are in constant demand and turn over quickly, so advances against them are safe and liquid. Goods (agricultural commodities, industrial raw materials, partly finished goods: all forms of movables) are charged to the bank by pledge or hypothecation.
6. **Bills purchased/discounted.** Advances against the security of clean or documentary bills, for approved customers with sanctioned limits, after the banker is satisfied about the creditworthiness of the drawer. Despite the name, the bank holds the bills **only as security**; besides its rights against the parties liable on the bills, it can exercise a pledgee's rights over the goods covered by the documents.

The working capital chapter's forms of bank credit (line of credit, letter of credit, bank guarantees and more) are in [[fm.wc.banking|financing of working capital]].

### 4. Export finance: before and after the ship sails

Commercial banks provide short-term export finance mainly as **pre-shipment** and **post-shipment** credit, in rupees as well as in foreign currency (SM 2.28). The RBI has eased the flow of export credit through rationalised and liberalised export credit interest rates, flexibility in repayment and prepayment of pre-shipment credit, a special package for large value exporters, export finance for agricultural exports and the Gold Card Scheme, and has allowed banks to source funds from abroad without limit for export credit in foreign currency.

**Pre-shipment finance: packing credit** (SM 2.28–2.30). Packing credit is an advance by a bank to an exporter for **buying, manufacturing, processing, packing and shipping** goods to overseas buyers. An exporter with a **firm export order** or an **irrevocable letter of credit** in his favour can apply. It must be **liquidated within 180 days from the date of its commencement**, by negotiation of export bills or receipt of export proceeds: it is essentially a short-term advance. Banks normally insist on irrevocable letters of credit, which evidence the arrangement for realising the export proceeds and indicate the finance needed; for customers of long standing, firm contracts may do.

Types of packing credit (SM 2.29–2.30):

- (a) **Clean packing credit**: made available only on production of a firm export order or a letter of credit, **without any charge or control over raw material or finished goods**. A suitable margin is kept, and the bank obtains **ECGC cover**.
- (b) **Packing credit against hypothecation of goods**: the goods are hypothecated to the bank with a stipulated margin; the exporter submits stock statements with the order or letter of credit, and then every fortnight and whenever stocks move.
- (c) **Packing credit against pledge of goods**: the exportable finished goods are pledged to the bank with approved clearing agents, who ship them as required; **possession lies with the bank**, under its lock and key.
- (d) **ECGC guarantee**: any loan to an exporter for the manufacture, processing, purchasing or packing of goods for export against a firm order qualifies for the packing credit guarantee of the Export Credit Guarantee Corporation.
- (e) **Forward exchange contract**: if the export bill is to be drawn in a foreign currency, the exporter should enter into a forward exchange contract with the bank, avoiding the risk of a change in the exchange rate.

**Post-shipment finance** takes these forms (SM 2.30–2.31):

- (a) **Purchase/discounting of documentary export bills**: export bills payable at sight are purchased, and usance export bills discounted, covering confirmed sales and backed by documents including documents of title such as the bill of lading, post parcel receipts or air consignment notes.
- (b) **ECGC guarantee**: post-shipment finance through purchase, negotiation or discount of an export bill qualifies for the post-shipment export credit guarantee, provided the exporter holds ECGC's shipments (comprehensive risks) policy covering both **political and commercial risks**. ECGC fixes a credit limit for each exporter, and its liability is limited to that limit, irrespective of the amount of the policy.
- (c) **Advance against export bills sent for collection**, considering the creditworthiness of the party, the nature of goods, usance and the standing of the drawee.
- (d) **Advance against duty drawbacks, cash subsidy, etc.** receivable against export performance. These are **clean** advances, so precaution is needed; the bank sees that the export bills pass through it, so that it can verify the claims. Such an advance must be liquidated **within 180 days from the date of shipment**.

Banks also (SM 2.31): (i) open letters of credit for approved exporters on their overseas or up-country suppliers; (ii) issue guarantees for waiver of excise duty, due performance of contracts, bonds in lieu of cash security deposits and advance payments; (iii) finance exports on deferred payment terms; (iv) obtain status reports on buyers and trade information through correspondents; and (v) provide economic intelligence on various countries.

### 5. Inter-corporate deposits and public deposits

**(ix) Inter-corporate deposits (ICDs)** (SM 2.31): companies can borrow for a short period, **say 6 months**, from other companies which have **surplus liquidity**; the rate varies with the amount and the period. The working capital chapter adds that the rate is higher than the bank rate, depends on the financial soundness of the borrower, and that the source reduces dependence on bank finance (SM 9.104).

**(x) Public deposits** (SM 2.31–2.32): a very important source of **short-term and medium-term** finance, particularly due to the credit squeeze by the RBI.

- A company can accept public deposits, subject to RBI stipulations from time to time, up to a ==maximum of 35 per cent of its paid up capital and reserves==.
- They may be accepted for a period of **six months to three years**.
- They are **unsecured loans**, and should **not be used for acquiring fixed assets**, since they are repayable within three years; they mainly finance working capital.

::: warning Version-sensitive limits
The 35% limit and the six-month to three-year period are as the Study Material states them (SM 2.31–2.32). Rules on deposits change: verify against the Study Material and Statutory Update for your attempt.
:::

## Worked examples

:::: example Kesar's Diwali shopping list
For the Diwali season Tapri needs finance for five things. Identify the most suitable short-term source for each, with reasons from its features.

(a) 2 lakh extra kulhads from the potters' co-operative, which will accept Tapri's bills of exchange payable in 60 days.

(b) Twelve custom-built festival kiosks that Tapri's workshop makes for franchisees over ten weeks; the franchisees will pay 40% of the price when they place the order.

(c) ₹25 crore for four months. Tapri is highly rated by a credit rating agency and wants to borrow directly from investors, unsecured, at a rate linked to the one-year government bond yield.

(d) ₹8 crore for six months. Chai Express Logistics Ltd, Tapri's subsidiary, has surplus cash for exactly that period.

(e) A running limit for buying sugar and packaging as the season goes, secured on that stock, paying interest only on what is used.

::: answer Model answer
**(a) Trade credit, in the form of bills payable.** Credit granted by suppliers as an incident of sale, usually for 15 to 90 days, in the form of an open account or bills payable; it arises automatically in the course of business, without any explicit cost, and grows with the volume of business. Sixty-day bills accepted by Tapri are bills payable, inside the usual duration.

**(b) Advances from customers.** Manufacturers and contractors producing costly goods with a considerable manufacturing or construction time usually demand advance money from customers when they accept the order: a cost free source of finance. The kiosks are costly, custom-built over ten weeks, and the franchisees pay 40% with the order.

**(c) Commercial paper.** An unsecured money market instrument issued as a promissory note, which enables highly rated corporate borrowers to diversify their short-term borrowings; issued in denominations of ₹5 lakh or multiples, with interest generally linked to the yield on the one-year government bond, after a rating from an agency such as CRISIL, ICRA, CARE or FITCH Ratings India. ₹25 crore is 500 units of ₹5 lakh.

**(d) Inter-corporate deposit.** Companies can borrow for a short period, say 6 months, from other companies with surplus liquidity, at a rate that varies with the amount and the period.

**(e) Cash credit.** An advance up to a limit, drawn only to the extent of requirements, with interest charged only on the amount actually availed; generally sanctioned against tradable goods by pledge or hypothecation.
:::

:::: example The Dubai premix order
Tapri has a firm order from a Dubai distributor for 40 tonnes of chai premix, payable in US dollars, backed by an irrevocable letter of credit. It needs money now to buy, make and pack the premix, and will ship in ten weeks; the buyer pays 60 days after the bill of lading. It can hypothecate the premix stock to its bank.

**Required:**

(i) Identify the finance Tapri needs before shipment and after shipment.

(ii) Explain the form of pre-shipment finance that suits Tapri, and the precaution the dollar bill calls for.

(iii) Explain any two forms of post-shipment finance open to Tapri, and the period within which the pre-shipment advance must be repaid.

::: answer Model answer
**(i)** Before shipment, **pre-shipment finance**, which generally takes the form of packing credit; after shipment, while the 60-day bill is outstanding, **post-shipment finance**.

**(ii) Packing credit against hypothecation of goods.** Packing credit is an advance by a bank to an exporter for buying, manufacturing, processing, packing and shipping goods to overseas buyers, available to an exporter with a firm export order or an irrevocable letter of credit, both of which Tapri has. Since Tapri can hypothecate the premix stock, it suits packing credit against hypothecation of goods: the goods are hypothecated to the bank with a stipulated margin, and Tapri submits stock statements with the order or letter of credit, then every fortnight and whenever stocks move. As the export bill will be drawn in US dollars, Tapri should enter into a **forward exchange contract** with the bank to avoid the risk of a change in the exchange rate.

**(iii) Post-shipment finance.**

1. *Purchase/discounting of documentary export bills*: the bank discounts the usance (60-day) export bill covering the confirmed sale, backed by documents including the bill of lading.
2. *ECGC guarantee*: post-shipment finance through purchase, negotiation or discount of an export bill qualifies for the post-shipment export credit guarantee if Tapri obtains ECGC's shipments (comprehensive risks) policy covering political and commercial risks.

Other forms: advance against export bills sent for collection, and advance against duty drawbacks and cash subsidy. The packing credit must be **liquidated within 180 days from the date of its commencement**, by negotiation of the export bills or receipt of the export proceeds.
:::

## Under the hood

### Free is not the same as costless

The Study Material calls trade credit "without any explicit cost" and customer advances "cost free". ICAI's answer to RTP May 2024 Q8(ii) adds that trade credit is not truly cost-free: it involves an **implicit cost**, because the supplier bears the opportunity cost of funds tied up in receivables and generally passes it on through a higher price or by not extending a cash discount. When a cash discount is offered, the cost of passing it up can be computed, as in [[fm.wc.payables|management of payables]].

### Why public deposits must not buy machines

A deposit repayable within three years funding a machine that pays back over ten is the matching rule broken in the other direction: the depositors want their money back long before the machine has earned it.

### The loan, the overdraft and the cash credit

| Point | Short-term loan | Overdraft | Cash credit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Disbursed | All at once | As drawn, within a limit | As drawn, within a limit |
| Interest on | The whole advance | Daily balances | Amount actually availed |
| Against | Shares, securities, policies, FDRs | A limit on the current account | Tradable goods, pledged or hypothecated |
| Repayment | In full or by schedule | On demand; renewed yearly | On demand; rarely recalled |

::: exam The wording traps
- "Without any explicit cost" (trade credit) is not "without cost": ICAI's answers point to its implicit cost.
- Packing credit: 180 days **from commencement**; advance against duty drawback: 180 days **from the date of shipment**.
- Clean packing credit has **no charge** over the goods; it still needs a firm order or letter of credit, a margin and ECGC cover.
- T-bills are issued by the **Government of India**, CDs by **banks**, CP by **highly rated corporates**, primary dealers and all-India financial institutions.
:::

::: trick Pre is the factory, post is the ship's paper
Pre-shipment finance pays for buying, making and packing the goods (packing credit, in its five types); post-shipment finance is raised on the export bill and its documents once the goods have sailed (purchase or discount, ECGC guarantee, collection, duty drawback). Condition: it sorts export credit by the moment of shipment; the other facilities (letters of credit, guarantees, status reports) are neither.
:::

## Answer frame

### "Show the difference between trade credit and bank overdraft" (RTP September 2025 Q10(b))

::: compare
| Point | Trade credit | Bank overdraft |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Credit extended by suppliers, allowing the firm to delay payment for goods or services | The customer may withdraw in excess of the credit balance in the current account, up to a limit |
| Period | Usually 15 to 90 days | Repayable on demand, but generally continues for long periods by annual renewal |
| Cost | A type of interest-free loan, so failing to avail it has an interest cost | Interest charged on daily balances |
| How it arises | Automatically, in the course of business | Applied for; operated like cash credit and current accounts |
| Growth | Grows automatically with the volume of business | A fixed limit, within which the borrower draws |
:::

### "Describe the types of packing credit" and "Explain the forms of post-shipment finance"

The five types in ICAI's order (clean; against hypothecation; against pledge; ECGC guarantee; forward exchange contract), each with its distinguishing line; then the 180-day rule. For post-shipment, the four forms (purchase/discounting of documentary export bills; ECGC guarantee; advance against bills for collection; advance against duty drawbacks and cash subsidy), then the 180-days-from-shipment rule for the last. A 1 + 3 question like May 2026 Q4(b) wants the identification first, in one line.

### "Explain in brief the features of commercial paper"

Unsecured promissory note → RBI scheme of 1989 for highly rated corporate borrowers (and primary dealers, all-India financial institutions) → ₹5 lakh or multiples → interest linked to the one-year government bond yield → compulsory credit rating (CRISIL, ICRA, CARE, FITCH Ratings India or another agency specified by the RBI).

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: shares for the Diwali stock
Financing a three-month seasonal stock with a share issue or a long-term loan. **Fix:** short-term needs from short-term sources; for permanent, hard-core working capital the long-term sources are right, but for the seasonal peak they leave idle, costly money after Bhai Dooj.
:::

::: mistake Clean packing credit "secured on the goods"
Writing that clean packing credit is secured by hypothecation. **Fix:** clean packing credit has no charge or control over raw material or finished goods; hypothecation and pledge are the second and third types.
:::

::: mistake The overdraft and the cash credit treated as one
**Fix:** an overdraft is a limit on a current account with interest on daily balances; a cash credit is a limit sanctioned against tradable goods by pledge or hypothecation, interest on the amount actually availed. Both are repayable on demand.
:::

::: mistake Trade credit called "free"
**Fix:** "without any explicit cost" (SM 2.24), but with an implicit cost passed on in the price or the lost cash discount.
:::

## Recap

- Ten short-term sources (SM order): trade credit, accrued expenses and deferred income, advances from customers, commercial paper, treasury bills, certificates of deposit, bank advances, export finance, inter-corporate deposits and public deposits.
- Trade credit: 15–90 days, open account or bills payable, automatic, no explicit cost, grows with business. Accruals and deferred income: spontaneous. Customer advances: cost free.
- CP: unsecured promissory note, RBI 1989, highly rated issuers, ₹5 lakh multiples, linked to the one-year government bond yield, rated. T-bills: Government of India, 14–364 days. CD: bank, 7 days to one year, saleable in the secondary market.
- Bank advances: loan (all at once), overdraft (daily balances), clean overdraft (personal security), cash credit (interest on amount availed, against goods), advances against goods, bills purchased/discounted (bills held as security).
- Packing credit: firm order or irrevocable L/C; liquidated within 180 days of commencement; five types (clean, hypothecation, pledge, ECGC guarantee, forward contract).
- Post-shipment: bills purchased/discounted, ECGC guarantee, advance against bills for collection, advance against duty drawback (within 180 days of shipment).
- ICDs: say 6 months, from companies with surplus liquidity. Public deposits: up to 35% of paid-up capital and reserves, 6 months to 3 years, unsecured, not for fixed assets. (The stock is gone by Bhai Dooj; so is the cash credit.)

## Flashcards

Q:: State the features of trade credit.
A:: Credit granted by suppliers as an incident of sale; usually 15 to 90 days; generates automatically and is common to almost all businesses; open account or bills payable; without any explicit cost and keeps rotating while the business is a going concern; enhances automatically with the volume of business.

Q:: Why are accrued expenses and deferred income called spontaneous sources of finance?
A:: Accrued expenses (wages, taxes, interest, dividends for services already received) arise out of day-to-day activities; deferred income (money received for goods and services to be provided in future) increases liquidity. Both arise without negotiation in the course of business.

Q:: Explain in brief the features of commercial paper.
A:: An unsecured money market instrument issued as a promissory note; RBI scheme of 1989 for highly rated corporate borrowers (also primary dealers and all-India financial institutions); denominations of ₹5 lakh or multiples; interest generally linked to the one-year government bond yield; credit rating from CRISIL, ICRA, CARE, FITCH or another RBI-specified agency.

Q:: What is a certificate of deposit, and its main advantage?
A:: A document of title like a time deposit receipt issued by a bank, maturity not less than 7 days up to one year, with no prescribed interest rate. The banker need not encash it before maturity, and the investor has liquidity by selling it in the secondary market.

Q:: Treasury bills: issuer and maturity?
A:: A class of Central Government securities issued by the Government of India for short-term borrowing, with maturities of 14 to 364 days.

Q:: Cash credit versus overdraft?
A:: Overdraft: withdrawal beyond the current account balance up to a limit, interest on daily balances, repayable on demand but renewed annually. Cash credit: a limit drawn as required, interest only on the amount availed, generally against tradable goods by pledge or hypothecation.

Q:: What is a clean overdraft?
A:: An advance relying on the personal security of a financially sound borrower of known integrity, with no tangible security; banks look at turnover and dealings, take guarantees of creditworthy persons, and grant it for a short period only.

Q:: What is packing credit, and within what period must it be liquidated?
A:: A pre-shipment advance by a bank to an exporter with a firm export order or irrevocable letter of credit, for buying, manufacturing, processing, packing and shipping goods; liquidated within 180 days from its commencement by negotiation of export bills or receipt of export proceeds.

Q:: Name the five types of packing credit.
A:: Clean packing credit; packing credit against hypothecation of goods; packing credit against pledge of goods; ECGC guarantee; forward exchange contract.

Q:: Name the four forms of post-shipment finance.
A:: Purchase/discounting of documentary export bills; ECGC guarantee; advance against export bills sent for collection; advance against duty drawbacks, cash subsidy, etc.

Q:: What are inter-corporate deposits?
A:: Short-period borrowings, say 6 months, from other companies with surplus liquidity, at a rate that varies with the amount and the period.

Q:: Public deposits: limit, period and use, as the Study Material states them?
A:: Up to 35% of paid-up capital and reserves, subject to RBI stipulations; for six months to three years; unsecured; not to be used for fixed assets, mainly for working capital.
