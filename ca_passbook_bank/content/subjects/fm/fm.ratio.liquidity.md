## In plain words

### Three questions from the bank

Tapri Tea Co. Ltd wants a ₹50 crore term loan from Narmada Bank for a new cold-chain fleet. The bank's credit officer has sent back one page: "Please give us your ratios."

> **Kesar:** Our ratio is simple: more chai, more money. Write that.
> **Dev:** They want three answers, Kesar-ji. Can we pay what falls due this year? Who paid for the business, we or the lenders? And how many times do our profits cover the interest and the instalments?
> **Kesar:** Tell them sab adjust ho jayega.
> **Dev:** The bank discounts that sentence to zero.

Dev opens Tapri's balance sheet at 31 March 2026. Within the next year Tapri must pay ₹70 crore (suppliers, unpaid expenses, tax); those are its **current liabilities**. Within the same year it expects ₹150 crore to turn into cash (stock, money owed by customers, cash itself); those are its **current assets**. ₹150 crore against ₹70 crore is ₹2.14 of current assets for every rupee due: that is the **current ratio**, the first test of **liquidity**, the ability to pay short-term liabilities.

Some of that ₹150 crore is stock that must first be sold. Take out stock (and expenses paid in advance, which no supplier will accept as payment) and ₹85 crore is left: ₹1.21 for every rupee due. That stricter test is the **quick ratio**.

The second question is about the long run. Owners have put in ₹300 crore (share capital plus retained profits); lenders ₹110 crore (debentures and a term loan). The **debt-equity ratio** of 0.37 says lenders have ₹37 in the business for every ₹100 of owners' money. Ratios like it, which show the mix of owners' and lenders' funds, are **capital structure ratios**.

The third question is about paying lenders. Tapri's profit before interest and tax is ₹132 crore and its interest ₹12 crore: profits cover interest 11 times. Ratios like this, which compare earnings with fixed charges, are **coverage ratios**.

::: analogy The household loan file
A bank deciding on your home loan asks the same three things. Can you pay this month's bills from your salary account and what friends owe you (the current ratio), without selling the family gold (the quick ratio)? How much of the house is yours and how much is the bank's (the debt-equity ratio)? How many times does your income cover the interest (interest coverage), and the whole EMI with the principal in it (debt service coverage)?
:::

::: key
A single figure says little; a figure compared with another says a lot. Liquidity ratios test whether short-term liabilities can be paid from assets that turn into cash within the year; capital structure ratios show how much of the business owners fund and how much lenders; coverage ratios show how many times earnings cover interest, instalments and preference dividend.
:::

## How it works

### What a ratio is, and where its numbers come from

ICAI defines a ratio as "the indicated quotient of two mathematical expressions and as the relationship between two or more things"; a financial (accounting) ratio is a mathematical expression of the relationship between two accounting figures (SM 3.3). **Ratio analysis** rests on the fact that a single accounting figure by itself may not communicate any meaningful information, but expressed relative to some other figure it may. Its calculation basis and objective (SM 3.3):

- **Calculation basis:** a relationship expressed in mathematical terms, between two individual figures or group of figures, connected with each other in some logical manner, selected from the financial statements of the concern.
- **Objective:** all stakeholders (owners, investors, lenders, employees etc.) can draw conclusions about the performance (past, present and future) and the strengths and weaknesses of a firm, and can take decisions in relation to the firm.

A ratio means most when it is compared: with the firm's own previous years (**intra-firm comparison**), with other companies (**inter-firm comparison**), with the industry, or even with the economy in general (SM 3.3). The sources of information for financial statement analysis are (SM 3.4): (i) annual reports, (ii) interim financial statements, (iii) notes to accounts, (iv) statement of cash flows, (v) business periodicals, (vi) credit and investment advisory services.

### Liquidity: three tests, each stricter than the last

The terms "liquidity" and "short-term solvency" are used synonymously. Inability to pay short-term liabilities affects a business's credibility and credit rating, and continuous default leads to commercial bankruptcy. ==Both lack of sufficient liquidity and excess liquidity is bad for the organization== (SM 3.5). Tapri's current assets, and the three tests drawn from them:

::: steps
1. **Current ratio** = current assets ÷ current liabilities = 150 ÷ 70 = **2.14**. It asks: "Does your business have enough current assets to meet the payment schedule of its current debts with a margin of safety for possible losses in current assets?" A generally acceptable current ratio is 2:1, but whether a ratio is satisfactory depends on the nature of the business and the characteristics of its current assets and liabilities (SM 3.5–3.6).
2. **Quick ratio** (acid-test ratio) = quick assets ÷ current liabilities, where quick assets = current assets − inventories − prepaid expenses = 150 − 60 − 5 = 85; 85 ÷ 70 = **1.21**. Inventories are left out because they are not near-cash and, in financial difficulty, may sell only at liquidation value (though in a seller's market they are near-cash too). An acid-test of 1:1 is considered satisfactory unless most quick assets are receivables whose collection lags behind the schedule for paying current liabilities (SM 3.6).
3. **Cash ratio** (absolute liquidity ratio) = (cash and bank balances + marketable securities or current investments) ÷ current liabilities = (30 + 15) ÷ 70 = **0.64**. It tests liquidity in terms of cash and marketable securities only (SM 3.7).
4. **Basic defense interval** = quick assets ÷ daily operating expenses. Daily operating expenses = (cost of goods sold + selling, administration and other general expenses − depreciation and other non-cash expenditure) ÷ days in the year. Tapri: (₹540 + ₹228 − ₹36) crore ÷ 360 = ₹2.03 crore a day, so 85 ÷ 2.03 = **41.8 days**: if all revenue stopped tomorrow, Tapri could pay its cash expenses for about 42 days without new financing (SM 3.7).
5. **Net working capital** = current assets − current liabilities = 150 − 70 = **₹80 crore**. It is more a measure of cash flow than a ratio; it should be positive, though in certain business models it may be negative. Bankers watch it over time to judge the ability to weather financial crises, and loans are often tied to a minimum working capital (SM 3.8).
:::

### Long-term solvency: who funded the business?

**Leverage ratios** measure the long-term stability and capital structure of the firm. They show the mix of funds provided by owners and lenders and assure long-term lenders about (i) periodic payment of interest during the loan and (ii) repayment of principal on maturity (SM 3.8). They are of two types: capital structure ratios and coverage ratios.

**Capital structure ratios** show the relative weight of the different sources of funds, which the balance sheet shows only in absolute amounts (SM 3.9). Tapri's long-term side at 31 March 2026 (₹ crore):

| Equity and liabilities | ₹ crore | Assets | ₹ crore |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Equity share capital (₹10 shares) | 40 | Fixed assets (net) | 355 |
| Reserves and surplus | 260 | Current assets | 150 |
| 8% Preference share capital | 25 | | |
| 10% Debentures | 60 | | |
| 12% Term loan | 50 | | |
| Current liabilities | 70 | | |
| **Total** | **505** | **Total** | **505** |

From it: shareholders' equity (equity capital + reserves) = ₹300 crore; total debt (debentures + term loan) = ₹110 crore; total outside liabilities (debt + current liabilities) = ₹180 crore; net assets (fixed assets + net current assets) = 355 + 80 = ₹435 crore, which is also equity 300 + preference 25 + debt 110 (that is why ICAI calls it **capital employed**).

| Ratio | Tapri's working | Result |
|---|---|---:|
| Equity ratio | 300 ÷ 435 | 0.69 |
| Debt ratio | 110 ÷ 435 | 0.25 |
| Debt to equity (total outside liabilities) | 180 ÷ 300 | 0.60 |
| Debt to equity (long-term debt) | 110 ÷ 300 | 0.37 |
| Debt to total assets | 180 ÷ 505 | 0.36 |
| Capital gearing | (25 + 60 + 50) ÷ 300 | 0.45 |
| Proprietary ratio | (40 + 25 + 260) ÷ 505 | 0.64 |

Read them as: owners fund 69% of net assets and debt 25%; ₹60 is owed (₹37 of it borrowed long-term) for every ₹100 the shareholders own; outsiders finance 36% of total assets; fixed-charge capital is ₹0.45 for every rupee of equity; shareholders finance 64% of total assets.

Higher equity and proprietary ratios mean a lower risk for lenders; higher debt, debt-equity, debt to total assets and gearing ratios mean more financial leverage and more risk (SM 3.9–3.11). **Debt equity ratio is the indicator of firm's financial leverage**, and it is very often used for capital structure decisions such as issuing shares or debentures (SM 3.10).

### Coverage: how many times do earnings pay the fixed claims?

**Coverage ratios** measure the firm's ability to service its fixed liabilities: (i) interest on loans, (ii) preference dividend, (iii) amortisation of principal, repayment of loan instalments or redemption of preference capital (SM 3.11). Tapri's year to 31 March 2026 (₹ crore): sales 900, cost of goods sold 540, operating expenses 228 (depreciation 36 included), so EBIT 132; interest 12 (10% on ₹60 crore debentures and 12% on the ₹50 crore term loan); tax at 25%, so profit after tax (EAT) 90; preference dividend 2; equity dividend ₹5.50 a share on 4 crore shares, ₹22; term loan instalment due ₹10.

::: steps
1. **Interest coverage ratio** ("times interest earned") = EBIT ÷ interest = 132 ÷ 12 = **11 times**. EBIT is used because interest is a deductible expense, so the tax burden does not affect the ability to pay it. A high ratio means interest can be met even if EBIT falls considerably; a low one indicates excessive use of debt or inefficient operations (SM 3.12).
2. **Debt service coverage ratio (DSCR)** = earnings available for debt service ÷ (interest + instalments), where earnings available = EAT + depreciation and other non-cash operating expenses + interest + other adjustments such as loss on sale of a fixed asset: (90 + 36 + 12) ÷ (12 + 10) = 138 ÷ 22 = **6.27 times**. Lenders use it to judge the firm's ability to pay current interest and instalments; normally a DSCR of 1.5 to 2 is satisfactory (SM 3.11–3.12).
3. **Preference dividend coverage ratio** = EAT ÷ preference dividend = 90 ÷ 2 = **45 times**: the margin of safety for preference shareholders (SM 3.12–3.13). The **equity dividend coverage ratio** = (EAT − preference dividend) ÷ equity dividend = 88 ÷ 22 = **4 times**.
4. **Fixed charges coverage ratio** = (EBIT + depreciation) ÷ (interest + repayment of loan) = (132 + 36) ÷ 22 = **7.64 times**: how many times the cash flow before interest and taxes covers all fixed financing charges; more than 1 is considered safe (SM 3.13).
:::

The rule behind the right-hand branch: with a current ratio above 1, adding the same amount to both sides pulls the ratio towards 1 (it falls), and removing the same amount from both pushes it away from 1 (it rises). Paying ₹10 crore of creditors from the bank takes Tapri from 150 ÷ 70 = 2.14 to 140 ÷ 60 = 2.33. Collecting ₹8 crore from debtors only swaps one current asset for another: 2.14 stays 2.14.

::: definition Ratio (SM 3.3)
"The indicated quotient of two mathematical expressions and as the relationship between two or more things." A financial or accounting ratio is a mathematical expression of the relationship between two accounting figures.
:::

::: formula Current ratio and quick (acid-test) ratio
$$\text{Current ratio} = \frac{\text{Current assets}}{\text{Current liabilities}} \qquad \text{Quick ratio} = \frac{\text{Quick assets}}{\text{Current liabilities}}$$
Quick assets = current assets − inventories − prepaid expenses. ICAI's norms: 2:1 and 1:1 (SM 3.38).
:::

::: formula Cash ratio (absolute liquidity ratio)
$$\text{Cash ratio} \qquad = \frac{\text{Cash} + \text{Marketable securities}}{\text{Current liabilities}}$$
Cash = cash and bank balances; current investments may replace marketable securities (SM 3.7).
:::

::: formula Basic defense interval (interval measure)
$$\text{BDI} = \frac{C + R + M}{\text{Daily operating expenses}} \qquad = \frac{\text{CA} - \text{Prepaid} - \text{Inventories}}{\text{Daily operating expenses}}$$
C = cash and bank balances, R = net receivables, M = marketable securities; CA = current assets; prepaid = prepaid expenses (SM 3.7).
$$\text{Daily operating expenses} \qquad = \frac{\text{COGS} + S - N}{\text{No. of days in a year}}$$
S = selling, administration and other general expenses; N = depreciation and other non-cash expenditure. The answer is in days; take 360 days unless the question says otherwise.
:::

::: formula Net working capital
$$\text{NWC} = \text{CA} - \text{CL}$$
NWC = net working capital; CA = current assets; CL = current liabilities.
The Study Material's definition adds "(excluding short-term bank borrowing)" to current liabilities (SM 3.8); see Under the hood.
:::

::: formula Equity ratio and debt ratio
$$\text{Equity ratio} = \frac{\text{Shareholders’ equity}}{\text{Net assets}} \qquad \text{Debt ratio} = \frac{\text{Total debt}}{\text{Net assets}}$$
Shareholders' equity = equity share capital + reserves and surplus (excluding fictitious assets). Net assets (capital employed) = net fixed assets + net current assets. Total debt = short and long-term borrowings from financial institutions, debentures and bonds, deferred payment arrangements for capital equipment, bank borrowings, public deposits and any other interest-bearing loan (SM 3.9–3.10).
:::

::: formula Debt to equity ratio
$$\text{D/E} = \frac{\text{Total outside liabilities}}{\text{Shareholders’ equity}} \qquad \text{or} \qquad \frac{\text{Long-term debt}}{\text{Shareholders’ equity}}$$
The first counts both current and non-current liabilities; sometimes only interest-bearing long-term debt is used (SM 3.10).
:::

::: formula Debt to total assets, capital gearing and proprietary ratio
$$\text{Debt to total assets} \qquad = \frac{\text{Total outside liabilities}}{\text{Total assets}}$$
$$\text{Capital gearing} = \frac{P + D + B}{E + R - L}$$
$$\text{Proprietary ratio} = \frac{\text{Proprietary fund}}{\text{Total assets}}$$
Debt to total assets may use total debt in the numerator. For gearing, P = preference share capital, D = debentures, B = other borrowed funds, E = equity share capital, R = reserves and surplus, L = losses. Proprietary fund = equity share capital + preference share capital + reserves and surplus; total assets exclude fictitious assets and losses (SM 3.10–3.11).
:::

::: formula Coverage ratios
$$\text{Interest coverage} = \frac{\text{EBIT}}{\text{Interest}} \qquad \text{DSCR} = \frac{\text{Earnings for debt service}}{\text{Interest} + \text{Instalments}}$$
Earnings (available) for debt service = EAT + depreciation and other non-cash operating expenses + interest + other adjustments such as loss on sale of fixed assets (SM 3.11–3.12).
$$\text{Preference dividend coverage} \qquad = \frac{\text{EAT}}{\text{Preference dividend}}$$
$$\text{Equity dividend coverage} \qquad = \frac{\text{EAT} - \text{Preference dividend}}{\text{Equity dividend}}$$
$$\text{Fixed charges coverage} \qquad = \frac{\text{EBIT} + \text{Depreciation}}{\text{Interest} + \text{Repayment of loan}}$$
ICAI's norms (SM 3.39): DSCR 2; interest, preference dividend and fixed charges coverage above 1.
:::

::: note ICAI's notes for calculating ratios (SM 3.13)
1. EBIT = PBIT (profit before interest and taxes); EAT = PAT (profit after taxes); EBT = PBT (profit before taxes).
2. Ratios shall be calculated based on requirement and availability of information and may deviate from the original formulae. If required, assumptions should be given.
3. The numerator should be taken in correspondence with the denominator, and vice versa.
:::

:::: example Mehra Masala Traders: what the two ratios hide
Kesar's cousin runs Mehra Masala Traders, which supplies spice mixes to Tapri. Its working capital is ₹18 lakh, current ratio 2.5 and quick ratio 1.4. Current assets include prepaid rent of ₹1.20 lakh. Cash operating expenses are ₹1.44 crore a year.

**Required:**

(i) Calculate current assets, current liabilities, quick assets and inventory.

(ii) Calculate the basic defense interval (360 days).

(iii) The cousin wants to "bring the current ratio down to a sensible 2" by buying stock on credit. Calculate the stock to be bought, and the quick ratio afterwards.

::: answer Show the working
**(i) Current assets and current liabilities**

Current ratio 2.5 means CA = 2.5 CL, so working capital = CA − CL = 1.5 CL.

$$1.5\,\text{CL} = 18 \qquad \text{CL} = \text{₹}12 \text{ lakh} \qquad \text{CA} = 2.5 \times 12 = \text{₹}30 \text{ lakh}$$

Quick assets = 1.4 × 12 = **₹16.80 lakh**. Inventory = CA − quick assets − prepaid = 30 − 16.80 − 1.20 = **₹12 lakh**.

**(ii) Basic defense interval**

Daily cash operating expenses = ₹144 lakh ÷ 360 = ₹0.40 lakh. BDI = 16.80 ÷ 0.40 = **42 days**.

**(iii) Stock bought on credit**

Buying stock of ₹x on credit adds x to both sides:

$$\frac{30 + x}{12 + x} = 2 \qquad 30 + x = 24 + 2x \qquad x = \text{₹}6 \text{ lakh}$$

After the purchase, quick assets are unchanged at ₹16.80 lakh and current liabilities are ₹18 lakh: quick ratio = 16.80 ÷ 18 = **0.93**. The current ratio did come down to 2, but the firm is less liquid, not more sensible: the new stock is not a quick asset and the new creditors must be paid in cash.
:::

:::: example Seven entries and one current ratio
Tapri's current assets are ₹150 crore and current liabilities ₹70 crore (current ratio 2.14). State, with reasons, whether each transaction below improves, reduces or leaves unchanged the current ratio. Take each transaction separately.

(i) Paid trade payables of ₹10 crore by cheque.

(ii) Bought premix stock of ₹5 crore on credit.

(iii) Raised a five-year term loan of ₹20 crore; the money is kept in the bank.

(iv) Sold stock costing ₹6 crore for ₹9 crore on credit.

(v) Collected ₹8 crore from debtors.

(vi) Bought a delivery van for ₹4 crore in cash.

(vii) Wrote off a debt of ₹2 crore as bad.

| (v) ₹8 crore collected from debtors | Debtors fall, cash rises: total CA unchanged | Unchanged (2.14) |
| (vi) Van bought for ₹4 crore cash | Cash (current) falls; the van is a fixed asset | Reduces (146 ÷ 70 = 2.09) |
| (vii) Bad debt of ₹2 crore written off | Debtors fall; no current liability changes | Reduces (148 ÷ 70 = 2.11) |

## Under the hood

### Why stock and prepaid expenses are not quick

Quick assets are only cash and near-cash assets. Inventory must first be sold, and in financial difficulty it may fetch only its liquidation value; a prepaid expense is a service already bought, which no creditor will accept as payment. In a seller's market, ICAI notes, inventories are also near-cash (SM 3.6), which is why the norm is a rule of thumb and not a law. The thumb rules for working capital (a current ratio of 2 for a manufacturer, a quick ratio of at least 1) and why the optimum depends on the business are in [[fm.wc.basics|working capital basics]].

### Which "debt" is in the debt-equity ratio?

The Study Material prints three versions (SM 3.10): total outside liabilities (current liabilities included), total debt, and long-term (interest-bearing) debt only. ICAI's own solutions follow the question: when the question gives "Debt to Equity 1.5:1" next to a figure called "12.5% Debt", the debt is that loan (May 2024 Q1(a)); when a balance sheet is analysed for a lender, short-term and long-term debt may both be counted (Illustration 5, SM 3.56). In the Study Material's Practical Problem 7 (SM 3.87) total assets are equity + preference capital + debt + current liabilities, so preference capital sits in neither debt nor equity. ==State the definition you use== in one line before the figure.

### Two numerators for the DSCR

The Study Material's earnings available for debt service start from profit after tax and add back non-cash expenses and interest; its footnote allows "fund from operations (or cash from operations) before interest and taxes" instead, as the requirement demands, and lease rentals may be added to both numerator and denominator (SM 3.12). RTP January 2025 Q4 used EBIT + depreciation ("cash profit before interest and tax") for the DSCR. Tapri on that basis: (132 + 36) ÷ 22 = 7.64 instead of 6.27. Name the basis.

### Net working capital and the bank overdraft

The definition at SM 3.8 reads current assets − current liabilities "(excluding short-term bank borrowing)", which treats the bank's own short-term credit as a source of working capital rather than a claim on it. The summary table (SM 3.38) and the worked problems use current assets − all current liabilities: in Practical Problem 6 (SM 3.84–3.85) the bank overdraft is part of the current liabilities from which working capital is found. Follow the question's data, and say so if you exclude bank borrowing.

### Interest coverage and the degree of financial leverage

With no preference dividend, DFL = EBIT ÷ (EBIT − interest). Divide top and bottom by interest and it becomes ICR ÷ (ICR − 1), so ICR = DFL ÷ (DFL − 1): a DFL of 1.10 means EBIT covers interest 11 times, as at Tapri (132 ÷ 120 = 1.10). The leverage itself is in [[fm.lev.leverage|operating, financial and combined leverage]].

- Two equations, two unknowns: a current ratio and a quick ratio together give current liabilities only after inventory is known (January 2026 Q1(a)); a current ratio with working capital gives CL = WC ÷ (CR − 1).

::: trick Working capital to current liabilities in one line
CL = working capital ÷ (current ratio − 1), and CA = CL × current ratio. Then inventory = CA − (quick ratio × CL) − prepaid expenses. Conditions: the working capital is CA − all CL, and quick assets exclude inventory and prepaid expenses.

### "Discuss liquidity ratios" and "Discuss gearing ratio"

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: the prepaid insurance that paid a supplier
Quick assets taken as current assets − inventory only. Malwa's quick ratio becomes 1.00 instead of 0.95, and "adverse" turns into "satisfactory". **Fix:** deduct inventories **and** prepaid expenses (SM 3.6).
:::

::: mistake Interest coverage on profit after tax
Dividing PAT (₹24 lakh) by interest gives 6 times instead of 9. **Fix:** the numerator is EBIT, because interest is paid before tax and is tax-deductible.
:::

::: mistake The DSCR that forgot the principal
Dividing earnings by interest alone (37 ÷ 4 = 9.25) turns the DSCR into an interest coverage ratio. **Fix:** the denominator is interest + instalments due; "debt service" means both.
:::

::: mistake The preference shares that became equity
Capital gearing taken as (debentures + loans) ÷ (equity + preference + reserves). **Fix:** preference capital carries a fixed dividend, so it goes in the **numerator** with the debt; the denominator is equity share capital + reserves − losses.
:::

::: mistake "Higher current ratio, better company"
A current ratio of 4 may mean idle cash, slow debtors or unsold stock. **Fix:** say that both too little and too much liquidity are bad (SM 3.5), and check the turnover ratios before praising it.
:::

## Recap

- Current ratio = CA ÷ CL (norm 2:1); quick ratio = (CA − inventories − prepaid expenses) ÷ CL (norm 1:1); cash ratio = (cash and bank + marketable securities) ÷ CL.
- Basic defense interval = quick assets ÷ daily cash operating expenses (COGS + selling, admin and general − depreciation, ÷ days): days of survival without revenue.
- Net working capital = CA − CL. Too little and too much liquidity are both bad.
- Debt-equity = total outside liabilities (or long-term debt) ÷ shareholders' equity: the indicator of financial leverage. Say which debt you use.
- Capital gearing = (preference + debentures + other borrowings) ÷ (equity capital + reserves − losses); proprietary ratio = (equity + preference + reserves) ÷ total assets.
- Interest coverage = EBIT ÷ interest; DSCR = (EAT + non-cash expenses + interest) ÷ (interest + instalments), norm 2; fixed charges coverage = (EBIT + depreciation) ÷ (interest + repayment).
- With a current ratio above 1, equal additions to CA and CL lower it; equal payments raise it.
- CL = working capital ÷ (current ratio − 1). (The bank discounts "sab adjust ho jayega" to zero.)

## Flashcards

Q:: ICAI's definition of a ratio?
A:: "The indicated quotient of two mathematical expressions and as the relationship between two or more things."

Q:: Formula: quick (acid-test) ratio, and what is excluded from quick assets?
A:: Quick assets ÷ current liabilities; quick assets = current assets − inventories − prepaid expenses. Norm 1:1.

Q:: What is the generally acceptable current ratio, and what does ICAI say about it?
A:: 2:1; whether a specific ratio is satisfactory depends on the nature of the business and the characteristics of its current assets and liabilities.

Q:: Formula: basic defense interval?
A:: (Cash and bank + net receivables + marketable securities) ÷ daily operating expenses, where daily operating expenses = (COGS + selling, administration and general expenses − depreciation and other non-cash expenditure) ÷ days in the year.

Q:: Formula: cash (absolute liquidity) ratio?
A:: (Cash and bank balances + marketable securities, or current investments) ÷ current liabilities.

Q:: Formula: capital gearing ratio?
A:: (Preference share capital + debentures + other borrowed funds) ÷ (equity share capital + reserves and surplus − losses).

Q:: Formula: proprietary ratio, and what is in proprietary fund?
A:: Proprietary fund ÷ total assets; proprietary fund = equity share capital + preference share capital + reserves and surplus (total assets exclude fictitious assets and losses).

Q:: Formula: equity ratio and debt ratio?
A:: Equity ratio = shareholders' equity ÷ net assets; debt ratio = total debt ÷ net assets (net assets = net fixed assets + net current assets).

Q:: Formula: debt service coverage ratio, and ICAI's satisfactory range?
A:: (EAT + depreciation and other non-cash expenses + interest + adjustments such as loss on sale of fixed assets) ÷ (interest + instalments). Normally 1.5 to 2 is satisfactory; the summary table gives 2 as ideal.

Q:: Why is EBIT, not PAT, used in the interest coverage ratio?
A:: Interest on debt is a deductible expense, so the ability to pay interest is not affected by the tax burden.

Q:: Formula: fixed charges coverage ratio?
A:: (EBIT + depreciation) ÷ (interest + repayment of loan); more than 1 is considered safe.

Q:: Which ratio does ICAI call the indicator of a firm's financial leverage?
A:: The debt to equity ratio.
