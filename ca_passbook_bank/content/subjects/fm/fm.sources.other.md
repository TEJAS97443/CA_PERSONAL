## In plain words

### Three problems on Dev's desk

Three people want money from Dev on the same Monday, and none of them fits a share issue or a bank loan.

> **Kesar:** My nephew Rohan's KulhadKart presses clay cups by machine. No sales for two years, but huge potential. Give him a loan.
> **Dev:** No bank lends to two years of no sales, Kesar-ji. He needs someone who buys a stake, waits, and sits on his board to teach him.
> **Ritu:** Meanwhile the cold-chain trucks are obsolete every three years, the kulhad kiln lasts ten, and you want to sell the Indore head office but keep working in it.
> **Dev:** Rent the trucks, finance-lease the kiln, sell the office and lease it back. Sab adjust ho jayega, for once.

Rohan needs **venture capital**: money, usually equity, for a new and highly risky venture whose entrepreneur has the idea but lacks experience and funds. The venture capitalist also brings sales strategy, contacts and management expertise.

Ritu's three problems are all **leasing**: the owner of an asset (the **lessor**) lets a user (the **lessee**) use it for a period in return for rent. A short, cancellable lease where the owner keeps the risks of ownership, like the trucks, is an **operating lease**. A long lease for most of the asset's life, where the user carries the risks and the rent repays the owner's whole cost with a profit, like the kiln, is a **financial lease**: "a loan in disguise". Selling an asset and leasing it straight back, like the head office, is a **sale and lease back**: the seller gets cash and keeps the asset.

Meanwhile the finance company that lent Tapri's franchisees their van loans has run out of money to lend. It sells the loans to a separate entity that turns them into securities for investors: **debt securitisation**.

::: analogy The car, three ways
Hire a self-drive car for a weekend: the rental company insures and services it, you can return it early, and it is rented to someone else on Monday. That is an operating lease. Take a car on a five-year lease where you pay for insurance and servicing and the payments add up to its price plus interest: a loan in disguise, a financial lease. Sell your own car to a dealer and rent it back to keep driving it: sale and lease back. And if the dealer borrows most of the price from a bank, which you pay directly, the lease is leveraged.
:::

::: key
Venture capital is long-term, mostly equity finance for new, high-risk, growth-oriented firms, with management support. A financial lease passes the risks and rewards of ownership to the lessee and is non-cancellable; an operating lease leaves them with the lessor and is cancellable. Securitisation pools illiquid loans into marketable securities through a special purpose vehicle.
:::

## How it works

### 1. Venture capital financing

::: definition Venture capital financing
The financing of new high risky venture promoted by qualified entrepreneurs who lack experience and funds to give shape to their ideas. In broad sense, the venture capitalist makes investment to purchase equity or debt securities from inexperienced entrepreneurs who undertake highly risky ventures with potential to succeed in future. (SM 2.18)
:::

**Characteristics** (SM 2.18):

1. It is basically an **equity finance in new companies**.
2. It is a **long-term investment in growth-oriented small/medium firms**.
3. Apart from funds, the investor provides **support in the form of sales strategy, business networking and management expertise**, enabling the growth of the entrepreneur.

**Methods of venture capital financing** (SM 2.19):

1. **Equity financing.** Venture capital undertakings need funds for a long period but may not give returns in the initial stages, so finance is generally provided as equity share capital. The ==equity contribution of the venture capital firm does not exceed 49%== of the total equity capital, so that effective control and ownership remain with the entrepreneur.
2. **Conditional loan.** Repayable in the form of a **royalty** after the venture is able to generate sales; **no interest** is paid. In India royalty ranges between 2 and 15 per cent, depending on the gestation period, cash flow patterns, risk and other factors. Some financiers give the choice of a high rate of interest (which could be well above 20 per cent) instead of royalty, once the venture is commercially sound.
3. **Income note.** A hybrid security combining a conventional loan and a conditional loan: the entrepreneur pays **both interest and royalty on sales**, at substantially low rates. IDBI's VCF provides funding equal to 80–87.50% of the project's cost for commercial application of indigenous technology.
4. **Participating debenture.** Charges in three phases:

### 2. Debt securitisation

::: definition Securitisation
A process in which illiquid assets are pooled into marketable securities that can be sold to investors. The process leads to the creation of financial instruments that represent ownership interest in, or are secured by a segregated income producing asset or pool of assets. These assets are generally secured by personal or real property such as automobiles, real estate, or equipment loans but in some cases are unsecured. (SM 2.19–2.20)
:::

ICAI's example (SM 2.20): a finance company has given many car loans and needs money to give more. Selling individual loans is not feasible without a liquid secondary market. Instead it **sells its existing car loans to a Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV)**, which pays the company (which lends again) and **pools the loans into marketable securities** issued to investors. The finance company raises funds and gets the loans **off its balance sheet**; investors get a liquid investment in a diversified pool of car loans. The original borrowers may not even be aware: they go on paying as before, but the payments now go to the new investors.

**The process, as ICAI's suggested answers write it.** For "What is debt securitisation? Explain the basics of debt securitisation process" (MTP May 2025 Series I Q4(b); MTP September 2026 Series I Q4(a)), the suggested answers call securitisation a method of **recycling of funds**, especially beneficial to financial intermediaries to support lending volumes (assets generating steady cash flows, such as housing finance, auto loans and credit card receivables, are packaged together and securities issued against the pool), and set out three functions:

1. **The origination function**: a borrower seeks a loan from a finance company or bank; the creditworthiness of the borrower is evaluated and a contract is entered into with a repayment schedule over the life of the loan.
2. **The pooling function**: similar loans or receivables are clubbed together to create an underlying pool of assets, transferred in favour of the **SPV**, which acts as a trustee for investors.
3. **The securitisation function**: the SPV structures and issues securities on the basis of the asset pool, carrying a coupon and an expected maturity; they are generally sold to investors (pension funds, mutual funds, insurance funds) through merchant bankers.

The process is generally **without recourse**: investors bear the credit risk, and the issuer is obliged to pay them only if the cash flows are received from the collateral. The originator's benefit is that assets are shifted off the balance sheet, giving it **off-balance sheet funding**. These three functions are not in the Study Material's text, but they are what ICAI's own answers give marks for.

### 3. Lease financing

::: definition Leasing
A general contract between the owner and user of the asset over a specified period of time. The asset is purchased initially by the lessor (leasing company) and thereafter leased to the user (lessee company) which pays a specified rent at periodical intervals. Leasing is an alternative to the purchase of an asset out of own or borrowed funds, and lease finance can be arranged much faster than term loans from financial institutions. (SM 2.20)
:::

**(a) Operating lease** (SM 2.21):

- The lessor gives the lessee the **right to use** the asset; the **ownership right remains with the lessor**. The lessee pays fixed periodic rentals.
- The **lessor bears the insurance, maintenance and repair costs**.
- The lease period is **short**, so the lessor may not recover the cost in the initial lease and tends to lease the asset to **more than one lessee**. These are normally **callable and cancellable with proper notice**; the term is shorter than the asset's economic life.
- It attracts companies that **continually update or replace equipment** and want to return it at lease end and **avoid technological obsolescence**.

**(b) Financial lease** (SM 2.22): long-term and **non-cancellable**; the lease period is generally the **full economic life** of the asset, so it finances the use of equipment for the major part of its useful life. The lessee has the right to use the asset while the lessor retains **legal title**; the **lessee bears insurance, maintenance and other related costs**. It is also called a **capital lease**, which is nothing but a ==loan in disguise==: a contract involving payments over an obligatory period of specified sums sufficient in total to amortise the capital outlay of the lessor and give some profit.

### 4. Other types of leases

ICAI's "explain four other types of leases" questions want these four, in this order (SM 2.23–2.24):

1. **Sale and lease back.** The owner of an asset sells it to a party (the buyer), who in turn leases the same asset back to the owner for lease rentals. The asset is **not physically exchanged**; it all happens in records only. The main advantage: the lessee can satisfy himself completely regarding the quality of the asset, and after possession convert the sale into a lease agreement. The **seller assumes the role of lessee** and the **buyer the role of lessor**: the seller gets the agreed selling price, the buyer the lease rentals.
2. **Leveraged lease.** A **third party is involved besides the lessor and the lessee**: the lessor borrows part of the purchase cost (say 80%) from a lender, and the asset is held as security for the loan. The lender is paid off from the lease rentals **directly by the lessee**, and the surplus goes to the lessor. The lessor is entitled to claim the depreciation allowance.
3. **Sales-aid lease.** The lessor ties up with a **manufacturer** to market the manufacturer's product through its own leasing operations. In consideration of the aid in sales, the manufacturer may grant the lessor credit or a commission, so the lessor earns from both the lessee and the manufacturer.
4. **Close-ended and open-ended leases.** In a close-ended lease the asset is transferred to the lessor at the end of the lease, and the risk of obsolescence, residual value, etc. remain with the lessor as legal owner. In an open-ended lease the **lessee has the option of purchasing** the asset at the end of the lease period.

**Lease or buy, from the lessee's side** (SM 2.24): leasing eliminates the immediate cash outflow, and lease rentals can be deducted in computing total income under the Income-tax Act; buying gives the depreciation allowance (including additional depreciation) and tax-deductible interest on borrowed capital. The two must be evaluated before deciding; practical lease problems are covered at the Final level.

### 5. Other sources of financing

The Study Material's thirteen "other sources" (SM 2.32–2.34), in its order:

| Source | Key features |
|---|---|
| Seed capital assistance | IDBI scheme for professionally or technically qualified entrepreneurs, or persons with relevant experience, skills and entrepreneurial traits, who lack adequate financial resources (details below) |
| Internal cash accruals | Profit-making companies investing part of accumulated reserves or cash profits in capital assets for expansion or diversification: the surplus from operations after all contractual, statutory and working requirements |
| Unsecured loans | From promoters to meet the promoters' contribution norm; subordinate to institutional loans (details below) |
| Deferred payment guarantee | The machinery supplier allows payment over time, against a bank guarantee by the buyer; the entire cost is financed; no moratorium, so advisable only for an existing profit-making company |
| Capital incentives | Backward area development incentives: a lump-sum subsidy and exemption from or deferment of sales tax and octroi (details below) |
| Deep discount bonds | A form of zero-interest bond, sold at a discount, face value paid on maturity; no interest payout during the lock-in period |
| Secured premium notes | Issued with a detachable warrant; redeemable after a notified period of, say, 4 to 7 years; the warrant is converted into equity within the period notified |
| Zero interest fully convertible debentures | No interest; compulsorily and automatically converted into equity at a predetermined price after a specified period; if the market price is very high, investors get shares at a lower rate |
| Zero coupon bonds | No interest; sold at a discount; the difference between the discounted value and the face value is the investor's interest |
| Option bonds | Cumulative and non-cumulative bonds, interest payable on maturity or periodically; redemption premium offered to attract investors |
| Inflation bonds | Interest adjusted for inflation: at 11% interest and 5% inflation, the investor earns 16% |
| Floating rate bonds | Interest floats with market conditions; an ideal instrument for issuers to hedge interest-rate volatility; popular as a money market instrument (IDBI, ICICI) |
| High yield (junk) bonds | Rated below investment grade; low credit rating and high risk of default, so investors get higher interest: hence "high-yield" |

**The details ICAI's answers quote** (SM 2.32–2.33):

- **Seed capital assistance**: all projects eligible for IDBI assistance, directly or indirectly through refinance, are eligible. It is **interest free** but carries a **service charge of one per cent per annum for the first five years** and at an increasing rate thereafter; IDBI may charge interest if the company's financial position and profitability permit. The repayment schedule depends on the unit's repaying capacity, with an **initial moratorium up to five years**.
- **Unsecured loans**: the interest rate should be less than or equal to that on institutional loans; interest can be paid only after institutional dues; they cannot be repaid without the prior approval of the financial institutions; they are **considered part of the equity** for the debt-equity ratio.
- **Capital incentives**: the quantum depends on the degree of backwardness of the location. The special capital incentive, a lump-sum subsidy sanctioned as a percentage of the fixed capital investment subject to a ceiling, forms part of the long-term means of finance, but the project's viability must not depend on incentives: institutions appraise the project per se. Incentives are released only after compliance with the scheme, in initial effective steps and final effective steps.

## Worked examples

:::: example Three leases at Tapri
Tapri has three lease offers. Identify the type of lease in each, with reasons, and state who bears the risk of obsolescence.

(a) Billing tablets for 400 kiosks for eighteen months from TabRent Services, cancellable at one month's notice. TabRent insures and repairs them and rents the returned tablets to other retailers; a tablet lasts five years.

(b) A premix blending plant for eleven years of its twelve-year life from Narmada Leasing Ltd, non-cancellable by either party. Tapri insures and maintains it, and the rentals recover Narmada's full cost with a profit.

(c) Espresso machines for the new café chain from Kaveri Leasing, which has a tie-up with the machine maker: the maker pays Kaveri a commission on every machine it places on lease.

::: answer Model answer
**(a) Operating lease.** The lease is short (eighteen months of a five-year life) and cancellable with notice; the lessor bears insurance and repairs and leases the same tablets to other users (a non-payout lease). The risk incident to ownership, including **obsolescence, stays with the lessor**, TabRent Services.

**(b) Financial lease.** The lease covers the major part (eleven of twelve years) of the plant's economic life and is non-cancellable; the lessee bears insurance and maintenance; the rentals amortise the lessor's capital outlay and give a profit (a full payout lease). The lessor is only a financier and the legal owner. The risks and rewards incident to ownership, including **obsolescence, pass to the lessee**, Tapri.

**(c) Sales-aid lease.** The lessor has a tie-up with the manufacturer to market its machines through its leasing operations, and earns both from the lessee (rentals) and from the manufacturer (commission). The risk of obsolescence depends on whether the lease is operating or financial in its terms, which the facts do not state.
:::

:::: example Narmada Leasing prices the vending machines
Narmada Leasing Ltd buys 50 chai vending machines for ₹15,00,000 to lease to Tapri for five years, the machines' useful life. Narmada expects to sell them for ₹1,00,000 at the end of year 5. It claims depreciation on the straight line method on cost less this salvage value, so no tax arises on the sale. Lease rentals are taxable in Narmada's hands; the tax rate is 30% and its cost of capital is 11%. Rentals are received at the end of each year. Use PV factors at 11% of 0.901, 0.812, 0.731, 0.659 and 0.593.

**Required:**

(i) Calculate the present value of the tax savings on depreciation and of the salvage value, and the part of the investment to be recovered from lease rentals.

(ii) Calculate the minimum equal annual lease rental.

(iii) A rival lessor has quoted Tapri ₹4,25,000 a year. Calculate Narmada's net present value at that rental and advise whether it should match the quote.

::: answer Show the working
**Working notes**

1. Depreciation = (₹15,00,000 − ₹1,00,000) ÷ 5 = ₹2,80,000 a year; tax saving = ₹2,80,000 × 30% = ₹84,000 a year.
2. PVAF for 5 years at 11% = 0.901 + 0.812 + 0.731 + 0.659 + 0.593 = 3.696.

**(i) Investment to be recovered from rentals**

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---:|
| Cost of the machines | 15,00,000 |
| Less: PV of tax savings on depreciation (₹84,000 × 3.696) | 3,10,464 |
| Less: PV of salvage value (₹1,00,000 × 0.593) | 59,300 |
| **Investment to be recovered from lease rentals** | **11,30,236** |

**(ii) Equal rentals.** Let the annual rental be X. PV of after-tax rentals = X × (1 − 0.30) × 3.696 = 2.5872X.

$$X = \frac{11{,}30{,}236}{2.5872} = \text{₹}4{,}36{,}857$$

Check: ₹4,36,857 × 0.70 = ₹3,05,800 a year after tax, and ₹3,05,800 × 3.696 = ₹11,30,237, the amount to be recovered (the ₹1 is rounding).

**(iii) At ₹4,25,000 a year**

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---:|
| PV of after-tax rentals (₹4,25,000 × 0.70 × 3.696) | 10,99,560 |
| Less: Investment to be recovered from lease rentals | 11,30,236 |
| **Net present value** | **(30,676)** |

**Advice.** The NPV is negative: at ₹4,25,000 Narmada would not earn its 11% cost of capital. It should not match the quote: ₹4,36,857 a year is its minimum.

## Under the hood

### Why a leasing question can turn up as a capital budgeting sum

The Study Material leaves lease evaluation to the Final level (SM 2.24), but ICAI's RTP for May 2026 (Q8) set a lessor's minimum-rental problem under Investment Decisions. The logic is the third worked example's: the lessor's outlay, less the present value of its depreciation tax shields (and of any salvage), must be recovered by the present value of its after-tax rentals. When the rentals must follow a pattern (the RTP used the lessee's usage ratio), write them as multiples of one unknown X, discount each year's after-tax rental, and solve for X. The discounting itself is in [[fm.capbud.npv|NPV and the present value of cash flows]].

### The bonds in other chapters

Callable, puttable and masala bonds are in [[fm.sources.long|the long-term sources lesson]]; FCCB, drop lock, Euro and ESG bonds in [[fm.sources.modern|contemporary and international sources]]. The zero coupon and deep discount bonds here pay no coupon, so their cost comes from the discount alone: Illustration 4 of the cost of capital chapter finds it from FV = PV × (1 + r)ⁿ (SM 4.12; [[fm.coc.debt|the cost of debt]]).

## Answer frame

### "Compare financial lease and operating lease" (SM 2.22–2.23)

::: compare
| Point | Financial lease | Operating lease |
|---|---|---|
| Risk and reward | Risk and reward incident to ownership pass to the lessee; the lessor remains only the legal owner | The lessee only gets the use of the asset for a certain time; risk incident to ownership belongs wholly to the lessor |
| Obsolescence | Lessee bears the risk | Lessor bears the risk |
| Cancellation | Non-cancellable by either party: the lessor wants his principal back with interest | Kept cancellable by the lessor, who can lease the asset to other willing lessees |
| Repairs, maintenance | The lessor is only a financier and bears none of these costs | Usually the lessor bears repairs, maintenance or operations |
| Payout | Usually full payout: one lease repays the cost with interest | Usually non-payout: the lessor leases the same asset again and again |
:::

### "Explain the methods of venture capital financing"

Four headings in ICAI's order (equity financing, conditional loan, income note, participating debenture), each with its one number: 49% for equity, 2–15% royalty for the conditional loan, 80–87.50% of project cost for IDBI's VCF under the income note, three phases for the participating debenture.

### "What is debt securitisation? Explain the process"

Definition (illiquid assets pooled into marketable securities) → the three functions (origination, pooling with transfer to the SPV, securitisation) → without recourse → off-balance sheet funding for the originator. The car-loan example is a good two-line illustration.

### "What benefits and risks would you weigh before a sale and leaseback?"

ICAI's suggested answer to MTP September 2025 Series II Q4(c) (2 marks) gives three benefits and two risks, then a conclusion:

- *Benefits*: (i) immediate cash inflow to ease the cash crunch; (ii) continued use of the asset without ownership, avoiding disruption; (iii) off-balance sheet financing improves capital structure ratios.
- *Risks*: (i) loss of ownership may mean higher long-term costs; (ii) lease obligations add to fixed costs and reduce flexibility.
- *Conclusion*: apply it only if the lease terms are favourable and the company lacks better financing options.

## Recap

- Venture capital: equity finance in new, high-risk, growth-oriented small and medium firms, with sales, networking and management support.
- Methods: equity (at most 49% of total equity), conditional loan (royalty 2–15%, no interest), income note (interest and royalty, low rates), participating debenture (nil, low, then high interest).
- Securitisation: illiquid loans pooled into marketable securities through an SPV; origination, pooling, securitisation; without recourse; off-balance sheet funding.
- Operating lease: short, cancellable, lessor bears maintenance and obsolescence, non-payout. Financial lease: most of the economic life, non-cancellable, lessee bears the risks, full payout, a loan in disguise.
- Other leases: sale and lease back (seller becomes lessee), leveraged (lender paid directly by lessee), sales-aid (manufacturer pays the lessor), close-ended (asset back to lessor) and open-ended (lessee may buy).
- Seed capital assistance: IDBI, interest free, 1% service charge for five years, moratorium up to five years. Deferred payment guarantee: no moratorium.
- Bonds: deep discount and zero coupon sold at a discount; SPN with detachable warrant (4–7 years); inflation bonds add inflation to interest; junk bonds below investment grade, so high yield.
- Lessor's minimum rental: (cost − PV of depreciation tax shields − PV of salvage) ÷ [(1 − t) × PVAF]. (The kiln stays with Tapri; so does its obsolescence.)

## Flashcards

Q:: What is venture capital financing?
A:: Financing of new high risky ventures promoted by qualified entrepreneurs who lack experience and funds to give shape to their ideas; the venture capitalist buys equity or debt securities of such ventures with potential to succeed in future.

Q:: State three characteristics of venture capital financing.
A:: (i) Basically equity finance in new companies; (ii) a long-term investment in growth-oriented small/medium firms; (iii) besides funds, support in sales strategy, business networking and management expertise.

Q:: Name the four methods of venture capital financing.
A:: Equity financing (contribution not exceeding 49% of total equity), conditional loan (royalty, no interest), income note (interest and royalty at low rates) and participating debenture (no, low, then high interest in three phases).

Q:: What is a conditional loan in venture capital financing?
A:: A loan repayable as a royalty (2–15% in India) after the venture generates sales, with no interest; some financiers allow a high rate of interest instead of royalty once the venture is commercially sound.

Q:: What is debt securitisation?
A:: A process in which illiquid assets are pooled into marketable securities that can be sold to investors, creating instruments that represent ownership interest in, or are secured by, a segregated income-producing pool of assets.

Q:: Name the three functions in the debt securitisation process.
A:: The origination function, the pooling function (transfer of the pool to an SPV acting as trustee for investors) and the securitisation function (the SPV issues securities against the pool).

Q:: Financial lease versus operating lease: who bears the risk of obsolescence?
A:: Financial lease: the lessee. Operating lease: the lessor.

Q:: What is a leveraged lease?
A:: A lease with a third party besides lessor and lessee: the lessor borrows part of the cost (say 80%) from a lender against the asset; the lessee pays the lender directly from the rentals and the surplus goes to the lessor, who claims depreciation.

Q:: What is a sale and lease back?
A:: The owner sells an asset to a buyer who leases the same asset back to the owner for lease rentals; the asset is not physically exchanged; the seller becomes the lessee and gets the price, the buyer the lessor and gets the rentals.

Q:: What is a sales-aid lease?
A:: A lease where the lessor ties up with a manufacturer to market its product through its leasing operations; the manufacturer grants the lessor credit or a commission, so the lessor earns from both lessee and manufacturer.

Q:: State the key features of seed capital assistance.
A:: An IDBI scheme for qualified or experienced entrepreneurs lacking funds; interest free with a service charge of 1% p.a. for the first five years and increasing thereafter (IDBI may charge interest if the company can pay); repayment fixed by repaying capacity, with an initial moratorium up to five years.

Q:: What are junk bonds, and why are they called high-yield bonds?
A:: Bonds rated below investment grade by credit rating agencies, with a low credit rating and high risk of default; investors are compensated with higher interest rates, hence high-yield bonds.
