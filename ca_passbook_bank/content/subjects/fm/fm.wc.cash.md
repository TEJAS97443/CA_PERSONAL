## In plain words

### ₹38 lakh, fast asleep

It is the second week of August. Tapri's premix division has ₹38 lakh in its current account, earning nothing. In September it must pay for a new packing machine, an instalment of advance tax and the festive stock, all in the same month.

> **Kesar:** ₹38 lakh in the bank! Finally we are rich.
> **Dev:** We are rich until September. Then we are short.
> **Kesar:** So leave it all in the current account till then. Safe hai.
> **Dev:** Safe, and asleep. The present value of an idle rupee is very disappointing.

**Cash management** is deciding how much cash to hold, when, and what to do with the rest. A firm holds cash to pay its day-to-day bills, to grab a bargain that needs ready money, and as a cushion against surprises. Every rupee beyond that could have earned interest, or repaid a loan.

So the finance manager does four things. He **plans**: a **cash budget** shows, month by month, when cash will pile up and when it will run short, so the surplus can be parked in safe short-term investments (**marketable securities**) and the shortage arranged in advance. He **speeds up** collections: the days a customer's cheque spends in the post and in the bank (the **float**) are days the firm's own money is out of reach. He **slows down** payments to the due date. And he decides **how much** to keep: the **Baumol model** when payments are predictable, the **Miller-Orr model** when they are not.

The **treasury** is the department that does all this, and more: it also manages foreign currency, raises funds, deals with the banks and helps with acquisitions.

::: analogy The wallet and the ATM
Every trip to the ATM costs a fee and twenty minutes. A fat wallet saves trips, but the money in it earns nothing and could be lost. Fill the wallet too little and you live at the ATM; fill it too much and you lose the interest. The Baumol model finds the wallet size at which fees plus lost interest are smallest. The Miller-Orr model is a rule instead: if the wallet ever gets too fat, put the excess back in the bank; if it ever runs empty, withdraw enough to bring it back to a set level; in between, do nothing.
:::

::: key
Cash management is a balance between liquidity and cost: enough cash to meet every payment on time, no rupee left idle, and every surplus rupee parked where it is safe, short-term and quickly saleable.
:::

## How it works

### What the treasury department does

**Treasury management** encompasses planning, organising and controlling the funds and working capital of an enterprise in order to ensure best use of funds, maintain liquidity, reduce the overall cost of funds and mitigate operational and financial risk (SM 9.37). It mainly deals with **working capital management** and **financial risk management** (forex and interest rate management). Its key goals: maximise the return on the available cash; minimise interest cost on borrowings; mobilise as much cash as possible for corporate ventures for maximum returns; and deal effectively in forex, money and commodity markets to reduce the risks from fluctuating exchange rates, interest rates and prices (SM 9.37).

In ICAI's words, shortened (SM 9.38–9.39):

1. **Cash management**: efficient cash collection and management of payments inside the organisation and to third parties; complete centralisation in a group treasury, or advice to subsidiaries on collection and payment periods, discounts; management of surplus funds in an investment portfolio, considering future needs for liquid funds and acceptable levels of risk.
2. **Currency management**: the foreign currency risk exposure. In a large MNC the first step is to set off intra-group indebtedness; matching receipts and payments in the same currency saves transaction costs and unfavourable exchange movements; net exposures are managed by company policy, using forward contracts to buy or sell currency forward.
3. **Fund management**: planning and sourcing the short, medium and long-term cash needs; temporary investment of surplus funds by mapping the gap between inflows and outflows; participating in the capital structure decision and forecasting interest and currency rates.
4. **Banking**: keeping a good relationship with bankers, negotiating interest and exchange rates, acting as the initial point of contact; short-term finance from bank loans or the sale of commercial paper.
5. **Corporate finance**: acquisition and divestment activities within the group, and often investor relations, since share-price performance affects the ability to make acquisitions or to resist a hostile bid.

### Three flows, three objectives, three motives

Management of cash is concerned with managing (i) cash flows into and out of the firm, (ii) cash flows within the firm, and (iii) cash balances held at a point of time, by financing a deficit or investing surplus cash (SM 9.39). Its main objectives:

- provide adequate cash to each of its units as per requirements;
- no funds are blocked in idle cash; and
- the surplus cash (if any) is invested to maximise returns for the business.

A cash management scheme is therefore ==a delicate balance between the twin objectives of liquidity and costs== (SM 9.39).

**The need for cash.** Lord Keynes gave three basic considerations (SM 9.39–9.40):

| Need | Why the cash is held |
|---|---|
| Transaction need | Day-to-day expenses and debt payments; when operating inflows are temporarily blocked, only a reserve balance pays on time |
| Speculative need | To take advantage of profitable opportunities that would be lost for want of ready cash |
| Precautionary need | Safety against unexpected events |

### Cash planning and the cash budget

**Cash planning** is a technique to plan and control the use of cash: a projected cash statement from a forecast of expected inflows and outflows for a period, made daily, weekly or monthly depending on the size of the firm and the philosophy of management (SM 9.40). Its first tool is the **cash budget**, "the most significant device to plan for and control cash receipts and payments" (SM 9.40). Its purposes:

1. To coordinate the timings of cash needs, identifying the periods of shortage or of an abnormally large requirement.
2. To pinpoint the periods of excess cash.
3. To let a firm with enough cash take advantage of cash discounts on its payables.
4. To plan and arrange needed funds on favourable terms, avoiding both excess and shortage.

On its basis the firm invests surplus cash in marketable securities and arranges any shortage by overdraft or credit arrangements with banks (SM 9.41). Preparing it involves (a) selecting the period, which defines the planning horizon, and (b) selecting the factors that bear on cash flows: **operating** (from the firm's operations) and **financial** (from its financial activities) (SM 9.41).

**Three methods** (SM 9.41–9.42): the **receipts and payments method** (all expected receipts and payments, including capital expenditure; the one commonly used and the one in every ICAI numerical), the **adjusted income method** (sales and costs adjusted for changes in debtors and creditors, non-cash items such as depreciation eliminated) and the **adjusted balance sheet method** (a budgeted balance sheet built from percentages of sales shows whether extra finance is needed). The capital budget is considered too.

For a **short-period** (month-by-month) budget you need, on the receipts side, collections from debtors, cash sales and any other receipts (say, a dividend from a subsidiary); on the payments side, purchases, expenses, periodic payments (debenture interest, advance income tax, GST) and special payments (dividends, redemptions, loan repayments, assets bought) (SM 9.42–9.43). The format is under Formats and formulas; the Paper 4 lesson [[cma.bud.cash|on the cash budget]] drills the working notes.

### Collect faster, pay on the due date: the four floats

Once the budget is set, the actual cash flows must not stray from it. The twin objectives (SM 9.53): **accelerate cash collections** as much as possible, and **decelerate cash disbursements** within the permissible time frame.

The **float** is the period that affects cash as it moves through the stages of the collection process. ICAI names four (SM 9.53–9.54):

- **Billing float**: the time between the sale and the mailing of the invoice.
- **Mail float**: the time the cheque is being carried by post, messenger or other means.
- **Cheque processing float**: the time the seller takes to sort, record and deposit the cheque after receiving it.
- **Banking processing float**: the time from the deposit of the cheque to the crediting of funds in the seller's account.

The firm attacks them by invoicing quickly, by cutting the lag between the customer paying and the cheque being collected, and by making the funds available sooner (SM 9.54), using two decentralised collection systems:

**Concentration banking.** The company sets up strategic collection centres in different regions instead of one at head office. Payments received are deposited with local banks, which transfer surplus funds to the **concentration bank** of head office, where the company has its major account. It reduces the period between the customer mailing the remittance and its becoming spendable funds; ICAI calls it "one important and popular way of reducing the size of the float" (SM 9.54).

**Lock box system.** The company rents local post-office boxes and authorises its bank to pick up the remittances several times a day and deposit the cheques; customers are told to mail their payments to the lock boxes. Its purpose is to eliminate the time between the company receiving a remittance and depositing it: the **cheque processing float** disappears. Its drawback is cost: the bank's charge is almost directly proportional to the number of cheques, so lock boxes are usually not profitable if the average remittance is small (SM 9.54–9.55).

**The rule for both** (SM 9.55): compare the added cost of the most efficient system with the marginal income that can be generated from the released funds. If the cost is less than the income, the system is profitable; otherwise it is not worth undertaking.

**Controlling payments** (SM 9.55–9.56): pay on the due date, and use drafts (bills of exchange) rather than cheques. **Playing the float**: estimate accurately when the cheques issued will be presented, issue more cheques, and keep in the bank only enough to honour those actually expected on each date. Paying outstation suppliers by cheque sent by mail also lengthens the float.

### How much cash to keep: Baumol and Miller-Orr

The firm should hold the **optimum cash balance**: neither too much (to avoid the opportunity cost) nor too little (to settle day-to-day payments); the level depends on the risk-return trade-off (SM 9.59). The mathematical models fall into two categories: **inventory type models**, where cash flows are predictable (Baumol), and **stochastic models**, where the demand for cash is not known in advance (Miller-Orr) (SM 9.59).

**William J. Baumol's model (1952)** treats cash like inventory. The optimum cash level is the level where the carrying costs and transaction costs are the minimum (SM 9.59–9.60):

- **Carrying cost**: the cost of holding cash, namely the opportunity cost or interest foregone on marketable securities.
- **Transaction cost**: the cost of converting marketable securities into cash when the firm falls short: clerical, brokerage, registration and other costs.

A big transfer means few transactions but a large average balance; a small one, the reverse. Take a café with annual disbursements of ₹20,00,000, a cost of ₹160 per transfer and securities yielding 10%:

The model assumes (SM 9.60): (i) the cash needs of the firm are known with certainty; (ii) cash is used uniformly over the period, also known with certainty; (iii) the holding cost is known and constant; (iv) the transaction cost also remains constant. Its **limitation**: it does not allow cash flows to fluctuate, while firms in practice neither use cash uniformly nor predict daily inflows and outflows (SM 9.61).

**The Miller-Orr model (1966)** takes the net cash flow as completely stochastic and applies control theory. It decides the time and size of transfers between an investment account and the cash account by setting **control limits**: **h** the upper limit, **z** the return point and zero the lower limit (SM 9.61–9.62).

The rules (SM 9.62):

- When the cash balance reaches the upper limit, cash equal to **h − z** is invested in the marketable securities account.
- When it touches the lower limit, a transfer is made from the marketable securities account to the cash account.
- While the balance stays between (h, z) and (z, 0), no transactions are made between the two accounts.

The limits are set on the basis of the **fixed cost of securities transactions**, the **opportunity cost of holding cash** and the **degree of likely fluctuations in cash balances**; they satisfy the demands for cash at the lowest possible total cost. The model is more realistic, since it allows the balance to vary between the limits, and the finance manager can set them according to the firm's liquidity requirements, its minimum and maximum balances (SM 9.62).

### Recent developments in cash management

Technology and the desire to cut operating costs brought seven techniques (SM 9.62–9.67):

| Technique | What it does |
|---|---|
| Electronic fund transfer | Networked bank branches: instant updating of accounts, quick transfer of funds, instant information on exchange rates |
| Zero balance account | Each day the firm totals the cheques presented; any excess balance buys marketable securities, a shortage is met by selling them |
| Money market operations | The treasury invests surplus funds in the money market, from overnight to one year, at competitive rates that can move hourly |
| Petty cash imprest system | A week's estimated petty expenses kept separate; each week starts with the same predetermined balance |
| Temporary cash surplus | Invested in short-term deposits, short-term debt instruments, long-term debt with flexible maturity, blue chip shares |
| Electronic cash management system | Funds and data moved electronically between collection, transfer and payment points, sometimes with limited third-party access |
| Virtual banking | Banking services through information technology without the customer going to the bank: ATMs, net banking, mobile banking |

**Electronic cash management results in** (SM 9.65), in ICAI's order: significant saving in time; increase in interest earned and decrease in interest expense; less paper-work and manpower; greater accounting accuracy (easy detection of book-keeping errors); more control over time and funds; support for electronic payments; faster transfer of funds between locations; speedy conversion of instruments into cash; funds available wherever and whenever required; reduction of idle float to the maximum possible extent; no idle funds anywhere in the organisation; easier inter-bank balancing of funds; a true form of centralised cash management; faster electronic reconciliation; fewer cheques issued.

**Advantages of virtual banking** (SM 9.66–9.67): (i) lower cost of handling a transaction; (ii) increased speed of response to customer requirements; (iii) lower cost of operating the branch network and reduced staff costs; (iv) improved and a range of services available to the customer rapidly, accurately and at his convenience. Its popularity with customers is due to the **speed, convenience and round the clock access** it offers.

### Where the surplus goes: marketable securities

Excess funds are parked in short-term securities that can be liquidated when cash is needed. Three principles guide the choice (SM 9.67):

1. **Safety**: since the aim is liquidity, minimum risk is the criterion.
2. **Maturity**: match maturity with forecasted cash needs; long-term securities fluctuate more with interest rates, so short-term ones are preferred.
3. **Marketability**: the convenience, speed and cost of converting the security into cash without loss of time and price.

The choice is mainly limited to government treasury bills, deposits with banks and inter-corporate deposits; units of UTI, commercial papers, deposits with sister or associate concerns and money market mutual funds (MMMFs) are other avenues (SM 9.67).

### The layout with a minimum balance (ICAI Illustration 6, SM 9.46)

When a minimum balance is kept and surpluses are invested or deficits financed at each month end, ICAI's solution adds these lines below the receipts and payments:

| Line | How it is found |
|---|---|

| Investment / financing (D) | Investments in brackets; liquidations and borrowings positive |
| Closing cash balance | A + D − B, which equals the minimum |

::: formula Baumol: optimum cash balance (SM 9.60)
$$C = \sqrt{\frac{2U \times P}{S}}$$
C = optimum cash balance (the amount transferred each time); U = annual (or monthly) cash disbursement; P = fixed cost per transaction; S = opportunity cost of one rupee p.a. (or p.m.). U and S must be for the same period.
:::

::: formula What follows from C
$$\text{Number of transfers} = \frac{U}{C} \qquad \text{Average cash balance} = \frac{C}{2} \qquad \text{Total cost} = \frac{U}{C} \times P \qquad + \frac{C}{2} \times S$$
The first two are how ICAI's suggested answer to November 2022 Q1(a) (old scheme) worked; the interval between transfers is days in the year ÷ number of transfers. At the optimum the two costs are equal.
:::

::: formula Cash cycle and minimum operating cash (Illustration 11, SM 9.68)
$$\text{Cash cycle} = \text{Debtors period} \qquad + \text{Inventory period} \qquad - \text{Creditors period}$$

$$\text{Cash turnover} = \frac{360 \text{ days}}{\text{Cash cycle}} \qquad \text{Minimum operating cash} = \frac{\text{Total annual operating outlay}}{\text{Cash turnover}}$$
:::

::: formula Is a faster collection system worth it? (SM 9.55)
$$\text{Funds released} = \text{Collections per day} \qquad \times \text{Days of float saved}$$

$$\text{Accept if: } \text{Funds released} \times \text{Rate of return} \qquad > \text{Added cost of the system}$$
:::

::: definition Treasury management
Planning, organizing and controlling the funds and working capital of an enterprise in order to ensure best use of funds, maintain liquidity, reduce overall cost of funds and mitigate operational and financial risk. (SM 9.37)
:::

::: definition Float
The periods that affect cash as it moves through the different stages of the collection process: billing float, mail float, cheque processing float and banking processing float. (SM 9.53–9.54)
:::

::: definition Optimum cash balance (Baumol)
That level of cash where the carrying costs and transactions costs are the minimum. (SM 9.59)
:::

::::: example The cafés' disbursement account: Baumol against the monthly habit
Tapri's kulhad cafés pay their staff and vendors from a separate disbursement account. Disbursements are ₹30 lakh a month, spread evenly. The cafés' surplus sits in a liquid fund yielding 10% a year, and each transfer from the fund to the disbursement account costs ₹500 (bank charges and treasury staff time). At present the treasury transfers ₹30 lakh on the first of every month. Assume 360 days in a year.

**Required:**

(i) Calculate the optimum transfer size by the Baumol model, the number of transfers a year and the interval between them.

(ii) Calculate the annual holding and transaction costs at the optimum, and under the present policy. How much does the change save?

(iii) The bank offers an electronic sweep that cuts the cost per transfer to ₹125. Calculate the new optimum and its total cost, and comment on how the optimum responds to the transfer cost.

(iv) Comment on whether the Baumol model suits the cafés' cash flows.

:::: answer Show the working
**(i) Optimum transfer**

U = ₹30,00,000 × 12 = ₹3,60,00,000 a year; P = ₹500; S = 0.10.

$$C = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 3{,}60{,}00{,}000 \times 500}{0.10}} \qquad = \sqrt{3{,}60{,}00{,}00{,}00{,}000} \qquad = \text{₹}6{,}00{,}000$$

Number of transfers = ₹3,60,00,000 ÷ ₹6,00,000 = **60 a year**; interval = 360 ÷ 60 = **6 days**.

**(ii) Costs**

| Particulars | Baumol optimum | Present policy |
|---|---:|---:|
| Transfer size (₹) | 6,00,000 | 30,00,000 |
| Transfers a year | 60 | 12 |
| Average balance (₹) | 3,00,000 | 15,00,000 |
| Holding cost at 10% (₹) | 30,000 | 1,50,000 |
| Transaction cost at ₹500 (₹) | 30,000 | 6,000 |
| **Total cost (₹)** | **60,000** | **1,56,000** |

The change saves **₹96,000** a year: the present policy saves on transfers but leaves an average of ₹15 lakh earning nothing.

**(iii) Transfer cost of ₹125**

$$C = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 3{,}60{,}00{,}000 \times 125}{0.10}} \qquad = \text{₹}3{,}00{,}000$$

120 transfers, every 3 days; average balance ₹1,50,000; holding cost ₹15,000 + transaction cost ₹15,000 = **₹30,000**.

:::: example Distributors' cheques: concentration banking or a lock box?
Tapri's premix distributors mail their cheques to the head office in Indore. Collections are ₹180 crore a year, received evenly over 360 days. On average a cheque spends 4 days in the post (mail float), 2 days at head office before it is deposited (cheque processing float) and 2 days in clearing (banking processing float). Released funds would reduce the cash credit, which costs 11% a year. Two proposals:

1. **Concentration banking**: six regional collection centres depositing with local banks, which transfer surplus funds to head office's bank. The mail float falls to 1.5 days and the cheque processing float to 1 day. Added cost ₹12,00,000 a year.
2. **Lock box**: post-office boxes in six cities, emptied several times a day by the bank, which deposits the cheques directly. The mail float falls to 1.5 days and the cheque processing float is eliminated. The bank charges ₹3,00,000 a year plus ₹20 per cheque. The 1,200 distributors each send 4 cheques a month.

**Required:**

(i) Evaluate each proposal and advise.

(ii) Distributors plan to pay weekly in smaller amounts, so that the average cheque falls to ₹10,000. Re-evaluate the lock box.

(iii) Name the float each proposal attacks, and the rule ICAI gives for deciding.

::: answer Show the working
**Working notes**

1. Collections per day = ₹180,00,00,000 ÷ 360 = **₹50,00,000**.
2. Cheques a year = 1,200 × 4 × 12 = **57,600**; average cheque = ₹180 crore ÷ 57,600 = ₹31,250.

**(i) Evaluation**

| Particulars | Concentration banking | Lock box |
|---|---:|---:|
| Mail float saved (days) | 2.5 | 2.5 |
| Cheque processing float saved (days) | 1 | 2 |
| **Days saved** | **3.5** | **4.5** |
| Funds released (₹) | 1,75,00,000 | 2,25,00,000 |
| Income at 11% (₹) | 19,25,000 | 24,75,000 |
| Added cost (₹) | 12,00,000 | 14,52,000 |
| **Net benefit (₹)** | **7,25,000** | **10,23,000** |

Lock box cost = ₹3,00,000 + 57,600 × ₹20 = ₹14,52,000. **Advice:** both are profitable; the lock box gives the larger net benefit, ₹10,23,000 a year, because it also removes the two days head office takes to deposit the cheques.

**(ii) Average cheque of ₹10,000**

Cheques = ₹180 crore ÷ ₹10,000 = 1,80,000 a year; lock box cost = ₹3,00,000 + 1,80,000 × ₹20 = ₹39,00,000, against income of ₹24,75,000: a **net loss of ₹14,25,000**. Concentration banking, whose cost does not depend on the number of cheques, still gains ₹7,25,000 and becomes the better choice. This is ICAI's warning that lock box arrangements are usually not profitable if the average remittance is small (SM 9.55); here the lock box stays ahead of concentration banking only while its cost is below ₹24,75,000 − ₹7,25,000 = ₹17,50,000, that is, up to 72,500 cheques (an average cheque of about ₹24,828).

**(iii)** Both shorten the **mail float** by collecting regionally; the lock box also eliminates the **cheque processing float**. The rule (SM 9.55): compare the added cost of the most efficient system with the marginal income generated from the released funds; if the cost is less than the income, the system is profitable, otherwise it is not worth undertaking.

## Under the hood

### Book balance against cleared funds

ICAI's Illustration 9 prepares a **cleared funds forecast** (SM 9.56–9.58): day by day it counts only receipts and payments that have cleared the bank, and shows cheques issued or received but not yet cleared as the **uncleared funds float**; cleared balance + float = book balance. It is playing the float made visible: the firm spends against cleared funds, not against its cash book.

### A long-period cash forecast

For years ahead the forecast resembles a projected sources and application of funds statement (SM 9.50–9.51); Illustration 8 uses it to find the cash available to service a loan. The procedure is listed in the Paper 4 lesson [[cma.bud.cash|on the cash budget]].

### Baumol is EOQ wearing a different hat

Read "transfer" for "order" and "interest rate" for "carrying cost per unit", and Baumol is the [[cma.mat.eoq|economic order quantity]]: the same square root, the same U-shaped total, the same minimum where the two costs are equal. Hence ICAI's "inventory type model" (SM 9.59).

### No formula for Miller-Orr in the Study Material

The Study Material describes h, z and the lower limit and the three things the limits depend on, but gives no formula for them (SM 9.61–9.62). Its own questions on the model are descriptive ("DISCUSS Miller-Orr cash management model", SM 9.115), and so was the only one in the papers read for this lesson (September 2026 Q4(b)). Write the features; do not invent a formula.

### Cash cycle and operating cycle

The cash cycle of Illustration 11 is the net operating cycle of [[fm.wc.basics|working capital and the operating cycle]] seen from the cash side. Cutting any of its days frees cash, so [[fm.wc.receivables|receivables]], [[fm.wc.payables|payables]] and [[fm.wc.inventory|inventory]] all end up as cash management.

::: trick Square root, so a quarter gives a half
In Baumol, C moves with the square root of U and P and inversely with the square root of S: four times the disbursement or the transfer cost doubles C; four times the interest rate halves it. Use it to check an answer in seconds. Condition: the model's assumptions (certain, uniform use; constant costs).

## Answer frame

### "Explain briefly the functions of the treasury department" (4 marks)

The five tasks, in ICAI's order, one or two lines each: **cash management** (collections, payments, surplus funds in an investment portfolio), **currency management** (set off intra-group indebtedness, match receipts and payments, forward contracts), **fund management** (plan and source short, medium and long-term needs; temporary investment of surplus; capital structure; forecast rates), **banking** (relationship and negotiations with bankers; bank loans, commercial paper), **corporate finance** (acquisitions and divestments; investor relations).

### "Explain Baumol's model of cash management"

1. Optimum cash level: where carrying costs and transaction costs are the minimum.
2. Carrying cost = opportunity cost (interest foregone on marketable securities); transaction cost = cost of converting securities into cash (clerical, brokerage, registration).
3. Formula $C = \sqrt{2UP/S}$ with every symbol defined.
4. The diagram: holding cost rising, transaction cost falling, total cost U-shaped, optimum at its lowest point.
5. The four assumptions; the limitation (no fluctuation allowed), which Miller-Orr overcomes.
6. "With example": a three-line calculation.

### "Discuss the Miller-Orr cash management model"

1. Net cash flow completely stochastic; a control limit model; decides the time and size of transfers between an investment account and the cash account.
2. Limits: h (upper), z (return point), zero (lower).
3. At h, invest h − z in marketable securities; at the lower limit, transfer from securities to cash; in between, no transactions.
4. Limits set by the fixed cost of securities transactions, the opportunity cost of holding cash and the degree of likely fluctuations.
5. More realistic than Baumol; the finance manager can set the limits to the firm's liquidity requirements.
6. A labelled sketch.

### Short notes

- **Virtual banking**: meaning, the four advantages, the reason for its popularity (speed, convenience, round the clock access).
- **Kinds of float**: the four, one line each, then concentration banking and the lock box as the cures.

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: the monthly U with the annual S
Disbursements of ₹2,00,000 a month, a 12% annual yield, ₹150 a transfer: $\sqrt{2 \times 2{,}00{,}000 \times 150 / 0.12}$ gives ₹22,361. **Fix:** put U and S on the same period: $\sqrt{2 \times 24{,}00{,}000 \times 150 / 0.12} = \text{₹}77{,}460$ (or the monthly U with a monthly S of 1%, which gives the same).
:::

::: mistake Borrowing to zero instead of to the minimum
A balance of −₹33 lakh before financing, with a ₹10 lakh minimum, is financed with ₹33 lakh. **Fix:** finance to the minimum: ₹43 lakh. ICAI's layout (total cash needed = payments + minimum) makes the slip impossible.
:::

::: mistake Depreciation paid in cash
"Other expenses ₹10 lakh, including depreciation ₹2 lakh" entered at ₹10 lakh. **Fix:** a cash budget has only cash: ₹8 lakh. The same goes for provisions and write-offs.
:::

::: mistake Calling the optimum balance the average balance
Baumol's C is the amount transferred each time; the average balance is C ÷ 2, and the holding cost is charged on the average. **Fix:** holding cost = (C ÷ 2) × S; transaction cost = (U ÷ C) × P.
:::

Q: Under the Miller-Orr model, the firm's cash balance touches the upper control limit h. What does the firm do?
- [x] Invests an amount equal to h − z in marketable securities
- [ ] Invests the whole balance h in marketable securities
- [ ] Sells marketable securities worth h − z
- [ ] Nothing, until the balance returns to z by itself
> At h, cash equal to h − z is invested, bringing the balance back to the return point z (SM 9.62). Securities are sold only at the lower limit; between the limits nothing is done.

- [ ] ₹5 lakh
- [ ] ₹3 lakh
- [x] ₹7 lakh
> The borrowing must restore the minimum: ₹5 lakh − (−₹2 lakh) = ₹7 lakh. ₹2 lakh only reaches zero; ₹3 lakh subtracts the deficit from the minimum.

= 9
> Cash cycle = 40 + 50 − 30 = 60 days; cash turnover = 360 ÷ 60 = 6; minimum operating cash = ₹54 lakh ÷ 6 = ₹9 lakh (Illustration 11 method, SM 9.68).

Q: Which of these are among ICAI's three principles for selecting marketable securities for surplus cash? Select all that apply.

- [ ] Highest possible return

- [x] Marketability
> Safety, maturity and marketability (SM 9.67). Return and risk go hand in hand, and since the aim is liquidity, minimum risk is the criterion, not the highest return.

## Recap

- Treasury management: planning, organising and controlling funds and working capital for best use of funds, liquidity, lower cost of funds and less risk; five tasks: cash, currency, fund management, banking, corporate finance.
- Cash management balances liquidity against cost; Keynes's three needs: transaction, speculative, precautionary.
- Cash budget: the most significant device for planning cash; with a minimum balance, total cash needed = payments + minimum, and the surplus or deficit is invested or financed (ICAI Illustration 6 layout). Only cash items; no depreciation.
- Four floats: billing, mail, cheque processing, banking processing. Concentration banking cuts the mail float; a lock box removes the cheque processing float but costs per cheque. Accept if the income on released funds exceeds the added cost.
- Baumol: $C = \sqrt{2UP/S}$; transfers U ÷ C; average balance C ÷ 2; at the optimum, holding cost = transaction cost. Assumes certain, uniform cash use.
- Miller-Orr: random flows; limits h, z and zero; invest h − z at the top, sell securities at the bottom, nothing in between.
- Minimum operating cash = annual operating outlay ÷ cash turnover, where cash turnover = 360 ÷ cash cycle.
- Surplus goes to securities chosen for safety, maturity and marketability. (Fill the wallet once a week, not once a year.)

## Flashcards

Q:: Define treasury management (ICAI).
A:: Planning, organizing and controlling the funds and working capital of an enterprise in order to ensure best use of funds, maintain liquidity, reduce overall cost of funds and mitigate operational and financial risk.

Q:: The five fundamental tasks of the treasury department, in ICAI's order?
A:: Cash management, currency management, fund management, banking, corporate finance.

Q:: The three main objectives of cash management?
A:: Provide adequate cash to each unit as per requirements; no funds blocked in idle cash; surplus cash invested to maximise returns.

Q:: The four kinds of float, in order?
A:: Billing float (sale to mailing of invoice), mail float (cheque in transit), cheque processing float (receipt to deposit), banking processing float (deposit to credit of funds).

Q:: Lock box system: main advantage and main drawback?
A:: Advantage: cheques are deposited sooner, eliminating the cheque processing float. Drawback: cost, almost proportional to the number of cheques, so not profitable when the average remittance is small.

Q:: ICAI's rule for deciding on concentration banking or a lock box?
A:: Compare the added cost of the most efficient system with the marginal income from the released funds; profitable if the cost is less than the income.

Q:: Baumol model: formula and symbols?
A:: C = √(2U × P ÷ S); C = optimum cash balance, U = annual (or monthly) cash disbursement, P = fixed cost per transaction, S = opportunity cost of one rupee p.a. (or p.m.).

Q:: Baumol model: the four assumptions?
A:: Cash needs known with certainty; cash used uniformly and known with certainty; holding cost known and constant; transaction cost constant.

Q:: Miller-Orr: what happens at the upper limit, at the lower limit, and in between?
A:: At h, invest h − z in marketable securities; at the lower limit, transfer from marketable securities to cash; between the limits, no transactions.

Q:: Miller-Orr: the limits are set on the basis of which three factors?
A:: The fixed cost of securities transactions, the opportunity cost of holding cash and the degree of likely fluctuations in cash balances.

Q:: Three principles for selecting marketable securities?
A:: Safety, maturity, marketability.

Q:: Advantages of virtual banking (ICAI)?
A:: Lower cost of handling a transaction; increased speed of response to customers; lower cost of operating the branch network and reduced staff costs; improved range of services available rapidly, accurately and at the customer's convenience.
