## In plain words

### Same twelve and a half per cent, different sums

Kesar has just read BrewBucks India's annual report.

> **Kesar:** BrewBucks India earns 12.5% on its shareholders' money. Our café company earns 12.5% too. We are as good as the Americans!
> **Dev:** Same answer, different sums, Kesar-ji. They keep ₹10 of every ₹100 of sales; our cafés keep ₹3.75. We reach 12.5% because most of our cafés are bought with borrowed money.
> **Kesar:** Borrowing is a strategy.
> **Dev:** A strategy that sends a bill every month. Present value of that compliment: small.

Profit by itself says little: ₹90 crore is a fortune for a tea stall and a disaster for a bank. **Profitability ratios** compare profit with something, and ICAI groups them by that something:

- with **sales**: how many rupees of each ₹100 of sales remain as gross profit, operating profit or net profit (the **margins**);
- with the **investment**: the return on assets, on capital employed and on the shareholders' equity;
- per **share**: earnings and dividend per share, the owner's view;
- with the **market price** of the share: the price-earnings ratio and the yields, the investor's view.

Tapri's year to 31 March 2026: sales ₹900 crore, profit after tax ₹90 crore, a **net profit ratio** of 10%. The shareholders' funds are ₹300 crore and they earned ₹88 crore after the preference dividend, a **return on equity** of 29.3%. With 4 crore shares, that is ₹22 a share (**EPS**), and at a market price of ₹330 the share sells at 15 times its earnings (the **P/E ratio**).

Dev's point about the cafés is the **DuPont analysis**: return on equity is the product of three ratios, the margin on sales, the sales produced per rupee of assets, and the assets carried per rupee of equity. The last one grows with debt, so it shows how much of the return is borrowed.

::: analogy Three ways to fill the glass
A chaiwala can earn more on his own money in three ways: keep more per cup (margin), sell more cups from the same stall (turnover), or set up a bigger stall with a moneylender's cash (the equity multiplier). The first two are skill. The third works only while each cup earns more than the moneylender charges, and the moneylender comes every month either way.
:::

::: key
Profitability ratios relate profit to sales (margins), to the money invested (ROA, ROCE, ROE), to each share (EPS, DPS) and to the share's price (P/E, yields, market-to-book). Return on investment = margin × turnover, and return on equity = net profit margin × asset turnover × equity multiplier: two companies with the same ROE can be very different businesses.
:::

## How it works

### Four groups

The profitability ratios measure the profitability or the operational efficiency of the firm. They reflect the final results of business operations; they are some of the most closely watched and widely quoted ratios, and management attempts to maximise them to maximise the firm's value (SM 3.18). ICAI's four groups (SM 3.19):

| Group | Ratios |
|---|---|
| (i) Based on sales | Gross profit ratio; net profit ratio; operating profit ratio; expenses ratios |
| (ii) Overall return on assets / investments | Return on investment (ROI): return on assets (ROA), return on capital employed (ROCE), return on equity (ROE) |
| (iii) From the owner's point of view | Earnings per share (EPS); dividend per share (DPS); dividend pay-out ratio |
| (iv) Market / valuation / investors | Price-earnings ratio; dividend and earnings yield; market value to book value per share; Q ratio |

### Margins: every line of the profit statement over sales

Tapri's statement of profit for 2025-26, each line as a percentage of sales:

| Particulars | ₹ crore | % of sales | Ratio |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Sales | 900 | 100.00 | |
| Less: Cost of goods sold | 540 | 60.00 | COGS ratio |
| **Gross profit** | **360** | **40.00** | Gross profit ratio |
| Less: Administrative expenses | 100 | 11.11 | |
| Less: Selling and distribution expenses | 128 | 14.22 | |
| **Operating profit (EBIT)** | **132** | **14.67** | Operating profit ratio |
| Less: Interest | 12 | 1.33 | Financial expenses ratio |
| **Profit before tax** | **120** | **13.33** | Pre-tax profit ratio |
| Less: Tax at 25% | 30 | 3.33 | |
| **Profit after tax** | **90** | **10.00** | Net profit ratio |

::: steps
1. **Gross profit ratio** = gross profit ÷ sales × 100 = **40%**: the percentage of each sale in rupees remaining after payment for the goods sold. It depends on the relationship between price, volume and costs; a high gross profit margin is a favourable sign of good management (SM 3.20).
2. **Operating profit ratio** = operating profit ÷ sales × 100 = **14.67%**, where operating profit = sales − COGS − operating expenses, often referred to as EBIT. It is the percentage of each sale that remains after all costs and expenses except interest and taxes, followed closely by analysts because it focuses on operating results (SM 3.20–3.21).
3. **Expenses ratios**: COGS ratio 60%; **operating expenses ratio** = (administrative + selling and distribution expenses) ÷ sales = 228 ÷ 900 = 25.33%; **operating ratio** = (COGS + operating expenses) ÷ sales = 768 ÷ 900 = **85.33%**, the cost of earning each rupee of sales; **financial expenses ratio** = 12 ÷ 900 = 1.33% (financial expenses exclude taxes, loss due to theft, goods destroyed by fire and the like). Administration and selling and distribution expense ratios are calculated the same way (SM 3.21).
4. **Net profit ratio** = net profit (EAT) ÷ sales × 100 = **10%**; the **pre-tax profit ratio** = EBT ÷ sales × 100 = 13.33%. It finds the proportion of revenue that finds its way into profits after meeting all expenses (SM 3.20).
:::

Since operating profit is what sales leave after COGS and operating expenses, **operating ratio + operating profit ratio = 100%**: 85.33% + 14.67%. ICAI used exactly this in May 2024 Q1(a), where an operating ratio of 85% gave an operating profit ratio of 15%.

### Returns: profit over the money invested

ROI is "the most important ratio of all": the percentage return on funds invested in the business, which tells the owner whether all the effort put into the business has been worthwhile. It splits into two ratios you already know (SM 3.22):

$$\text{ROI} = \frac{\text{Return}}{\text{Sales}} \times \frac{\text{Sales}}{\text{Investment}} \qquad = \text{Profitability ratio} \times \text{Investment turnover}$$

So ROI can be improved by improving the margin, the turnover, or both. "Investment" may be total assets or net assets; funds employed in net assets are called **capital employed**, which is net worth plus debt; and the return may be measured in several ways according to the requirement and the information available (SM 3.23). Tapri (₹ crore): total assets 505; capital employed 435 (equity 300 + preference 25 + debt 110); EBIT 132; tax rate 25%.

**Return on equity** = (profit after tax − preference dividend) ÷ net worth (equity shareholders' funds) × 100. It measures how profitably the owners' funds have been utilised, and it is one of the most important indicators of a firm's profitability and potential growth. But "two companies can have the same return on equity, yet one can be a much better business" (SM 3.24–3.25), which is where DuPont comes in.

### DuPont: one return, three engines

A finance executive at E.I. Du Pont de Nemours and Co. created the DuPont system of financial analysis in 1919; it is still used around the world as the basis of the components that make up return on equity (SM 3.25):

$$\text{ROE} = \frac{\text{Net income}}{\text{Sales}} \times \frac{\text{Sales}}{\text{Assets}} \qquad \times \frac{\text{Assets}}{\text{Shareholders’ equity}}$$

Tapri Kulhad Cafés Ltd (Tapri's café subsidiary, with no preference shares): sales ₹50 crore, profit after tax ₹1.875 crore, total assets ₹40 crore, financed by ₹15 crore of equity and ₹25 crore of 10% loans.

::: steps
1. **Net profit margin** = 1.875 ÷ 50 = 3.75%: the after-tax profit generated for each rupee of revenue. It varies across industries, so compare with competitors; it is a safety cushion, and the lower it is, the less room for error: a business with a 1% margin has no room for flawed execution (SM 3.25–3.26).
2. **Asset turnover** (investment or capital turnover) = 50 ÷ 40 = 1.25: how effectively the company converts its assets into sales. It tends to be inversely related to the net profit margin, which lets an investor compare a low-profit, high-volume model with a high-profit, low-volume one (SM 3.26).
3. **Equity multiplier** = 40 ÷ 15 = 2.67: a measure of financial leverage. A company with terrible sales and margins can take on excessive debt and artificially increase its ROE; the multiplier shows what portion of ROE is the result of debt (SM 3.26).
:::

BrewBucks India Pvt Ltd also earns 12.5%: sales ₹80 crore, profit after tax ₹8 crore, assets ₹80 crore, equity ₹64 crore, so 10% × 1.00 × 1.25. Leave out the multiplier to see what each would earn debt-free (the Study Material's own analysis in Example 1, SM 3.27): the cafés 3.75% × 1.25 = 4.69%, BrewBucks 10% × 1.00 = 10%. Of the cafés' 12.5%, only 4.69 points come from margins and sales and 7.81 points from debt at work; for BrewBucks, 2.5 points. On the Study Material's reasoning, the company whose ROE comes more from its own sales and margins is the more attractive one.

### Per share, and against the share price

Tapri has 4 crore equity shares of ₹10 and paid a dividend of ₹5.50 a share; the market price is ₹330; net worth is ₹300 crore.

::: steps
1. **Earnings per share** = net profit available to equity shareholders ÷ number of equity shares outstanding = (90 − 2) ÷ 4 = **₹22**. It is the profitability of the firm from the ordinary shareholder's point of view (SM 3.27).
2. **Dividend per share** = total dividend paid to equity shareholders ÷ number of equity shares = **₹5.50**: how much of the profit is distributed per share (SM 3.27). **Dividend pay-out ratio** = DPS ÷ EPS = 5.50 ÷ 22 = **25%**: the extent to which earnings are paid out and, by difference, retained (SM 3.28).
3. **Price-earnings ratio** = market price per share ÷ EPS = 330 ÷ 22 = **15 times**. It indicates the expectation of equity investors about the firm's earnings, and is taken as a summary measure of growth potential, risk characteristics, shareholders' orientation, corporate image and degree of liquidity. It indicates the payback period to investors; a higher P/E could mean the share is over-valued or that investors expect high growth (SM 3.28).
4. **Dividend yield** = DPS ÷ MPS × 100 = 5.50 ÷ 330 = **1.67%**; **earnings yield** (earnings-price ratio) = EPS ÷ MPS × 100 = **6.67%**, the reciprocal of the P/E. Dividend (%) shows the return on the paid-up value of shares; yield (%) is the indicator of true return, with share capital taken at its market value (SM 3.28–3.29).
5. **Market value to book value per share** = 330 ÷ (300 ÷ 4) = 330 ÷ 75 = **4.4**: the market response to the shareholders' investment; the higher, the better the shareholders' position in terms of return and capital gains (SM 3.29).
6. **Q ratio** (proposed by James Tobin) = market value of equity and liabilities ÷ estimated replacement cost of assets. It relates market valuation to intrinsic value: equilibrium is 1; below 1 the stock could be undervalued, above 1 overvalued (SM 3.29).
:::

## Formats and formulas

*Reproduce these from memory. Every "× 100" ratio is a percentage; the others are in times or rupees.*

::: formula Margins on sales
$$\text{Gross profit ratio} \qquad = \frac{\text{Gross profit}}{\text{Sales}} \times 100 \qquad \text{Net profit ratio} = \frac{\text{EAT}}{\text{Sales}} \times 100$$
$$\text{Pre-tax profit ratio} = \frac{\text{EBT}}{\text{Sales}} \times 100 \qquad \text{Operating profit ratio} = \frac{\text{EBIT}}{\text{Sales}} \times 100$$
Operating profit = sales − COGS − operating expenses (SM 3.20–3.21).
:::

::: formula Expenses ratios
$$\text{COGS ratio} = \frac{\text{COGS}}{\text{Sales}} \times 100 \qquad \text{Operating expenses ratio} \qquad = \frac{A + S}{\text{Sales}} \times 100$$
$$\text{Operating ratio} \qquad = \frac{\text{COGS} + \text{Operating expenses}}{\text{Sales}} \times 100$$
$$\text{Financial expenses ratio} \qquad = \frac{\text{Financial expenses}}{\text{Sales}} \times 100$$
A = administrative expenses; S = selling and distribution overheads. Financial expenses exclude taxes, loss due to theft, goods destroyed by fire etc. (SM 3.21).
:::

::: formula Return on investment
$$\text{ROI} = \frac{\text{Return}}{\text{Investment}} \times 100 \qquad = \frac{\text{Return}}{\text{Sales}} \times \frac{\text{Sales}}{\text{Investment}}$$
(SM 3.22)
:::

::: formula Return on assets
$$\text{ROA} = \frac{\text{PAT}}{\text{Average total assets}} \qquad \text{or} \qquad \frac{\text{PAT} + \text{Interest}}{\text{Average total assets}}$$
$$\text{ROTA} = \frac{\text{EBIT}(1 - t)}{\text{Average total assets}} \qquad \text{RONA} = \frac{\text{EBIT}(1 - t)}{\text{Average net assets}}$$
PAT = net profit after taxes; t = tax rate. Average tangible assets or average fixed assets may replace total assets in the first two; total assets are sometimes used instead of average assets (SM 3.23).
:::

::: formula Return on capital employed
$$\text{ROCE (pre-tax)} \qquad = \frac{\text{EBIT}}{\text{Capital employed}} \times 100$$
$$\text{ROCE (post-tax)} \qquad = \frac{\text{EBIT}(1 - t)}{\text{Capital employed}} \times 100$$
Sometimes (PAT + interest) ÷ capital employed. Capital employed = total assets − current liabilities = fixed assets + working capital = equity + long-term debt; include intangibles (goodwill, patents, trade marks), exclude fictitious assets (deferred expenses); use average capital employed if available (SM 3.24, 3.30).
:::

::: formula Return on equity and the DuPont model
$$\text{ROE} \qquad = \frac{\text{PAT} - \text{Preference dividend}}{\text{Equity shareholders funds}} \times 100$$
$$\text{ROE} = \text{Net profit margin} \qquad \times \text{Asset turnover} \qquad \times \text{Equity multiplier}$$
Net profit margin = net income ÷ sales; asset (investment, capital) turnover = sales ÷ investment, assets or capital; equity multiplier = investment, assets or capital ÷ shareholders' equity (SM 3.25–3.26).
:::

::: formula Owner's ratios
$$\text{EPS} = \frac{\text{Equity earnings}}{\text{No. of equity shares}} \qquad \text{DPS} = \frac{\text{Equity dividend}}{\text{No. of equity shares}}$$
$$\text{Dividend pay-out ratio} = \frac{\text{DPS}}{\text{EPS}}$$
Equity earnings = net profit available to equity shareholders; equity dividend = total dividend paid to equity shareholders; both are divided by the number of equity shares outstanding. In the absence of preference dividend, PAT is the earnings available to equity shareholders (SM 3.27–3.28, 3.30).
:::

::: formula Market ratios
$$\text{P/E ratio} = \frac{\text{MPS}}{\text{EPS}} \qquad \text{Earnings yield} = \frac{\text{EPS}}{\text{MPS}} \times 100$$
$$\text{Dividend yield} = \frac{\text{DPS}}{\text{MPS}} \times 100 \qquad \text{or} \qquad \frac{\text{Dividend} \pm \text{Price change}}{\text{Initial share price}} \times 100$$
$$\text{MV/BV} = \frac{\text{Share price}}{\text{Book value per share}} \qquad \text{Q ratio} \qquad = \frac{\text{Market value of a company}}{\text{Replacement cost of assets}}$$
Share price = the average or the closing share price; book value per share = net worth ÷ number of equity shares; price change = the change in share price over the period. The Q ratio is also written as market value of equity and liabilities ÷ estimated replacement cost of assets (SM 3.28–3.29).
:::

## Worked examples

:::: example Tapri Cold Chain: next year's plan in seven ratios
Tapri Cold Chain Ltd, the new refrigerated-transport subsidiary, plans to employ total assets of ₹20 crore next year, 40% financed by 10% debentures and the rest by equity; it will have no current liabilities. Direct costs are estimated at ₹15 crore and other operating expenses at ₹3 crore. Services will be billed at 140% of direct costs. The tax rate is 25%.

**Required:**

(i) Prepare the projected statement of profit.

(ii) Calculate the gross profit ratio, operating profit ratio and operating ratio.

(iii) Calculate the net profit margin, return on assets (on operating profit after tax), pre-tax ROCE, asset turnover and return on equity.

(iv) Verify the ROE with the DuPont model.

(v) Dev suggests financing 60% of the assets with the same 10% debentures. Calculate the new ROE, and explain the change.

::: answer Show the working
**(i) Projected statement of profit**

| Particulars | ₹ crore |
|---|---:|
| Sales (140% of ₹15 crore) | 21.00 |
| Less: Direct costs | 15.00 |
| **Gross profit** | **6.00** |
| Less: Other operating expenses | 3.00 |
| **EBIT** | **3.00** |
| Less: Interest (10% of 40% of ₹20 crore = 10% × ₹8 crore) | 0.80 |
| **Profit before tax** | **2.20** |
| Less: Tax at 25% | 0.55 |
| **Profit after tax** | **1.65** |

**(ii) Ratios on sales**

- Gross profit ratio = 6 ÷ 21 × 100 = **28.57%**
- Operating profit ratio = 3 ÷ 21 × 100 = **14.29%**
- Operating ratio = (15 + 3) ÷ 21 × 100 = **85.71%** (check: 85.71% + 14.29% = 100%)

**(iii) Returns**

| Ratio | Working | Result |
|---|---|---:|
| Net profit margin | 1.65 ÷ 21 | 7.86% |
| Return on assets, EBIT(1 − t) ÷ assets | 3 × 0.75 ÷ 20 | 11.25% |
| ROCE, pre-tax (capital employed = ₹20 crore, no current liabilities) | 3 ÷ 20 | 15.00% |
| Asset turnover | 21 ÷ 20 | 1.05 times |
| Return on equity (equity = 60% of ₹20 crore = ₹12 crore) | 1.65 ÷ 12 | 13.75% |

**(iv) DuPont check**

$$\text{ROE} = 7.857\% \times 1.05 \times \frac{20}{12} \qquad = 7.857\% \times 1.05 \times 1.667 = 13.75\%$$

**(v) 60% debt**

Debt = ₹12 crore, interest ₹1.20 crore; equity ₹8 crore. PBT = 3.00 − 1.20 = ₹1.80 crore; PAT = ₹1.35 crore; ROE = 1.35 ÷ 8 = **16.88%**.

ROE rises although profit after tax falls, because the assets earn a pre-tax return (ROCE) of 15%, more than the 10% paid on the debentures: the surplus on the extra borrowed ₹4 crore goes to a smaller equity base. In DuPont terms the margin falls (1.35 ÷ 21 = 6.43%) but the equity multiplier rises from 1.67 to 2.5. The advantage reverses if the return on assets falls below the cost of debt (see [[fm.lev.leverage|trading on equity]]).
:::

:::: example Cafés against BrewBucks: whose 12.5% is better?
Kesar wants to tell the board that Tapri Kulhad Cafés Ltd "earns as well as BrewBucks India". Dev extracts (₹ crore):

| Particulars | Tapri Kulhad Cafés | BrewBucks India |
|---|---:|---:|
| Sales | 50.00 | 80.00 |
| Profit after tax | 1.875 | 8.00 |
| Total assets | 40.00 | 80.00 |
| Shareholders' equity | 15.00 | 64.00 |

Neither company has preference shares.

**Required:**

(i) Calculate each company's ROE using the DuPont model.

(ii) Calculate the ROE each would earn without the equity multiplier, and the part of ROE that is due to leverage.

(iii) Advise Kesar on his claim.

::: answer Show the working
**(i) DuPont**

| Component | Tapri Kulhad Cafés | BrewBucks India |
|---|---:|---:|
| Net profit margin (PAT ÷ sales) | 1.875 ÷ 50 = 3.75% | 8 ÷ 80 = 10.00% |
| Asset turnover (sales ÷ assets) | 50 ÷ 40 = 1.25 | 80 ÷ 80 = 1.00 |
| Equity multiplier (assets ÷ equity) | 40 ÷ 15 = 2.667 | 80 ÷ 64 = 1.25 |
| **ROE** | **12.50%** | **12.50%** |

**(ii) Without the multiplier**

| Particulars | Tapri Kulhad Cafés | BrewBucks India |
|---|---:|---:|
| Margin × turnover | 3.75% × 1.25 = 4.69% | 10% × 1.00 = 10.00% |
| Part of ROE due to leverage | 12.50 − 4.69 = 7.81 points | 12.50 − 10.00 = 2.50 points |

**(iii) Advice**

The two returns on equity are equal, but they are earned differently. BrewBucks India earns 10% on its sales and assets before leverage, and only 2.5 points of its ROE come from debt. The cafés earn 4.69% from operations and 7.81 points, more than half their ROE, from debt: their margin is thin (3.75%), so there is little room for error, and each ₹1 of equity carries ₹2.67 of assets. The claim is not correct: on equal ROE, the business whose return arises from its own margins and sales is the more attractive. The cafés should work on their margin, or on the sales produced by each rupee of assets, rather than on further borrowing.
:::

:::: example Malwa Foods Ltd: the share price talks back
Malwa Foods Ltd, a listed snack maker that competes with Tapri's samosas, has 50 lakh equity shares of ₹10 and 9% preference share capital of ₹10 crore. Its share trades at ₹240, at a P/E of 12 times and 1.6 times its book value per share. It pays out 40% of its earnings to equity shareholders. A year ago the share was ₹200.

**Required:**

(i) Calculate the EPS, DPS, dividend yield and earnings yield.

(ii) Calculate the book value per share, net worth and return on equity.

(iii) Calculate the profit after tax, the equity dividend coverage and the total dividend coverage (profit after tax ÷ all dividends).

(iv) Calculate the yield including the change in share price over the year.

::: answer Show the working
**(i) Per share and yields**

- EPS = MPS ÷ P/E = 240 ÷ 12 = **₹20**
- DPS = 40% × ₹20 = **₹8**
- Dividend yield = 8 ÷ 240 × 100 = **3.33%**
- Earnings yield = 20 ÷ 240 × 100 = **8.33%** (= 1 ÷ 12)

**(ii) Book value and ROE**

- Book value per share = MPS ÷ (MV/BV) = 240 ÷ 1.6 = **₹150**
- Net worth (equity shareholders' funds) = ₹150 × 50 lakh shares = **₹75 crore**
- Earnings for equity = ₹20 × 50 lakh = ₹10 crore; ROE = 10 ÷ 75 = **13.33%** (also EPS ÷ book value per share)

**(iii) Profit and dividend cover**

- Preference dividend = 9% × ₹10 crore = ₹0.90 crore; PAT = 10 + 0.90 = **₹10.90 crore**
- Equity dividend = ₹8 × 50 lakh = ₹4 crore; equity dividend coverage = (10.90 − 0.90) ÷ 4 = **2.5 times**
- Total dividend coverage = 10.90 ÷ (4 + 0.90) = **2.22 times**

**(iv) Yield with the price change**

$$\text{Yield} = \frac{\text{Dividend} + \text{Change in price}}{\text{Initial price}} \times 100 \qquad = \frac{8 + (240 - 200)}{200} \times 100 \qquad = 24\%$$
:::

## Under the hood

### The variants, and how to choose

- **Which return, which investment.** The Study Material keeps saying that returns and investments "may vary as per the requirement and availability of information" (SM 3.23). Its notes: EBIT = PBIT, EAT = PAT, EBT = PBT; numerator in correspondence with the denominator; average capital employed if available; assumptions stated if needed (SM 3.30). A return after interest (PAT) belongs over equity; a return before interest (EBIT, or PAT + interest, or EBIT(1 − t)) belongs over assets or capital employed, which lenders also finance.
- **ROE for all shareholders.** If the return to equity and preference shareholders together is wanted, profit after tax before preference dividend is divided by total shareholders' funds including preference capital (SM 3.25).
- **Two ROAs.** "Here, net profit is exclusive of interest": because lenders also finance assets, ROA can add interest back, or use EBIT(1 − t) (SM 3.23). September 2025 Q1(b) took ROA as EBIT(1 − t) ÷ total assets and accepted a net profit margin either on PAT or on EBIT(1 − t).

### Coverage of dividends

The preference and equity dividend coverage ratios are in [[fm.ratio.liquidity|the coverage ratios]]. May 2026 Q1(c) used a **total dividend coverage ratio** = PAT ÷ (equity dividend + preference dividend) and asked for the preference dividend rate from it, by solving (6,00,000 + P) ÷ (1,92,000 + P) = 2.863 for P.

### Extended DuPont

RTP January 2026 MCQ 3 wrote ROE = NPM × TATR × x/y and asked for x/y: total assets ÷ equity, the equity multiplier. When there are preference shares, NPM × TAT × EM gives PAT ÷ equity, which is ROE only after the preference dividend is deducted from PAT; work the DuPont split on companies without preference capital, or say what you did.

### Where these ratios lead

The P/E and the earnings yield reappear in valuing shares and the cost of equity ([[fm.coc.equity|cost of equity]]); the pay-out ratio and retention drive the dividend models ([[fm.div.models|Walter and Gordon]]); trading on equity, the reason the equity multiplier can raise ROE, is in [[fm.lev.leverage|financial leverage]].

## Answer frame

### "Discuss the DuPont model" (SM 3.65, theory questions 4 and 6)

1. Origin: created in 1919 by a finance executive at E.I. Du Pont de Nemours and Co.; used worldwide as the basis of the components of ROE.
2. The model: ROE = net profit margin × asset (investment) turnover × equity multiplier.
3. Each component, its formula and meaning: net profit margin (profit for each rupee of revenue; a safety cushion); asset turnover (how effectively assets are converted into sales; inversely related to margin); equity multiplier (financial leverage; shows what portion of ROE results from debt).
4. Use: by examining each input, the sources of a company's ROE can be discovered and compared with competitors; a high ROE from leverage is less attractive than one from margins and turnover.
5. A two-line numerical illustration.

### "Discuss any three ratios computed for investment analysis" (SM 3.65, theory question 1)

The Study Material's answer key points to paragraph 3.4.2, the return on investment ratios. For each of ROA, ROCE and ROE: definition, formula with the components named, interpretation (ROCE above the borrowing rate; ROE as an indicator of profitability and potential growth), and who uses it.

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: the preference dividend that went to the equity shareholders
EPS taken as PAT ÷ shares: Malwa's ₹10.90 crore ÷ 50 lakh = ₹21.80 instead of ₹20, and every ratio built on it (P/E, yields, ROE) goes wrong. **Fix:** deduct the preference dividend first; only what is left is available to equity shareholders.
:::

::: mistake The return that did not match its investment
EBIT over equity, or PAT over total assets labelled "ROCE". **Fix:** returns before interest (EBIT, EBIT(1 − t), PAT + interest) go over assets or capital employed; returns after interest and preference dividend go over equity shareholders' funds.
:::

::: mistake DuPont with two engines
Multiplying margin and turnover and calling it ROE (the cafés' 4.69%). **Fix:** that product is a return on assets; ROE needs the equity multiplier too.
:::

::: mistake Fictitious assets in capital employed
Adding deferred expenses or accumulated losses to capital employed lowers ROCE without a single rupee more invested. **Fix:** include intangibles such as goodwill, patents and trade marks; exclude fictitious assets (SM 3.24).
:::

## Practice

Q: A company's ROE is 18% and its net profit margin 4%. Its total assets are ₹50 crore and shareholders' equity ₹20 crore. What is its total asset turnover (times)?
= 1.79..1.81
> Equity multiplier = 50 ÷ 20 = 2.5; ROE = NPM × TAT × EM, so TAT = 18% ÷ (4% × 2.5) = 1.8 times.

Q: Case scenario. To lift Tapri Kulhad Cafés' ROE before an investor meeting, Kesar proposes to borrow ₹5 crore and buy back shares of the same amount, with sales, operating profit and the asset base unchanged. Dev warns that this changes only one part of the DuPont equation directly. Which part?
- [ ] Net profit margin, because interest is an operating expense
- [ ] Asset turnover, because borrowing adds to the assets
- [x] Equity multiplier, because the same assets are financed by less equity
- [ ] None, because a buyback does not affect ROE
> Assets are unchanged and equity falls, so assets ÷ equity rises. The margin also falls indirectly (more interest, lower PAT), but the direct lever is the multiplier: ROE rises only while the return on assets exceeds the cost of the new debt.

Q: According to ICAI, a Q ratio of more than 1 could mean that the stock is:
- [ ] Undervalued
- [x] Overvalued
- [ ] In equilibrium
- [ ] Not quoted
> Q = market value of equity and liabilities ÷ replacement cost of assets. Equilibrium is 1; below 1 the stock could be undervalued, above 1 overvalued (SM 3.29).

Q: A company's profit after tax is ₹50 lakh, its 10% preference share capital is ₹1 crore and it has 8 lakh equity shares. What is its EPS?
- [ ] ₹6.25
- [x] ₹5.00
- [ ] ₹6.00
- [ ] ₹4.37
> Preference dividend = ₹10 lakh; earnings for equity = ₹40 lakh; EPS = 40 ÷ 8 = ₹5. ₹6.25 forgets the preference dividend.

Q: A firm's cost of goods sold is 62% of sales and its operating expenses 23% of sales. Which statements are correct? Select all that apply.
- [x] Its operating ratio is 85%
- [x] Its operating profit ratio is 15%
- [ ] Its gross profit ratio is 15%
- [x] Its gross profit ratio is 38%
> Operating ratio = 62% + 23% = 85%; operating profit ratio = 100% − 85% = 15%; gross profit ratio = 100% − 62% = 38%.

## Recap

- Gross profit, operating profit (EBIT) and net profit ratios = that profit ÷ sales × 100; operating ratio = (COGS + operating expenses) ÷ sales, and operating ratio + operating profit ratio = 100%.
- ROI = margin × investment turnover; ROA = PAT (or PAT + interest, or EBIT(1 − t)) ÷ average assets.
- ROCE = EBIT (pre-tax) or EBIT(1 − t) (post-tax) ÷ capital employed; it should exceed the borrowing rate; intangibles in, fictitious assets out.
- ROE = (PAT − preference dividend) ÷ equity shareholders' funds.
- DuPont: ROE = net profit margin × asset turnover × equity multiplier (assets ÷ equity); the multiplier shows the part of ROE that comes from debt.
- EPS = earnings for equity ÷ equity shares; DPS; pay-out = DPS ÷ EPS.
- P/E = MPS ÷ EPS; dividend yield = DPS ÷ MPS (or dividend ± price change ÷ initial price); earnings yield = EPS ÷ MPS; MV/BV; Q ratio = market value ÷ replacement cost (1 = equilibrium).
- Same ROE, different businesses: ask which engine produced it. (Debt is a strategy that sends a bill every month.)

## Flashcards

Q:: ICAI's four groups of profitability ratios?
A:: (i) Related to sales; (ii) related to overall return on assets / investments; (iii) required for analysis from the owner's point of view; (iv) related to market / valuation / investors.

Q:: Formula: operating ratio, and how it relates to the operating profit ratio?
A:: (COGS + operating expenses) ÷ sales × 100; operating ratio + operating profit ratio = 100% when operating profit = sales − COGS − operating expenses.

Q:: ROI split into two ratios?
A:: ROI = (return ÷ sales) × (sales ÷ investment) = profitability ratio × investment turnover ratio.

Q:: Formula: ROCE pre-tax and post-tax, and three ways to find capital employed?
A:: EBIT ÷ capital employed × 100; EBIT(1 − t) ÷ capital employed × 100. Capital employed = total assets − current liabilities = fixed assets + working capital = equity + long-term debt.

Q:: ICAI's rule for ROCE against the borrowing rate?
A:: ROCE should always be higher than the rate at which the company borrows.

Q:: Formula: return on equity?
A:: (Net profit after taxes − preference dividend, if any) ÷ net worth (equity shareholders' funds) × 100.

Q:: DuPont model: the three components of ROE?
A:: Net profit margin (net income ÷ sales) × asset turnover (sales ÷ assets) × equity multiplier (assets ÷ shareholders' equity).

Q:: What does the equity multiplier show in the DuPont model?
A:: Financial leverage: what portion of the return on equity is the result of debt.

Q:: Formula: dividend pay-out ratio?
A:: Dividend per equity share (DPS) ÷ earnings per share (EPS).

Q:: What does a high P/E ratio indicate, per ICAI?
A:: Either the share is over-valued or investors expect high growth rates in future; the P/E also indicates the payback period to investors.

Q:: Two formulas for dividend yield?
A:: DPS ÷ MPS × 100; or (dividend ± change in share price) ÷ initial share price × 100.

Q:: Q ratio: who proposed it, the formula, and the equilibrium value?
A:: James Tobin; market value of equity and liabilities ÷ estimated replacement cost of assets; equilibrium at 1 (below 1 possibly undervalued, above 1 possibly overvalued).
