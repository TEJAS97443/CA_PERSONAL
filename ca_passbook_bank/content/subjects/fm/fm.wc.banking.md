## In plain words

### Four doors to the same ₹2 crore

The festive season is ten weeks away, and Tapri's premix division needs ₹2 crore for 90 days to build stock.

> **Kesar:** Just take ₹2 crore from the bank. Money is money.
> **Dev:** Money has a price tag, and some tags are hidden in the small print. A bank loan that makes us keep part of it idle in a current account. Discounting our distributors' bills. Commercial paper. Skipping a supplier's discount.
> **Kesar:** Four doors, all leading to ₹2 crore?
> **Dev:** Four doors, four rents. I will calculate each one per rupee we can actually spend.

Once the finance manager knows how much working capital the business needs, the next step is to **arrange the funds**. First he splits the need in two. The part that is always there whatever the season, the **permanent working capital**, should come from long-term sources such as equity and long-term debt. The part that comes and goes with the season, the **temporary working capital**, can come from short-term sources.

Short-term sources are of two kinds. **Spontaneous** sources arise by themselves in the course of business: suppliers' credit, wages and taxes not yet paid. **Negotiated** sources have to be arranged with a lender: loans from other companies, commercial paper, public deposits, bill discounting, factoring and, above all, **bank credit**.

Banks are the biggest lenders of working capital. For decades they followed limits set by RBI committees (Tandon and Chore) under a concept called maximum permissible bank finance; RBI withdrew it in 1997, and each bank now uses its own Board-approved method. Bank credit comes in seven forms, from the cash credit (draw what you need up to a limit) to the bank guarantee (the bank promises a third party on your behalf).

The price of each source is not just its quoted rate: it is what you pay divided by what you can actually use.

::: analogy Paying for a family wedding
Some of the money comes on its own: the caterer will be paid after the function, the relatives' help asks no interest (spontaneous). The rest has to be asked for: a loan from a cousin's business, a gold loan, a credit card you draw on as bills arrive and repay when the gifts come in. A rich uncle might take your signed IOU, but only because your family's name is excellent: that is commercial paper. And a loan that makes you keep a fixed deposit with the same bank is dearer than its rate says.
:::

::: key
Finance permanent working capital from long-term sources and temporary working capital from short-term ones. Among short-term sources, compare the effective cost per rupee you can actually use, and weigh credit rating, feasibility, reliability and restrictions before choosing.
:::

## How it works

### Two needs, two kinds of source

After determining the amount of working capital required, the next step is to arrange the funds (SM 9.102). The finance manager should bifurcate the requirement between **permanent** working capital, always needed irrespective of sales fluctuations and hence financed by long-term sources such as debt and equity, and **temporary** working capital, which may be financed by short-term sources (SM 9.102). This is the matching (hedging) approach of [[fm.wc.basics|working capital and its financing policy]].

**Spontaneous sources** naturally arise in the course of business operations: trade credit, credit from employees, credit from suppliers of services. **Negotiated sources** have to be specifically negotiated with lenders, say commercial banks, financial institutions or the general public (SM 9.102). The choice of a source, or a combination, is guided by (SM 9.102–9.103): (i) cost factor; (ii) impact on credit rating; (iii) feasibility; (iv) reliability; (v) restrictions; (vi) the hedging or matching approach, i.e. financing assets with the same maturity as the assets.

### The sources, one by one

| Source | What ICAI says (SM 9.103–9.106) |
|---|---|
| Trade credit | Extended by sellers or service providers; about one-third of total short-term requirements; credit without much formality is open account trade credit; see [[fm.wc.payables|management of payables]] |
| Bills payable | The purchaser gives a written promise to pay on demand or at a fixed future date; simple, easily available, lesser explicit cost; helpful to small and medium enterprises |
| Accrued expenses | Services availed but not yet paid for (wages, salaries, taxes, duties); built-in, automatic, interest free, with no explicit or implicit cost |
| Inter-corporate loans and deposits | Firms with surplus funds lend for a short period; rate higher than the bank rate, depending on the borrower's financial soundness; reduces dependence on banks |
| Commercial paper | An unsecured promissory note for a short period, for highly rated borrowers; details below |
| Funds from operations | Profit and depreciation; working capital increases by the funds generated |
| Public deposits | For well-established big companies with a huge capital base; short and medium term |
| Bills discounting | The supplier draws a bill of exchange on the buyer, gets it accepted, and discounts it |
| Bill rediscounting scheme | Introduced by RBI from 1 November 1970 to develop a bill market; all licensed scheduled banks may offer eligible bills to RBI for rediscount |
| Factoring | The firm sells its trade debts at a discount to a factor, with or without recourse; see [[fm.wc.receivables|management of receivables]] |

**Commercial paper (CP)** is an unsecured promissory note issued by a firm to raise funds for a short period. It enables highly rated corporate borrowers to borrow short term and gives investors an instrument with a freely negotiable interest rate. Maturity ranges from a minimum of **7 days to less than 1 year** from the date of issue; it is issued in denominations of **₹5 lakh or multiples** (SM 9.104).

| Advantages to the issuer (SM 9.104) | Limitations (SM 9.104–9.105) |
|---|---|
| (a) Sold on an unsecured basis, with no restrictive conditions | (i) Only highly rated firms can use it; new and moderately rated firms generally cannot issue CP |
| (b) Maturing CP can be repaid by selling new CP: a continuous source of funds | (ii) CP can neither be redeemed before maturity nor extended beyond maturity |
| (c) Maturity can be tailored to the issuer's requirement | |
| (d) It can be issued even when the money market is tight | |
| (e) Its cost is generally lower than that of commercial bank loans | |

### Bank finance: from MPBF to the bank's own method

Banks in India constitute the major suppliers of working capital credit; some term-lending institutions also offer it. Two committees, the **Tandon Committee** and the **Chore Committee**, evolved the guidelines and parameters that laid the foundations of working capital financing (SM 9.106). What applies now (SM 9.106–9.107):

1. The Reserve Bank of India **withdrew** the prescription for assessing working capital needs on the basis of **Maximum Permissible Bank Finance (MPBF)** in **April 1997**.
2. Banks are now free to evolve, **with the approval of their Boards**, methods for assessing the working capital requirements of borrowers, **within the prudential guidelines and exposure norms** prescribed.
3. They must still take into account RBI's instructions on **directed credit** (priority sector, export, etc.) and on **prohibition of credit** (bridge finance, rediscounting of bills earlier discounted by NBFCs) while framing their lending policies.
4. All instructions relating to MPBF stand withdrawn; the guidelines on lending discipline in appraisal, sanction, monitoring and utilisation cease to be mandatory, though banks may incorporate those they consider necessary in their own policies.

::: warning
RBI norms for bank credit are version-sensitive. Write them as the Study Material states them, and verify against the Study Material and Statutory Update for your attempt.
:::

A bank's own method can still be stated as a percentage: in the Study Material's case scenario the bank grants 75% of net current assets as advance against working capital, and asks for 10% of the loan to be kept as a minimum average balance in a current account (SM 9.127, 9.156). The other 25%, the borrower's **margin**, comes from its own long-term funds.

### The seven forms of bank credit

In ICAI's order (SM 9.107–9.108):

| Form | ICAI's description |
|---|---|
| Cash credit | Credit facility given on a continuous basis; the borrower is not allowed to exceed the limits sanctioned |
| Bank overdraft | Short-term borrowing for urgent needs, within limits; quickly and easily repaid when no longer required; the bank can call it in at short notice |
| Bills discounting | The seller draws a bill on the buyer, who accepts it; the seller discounts it with his banker, who earmarks a discounting limit |
| Bills acceptance | The company draws a bill of exchange on the bank; the bank accepts it, promising to pay the amount at a specified future date |
| Line of credit | A commitment by a bank to lend a certain amount of funds on demand, specifying the maximum amount |
| Letter of credit | The issuing bank, on a customer's instructions or on its own behalf, undertakes to pay, accept or negotiate (or authorises another bank to do so) against stipulated documents, subject to the specified terms and conditions |
| Bank guarantee | A facility the bank extends on behalf of its client in favour of a third party, the beneficiary of the guarantee |

Chapter 2 of the Study Material adds the bank's other short-term facilities (short-term loans, clean overdrafts, advances against goods, bills purchased, export finance): [[fm.sources.short|short-term sources of finance]]. A cash credit, for instance, charges interest only on the amount actually availed, not on the full limit, and is usually secured by pledge or hypothecation of goods (FM Study Material 2.27).

### The price per usable rupee

Every short-term source is compared on one yardstick, the **effective annual cost**: what the source costs in a year, divided by the funds the firm can actually use.

- **A loan with a minimum balance.** If part of the loan must sit idle in a current account, the interest is paid on the whole loan but only the rest is usable. The Study Material's case: a 16.5% loan with 10% kept as a minimum balance costs 16.5 ÷ 90% = **18.33%** (SM 9.156). Only a balance the firm **would not otherwise maintain** counts; ICAI's old-scheme question says so in those words (RTP December 2021 Q8).
- **A discounted instrument** (a discounted bill, commercial paper): the charge is deducted at the start, so the cost is the charge divided by the **net proceeds**, annualised for the days it runs.
- **Trade credit**: the cost of the discount forgone ([[fm.wc.payables|management of payables]]).
- **Factoring**: the net cost (commission and interest less savings) on the advance received ([[fm.wc.receivables|management of receivables]]).

## Formats and formulas

*Reproduce the effective cost formulas and the comparison layout from memory.*

::: formula Loan with a compensating (minimum) balance
$$\text{Effective cost} = \frac{\text{Interest rate}}{1 - b}$$
b = the minimum balance as a fraction of the loan, counted only if the firm would not otherwise keep it. SM 9.156: 16.5% ÷ (1 − 0.10) = 18.33%. Amount to borrow for a usable sum S: S ÷ (1 − b).
:::

::: formula Discounted bill or commercial paper
$$\text{Effective annual cost} = \frac{\text{Discount} + \text{Charges}}{\text{Net proceeds}} \qquad \times \frac{365}{\text{Days to maturity}}$$
Net proceeds = face value − discount − charges. Never divide by the face value.
:::

::: formula Bank finance as a share of net current assets (SM case scenario)
$$\text{Bank finance} = 75\% \times \text{Net current assets} \qquad \text{Margin} = \text{Net current assets} - \text{Bank finance}$$
Net current assets = current assets − current liabilities other than the bank borrowing. The percentage is the bank's stipulation in the question.
:::

### Comparison statement of short-term sources

| Source | Basis of cost | Effective annual cost |
|---|---|---:|
| Bank loan with minimum balance | Rate ÷ (1 − balance %) | x% |
| Bills discounting | Discount and charges ÷ net proceeds × 365 ÷ days | x% |
| Commercial paper | (Face − net proceeds) ÷ net proceeds × 365 ÷ days | x% |
| Trade credit (discount forgone) | d ÷ (100 − d) × 365 ÷ t | x% |

Rank the sources, choose the cheapest that is feasible, and comment on rating, reliability and restrictions.

An unsecured promissory note issued by a firm to raise funds for a short period; an instrument that enables highly rated corporate borrowers to borrow short term. (SM 9.104)
:::

:::

## Worked examples

:::: example ₹2 crore for 90 days: four sources compared
Tapri's premix division needs about ₹2 crore of usable funds for 90 days to build festive stock. Tapri is a listed company and its rating makes it eligible to issue commercial paper. Four options are available (assume 365 days):

1. **Bank loan** at 11% a year. The bank requires a minimum balance of 15% of the loan in a current account, which Tapri would not otherwise keep.
2. **Forgo a supplier's discount.** The main ingredient supplier offers 1.5/10 net 50, and Tapri usually takes the discount; paying on day 50 instead would provide the funds.
3. **Bills discounting.** Distributors accept 90-day bills; the bank discounts them at 10% a year on the face value and charges a processing fee of 0.25% of the face value.
4. **Commercial paper** for 91 days, issued at ₹98 per ₹100 of face value; rating, issuing and paying agent and stamp costs come to ₹0.20 per ₹100 of face value.

**Required:**

(i) Calculate the effective annual cost of each option.

(ii) Calculate the amount Tapri must borrow under option 1, and the face value of CP it must issue under option 4, to have ₹2 crore to use.

(iii) Rank the options and advise, commenting on the factors other than cost.

::: answer Show the working
**(i) Effective annual cost**

1. Bank loan: 11% ÷ (1 − 0.15) = **12.94%**.
2. Forgone discount, t = 50 − 10 = 40 days: 1.5 ÷ 98.5 × 365 ÷ 40 = **13.90%** nominal (compound: (100 ÷ 98.5)^(365 ÷ 40) − 1 = 14.79%).
3. Bills discounting, per ₹100 of face value: discount = 100 × 10% × 90 ÷ 365 = ₹2.466; fee ₹0.25; net proceeds = ₹97.284.

$$\text{Cost} = \frac{2.466 + 0.25}{97.284} \times \frac{365}{90} \qquad = 11.32\%$$

4. Commercial paper, per ₹100 of face value: net proceeds = ₹98 − ₹0.20 = ₹97.80.

$$\text{Cost} = \frac{100 - 97.80}{97.80} \times \frac{365}{91} \qquad = 9.02\%$$

**(ii) Amounts**

- Bank loan: ₹2,00,00,000 ÷ 0.85 = **₹2,35,29,412**, of which ₹35,29,412 sits idle; interest for 90 days = ₹2,35,29,412 × 11% × 90 ÷ 365 = ₹6,38,195.
- CP: ₹2,00,00,000 ÷ 0.978 = ₹2,04,49,898 of face value; CP is issued in multiples of ₹5 lakh, so Tapri issues **₹2,05,00,000** (41 lots), receiving ₹2,05,00,000 × 0.978 = ₹2,00,49,000 and repaying ₹2,05,00,000 on maturity.

**(iii) Ranking and advice**

| Rank | Source | Effective annual cost |
|---:|---|---:|
| 1 | Commercial paper | 9.02% |
| 2 | Bills discounting | 11.32% |
| 3 | Bank loan with 15% minimum balance | 12.94% |
| 4 | Forgone supplier discount | 13.90% |

**Advice.** Issue commercial paper: it is the cheapest by about 2.3 percentage points, unsecured and free of restrictive conditions (SM 9.104). Points to weigh:

- CP can neither be redeemed before maturity nor extended beyond it (SM 9.105): if festive sales come in early, Tapri cannot repay early; if they come in late, it must roll over by a fresh issue or fall back on the bank. A bank cash credit, where interest runs only on the amount used, is the natural standby.
- Bills discounting, the second choice, depends on distributors agreeing to accept bills, and it is limited by the bank's discounting limit.
- The bank loan's quoted 11% is really 12.94%, because ₹35 lakh must lie idle.
- Forgoing the discount is the dearest, and it raises the price of every purchase while it lasts.
:::

:::: example Narmada Bank assesses Tapri Cold Chain
Tapri Cold Chain Pvt Ltd, Tapri's refrigerated logistics subsidiary, applies to Narmada Bank for working capital finance. Its projected current assets and current liabilities (other than bank borrowing) are:

| Current assets | ₹ | Current liabilities | ₹ |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Inventory of spares and fuel | 1,80,00,000 | Trade creditors | 90,00,000 |
| Receivables | 2,40,00,000 | Outstanding expenses | 30,00,000 |
| Cash and bank | 20,00,000 | Advances from customers | 10,00,000 |
| Prepaid expenses | 10,00,000 | | |

The bank will grant 75% of net current assets as working capital finance, at 12% a year, on condition that 10% of the loan is kept as a minimum average balance in a current account with it.

**Required:**

(i) Calculate the working capital finance the bank will grant and the margin the company must find.

(ii) State how the margin should be financed.

(iii) Calculate the effective cost of the bank finance, assuming the minimum balance is entirely extra.

(iv) The company's own operating cash of ₹20,00,000 can be kept in this current account. Recalculate the effective cost.

::: answer Show the working
**(i) Bank finance and margin**

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---:|
| Current assets (1,80,00,000 + 2,40,00,000 + 20,00,000 + 10,00,000) | 4,50,00,000 |
| Less: current liabilities other than bank (90,00,000 + 30,00,000 + 10,00,000) | 1,30,00,000 |
| **Net current assets** | **3,20,00,000** |
| Bank finance at 75% | **2,40,00,000** |
| **Margin (25%)** | **80,00,000** |

**(ii) The margin** is the part of working capital the bank will not fund. Being a continuing requirement, it is in effect permanent working capital, which should be financed by long-term sources such as equity or long-term debt (SM 9.102).

**(iii) Minimum balance entirely extra**

Minimum balance = 10% × ₹2,40,00,000 = ₹24,00,000; usable funds = ₹2,16,00,000; interest = ₹2,40,00,000 × 12% = ₹28,80,000.

$$\text{Effective cost} = \frac{28{,}80{,}000}{2{,}16{,}00{,}000} = 13.33\% \qquad \left(= \frac{12\%}{1 - 0.10}\right)$$

**(iv) Own cash counted towards the balance**

The company would keep ₹20,00,000 anyway, so only ₹4,00,000 is extra idle money; usable funds = ₹2,40,00,000 − ₹4,00,000 = ₹2,36,00,000.

$$\text{Effective cost} = \frac{28{,}80{,}000}{2{,}36{,}00{,}000} = 12.20\%$$

**Comment.** A compensating balance raises the cost only to the extent the borrower would not otherwise maintain it. The effect of the extra balance on the assessment itself has been ignored.
:::

:::: example Four needs, four forms of bank credit
Kesar has four requests for Narmada Bank this year:

(a) Indian Railways has invited tenders for kulhad cafés at 30 stations; the tender requires a performance security in the railway's favour, payable if Tapri fails to perform.

(b) Tapri is importing a tea-packing line from Germany; the German supplier will ship only if a bank undertakes to pay against the shipping documents.

(c) The cafés' working capital need swings between ₹2 crore and ₹6 crore over the year; Tapri wants to draw only what it needs, within a limit, and repay as cash comes in.

(d) A supermarket chain buys premix on 60-day bills of exchange, but Tapri wants the money now.

**Required:** Advise the form of bank credit for each request, with reasons.

::: answer Model answer
**(a) Bank guarantee.** A bank guarantee is a facility that commercial banks extend on behalf of their clients in favour of third parties, who are the beneficiaries of the guarantees (SM 9.108). Here the bank would issue the guarantee on Tapri's behalf in favour of Indian Railways, the beneficiary, which can claim on it if Tapri fails to perform; no funds are lent unless the guarantee is invoked.

**(b) Letter of credit.** Under a letter of credit the issuing bank, on the instructions of its customer, undertakes to pay or accept or negotiate, or authorises another bank to do so, against stipulated documents, subject to compliance with specified terms and conditions (SM 9.108). Narmada Bank, on Tapri's instructions, would undertake to pay the German supplier against the shipping documents, giving the supplier the assurance it needs.

**(c) Cash credit.** A cash credit is a credit facility given on a continuous basis, within a sanctioned limit that the borrower is not allowed to exceed (SM 9.107); the borrower draws only to the extent of his requirements and deposits surplus funds, and interest is charged on the amount actually availed (FM Study Material 2.27). A fluctuating need of ₹2 crore to ₹6 crore suits a cash credit limit of about ₹6 crore. (If Tapri wanted only a standby commitment, a **line of credit**, a commitment by the bank to lend up to a specified maximum on demand, would serve.)

**(d) Bills discounting.** Tapri draws a bill on the chain, which accepts it; Tapri then discounts the bill with its banker, which will generally earmark a discounting limit (SM 9.107). Tapri receives the face value less the discount at once, and the bank collects from the chain on maturity.
:::

## Under the hood

### The Tandon methods, in old-scheme answers

Old-scheme papers asked for MPBF "under three methods as per Tandon Committee lending norms" (May 2022 Q1(b), 5 marks; RTP May 2023). ICAI's suggested answers used:

| Method | MPBF |
|---|---|
| I | 75% × (current assets − current liabilities) |
| II | 75% × current assets − current liabilities |
| III | 75% × (current assets − core current assets) − current liabilities |

The current Study Material does not teach these methods; it states that RBI withdrew MPBF in April 1997 (SM 9.106). Learn them only to read older practice questions, and follow the question's own stipulation (as in the Study Material's case scenario, 75% of net current assets). The Study Material's own MCQ asks who coined "core current assets": the **Tandon Committee** (SM 9.112, answer at 9.128).

### Commercial paper in Chapter 2

Chapter 2 adds that RBI introduced the CP scheme in 1989; that primary dealers and all-India financial institutions may also issue CP; that issuers need a credit rating from CRISIL, ICRA, CARE, FITCH Ratings India or another agency specified by RBI; and that the interest rate is generally linked to the yield on the one-year government bond (FM Study Material 2.25). Old-scheme MTPs asked "Explain in brief the features of commercial paper" several times (July 2021, November 2020, November 2022, November 2023).

### Factoring against bills discounting

ICAI's four differences (SM 9.106): factoring is "invoice factoring", bills discounting "invoice discounting"; the parties are client, factor and debtor against drawer, drawee and payee; factoring is management of book debts, bills discounting a borrowing from banks; and no specific Act governs factoring, while the Negotiable Instruments Act applies to bills. The comparison is set out in [[fm.wc.receivables|management of receivables]].

### Exporters' finance

Pre-shipment (packing credit) and post-shipment finance (purchase or discounting of export bills, ECGC guarantee, advances against bills sent for collection, against duty drawback) are bank credit for exporters; they are taught in Chapter 2 and asked as such (May 2026 Q4(b)): [[fm.sources.short|short-term sources of finance]]. A letter of credit also shortens the funds blocked in export debtors: the Parshvam case of RTP May 2025 Q9 took export receivables at one month instead of three, because the financial institution paid the export value within a month against the LC, for a fee.

::: exam Traps
- **Usable funds, not loan.** Divide interest by the funds the firm can use; a compensating balance counts only if it would not otherwise be kept.
- **Net proceeds, not face value**, for any discounted instrument.
- **MPBF is withdrawn.** A theory answer that presents Tandon's norms as current RBI rules contradicts the Study Material.
- **ICAI's order** of the seven forms: cash credit, bank overdraft, bills discounting, bills acceptance, line of credit, letter of credit, bank guarantee.
:::

::: trick Divide by what you can spend
Any source whose price is deducted up front or whose funds are partly locked away: effective cost = annual cost in rupees ÷ rupees you can actually spend. Condition: compare all sources over the same year basis (365 or 360, as the question says).
:::

## Answer frame

### "Describe the forms of bank credit" (4 marks, any four)

Write each form with ICAI's one-line description, in ICAI's order: cash credit (continuous facility within a sanctioned limit); bank overdraft (short-term, urgent needs, within limits, callable at short notice); bills discounting (seller discounts the accepted bill; discounting limit); bills acceptance (company draws on the bank, which accepts it); line of credit (commitment to lend on demand up to a maximum); letter of credit (bank undertakes to pay against stipulated documents); bank guarantee (on behalf of the client in favour of a third-party beneficiary). Four well-explained forms earn the marks; ICAI's answer also accepted short-term loans.

### "Explain commercial paper: its advantages and limitations"

1. Definition: an unsecured promissory note for a short period; for highly rated corporate borrowers; freely negotiable interest rate.
2. Maturity 7 days to less than 1 year; denominations of ₹5 lakh or multiples.
3. Advantages (a) to (e) in ICAI's order: unsecured, no restrictive conditions; rollover; tailored maturity; available in a tight money market; cheaper than bank loans.
4. Limitations: only highly rated firms; neither redeemable before nor extendable beyond maturity.

### "Assessment of working capital by banks after 1997"

The four points of SM 9.106–9.107 in order: MPBF withdrawn (April 1997); banks' own Board-approved methods within prudential guidelines and exposure norms; RBI's directed credit and prohibition of credit still apply; MPBF instructions withdrawn and lending-discipline guidelines optional.

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: the 11% loan that cost 11%
Quoting the bank loan at its headline rate when 15% of it must lie idle. **Fix:** 11% ÷ 0.85 = 12.94%, and borrow ₹2,35,29,412 to have ₹2 crore to use.
:::

::: mistake Discount divided by the face value
For a bill or CP, dividing the discount by the face value understates the cost (2.20 ÷ 100 × 365 ÷ 91 = 8.82% instead of 9.02%). **Fix:** divide by the net proceeds.
:::

::: mistake MPBF written as today's rule
"Banks lend 75% of the working capital gap as per RBI's Tandon norms." **Fix:** RBI withdrew MPBF in April 1997; banks use their own Board-approved methods. Use a percentage only when the question stipulates it.
:::

::: mistake The CP that was repaid early
Planning to retire commercial paper as soon as cash comes in. **Fix:** CP can neither be redeemed before maturity nor extended beyond it; plan the maturity to the need, with a bank limit as standby.
:::

## Recap

- Permanent working capital from long-term sources (debt, equity); temporary from short-term sources (SM 9.102).
- Spontaneous sources arise naturally (trade credit, bills payable, accrued expenses); negotiated sources are arranged with lenders.
- Choice of source: cost, impact on credit rating, feasibility, reliability, restrictions, matching of maturities.
- Commercial paper: unsecured promissory note, highly rated firms, 7 days to less than 1 year, ₹5 lakh or multiples; cheaper than bank loans; cannot be redeemed early or extended.
- Tandon and Chore Committees set the old norms; RBI withdrew MPBF in April 1997; banks now use Board-approved methods within prudential guidelines and exposure norms.
- Seven forms of bank credit: cash credit, overdraft, bills discounting, bills acceptance, line of credit, letter of credit, bank guarantee.
- Effective cost = annual cost ÷ usable funds: a loan with a minimum balance costs rate ÷ (1 − balance %); a discounted bill or CP costs its charge ÷ net proceeds, annualised. (Four doors, four rents: read the small print.)

## Flashcards

Q:: How should permanent and temporary working capital be financed (ICAI)?
A:: Permanent working capital by long-term sources such as debt and equity; temporary working capital by short-term sources.

Q:: Spontaneous vs negotiated sources of working capital finance?
A:: Spontaneous sources arise naturally in the course of business (trade credit, credit from employees and service suppliers, bills payable, accrued expenses); negotiated sources have to be specifically negotiated with lenders such as banks, financial institutions and the public.

Q:: The six parameters guiding the choice of a working capital source?
A:: Cost factor; impact on credit rating; feasibility; reliability; restrictions; hedging or matching approach (same maturity as the assets).

Q:: Commercial paper: maturity and denomination per the Study Material?
A:: Minimum 7 days to less than 1 year from the date of issue; ₹5 lakh or multiples thereof.

Q:: Commercial paper: its two limitations?
A:: Only highly rated firms can use it; it can neither be redeemed before maturity nor extended beyond maturity.

Q:: When did RBI withdraw MPBF, and what replaced it?
A:: April 1997; banks evolve their own methods of assessing working capital, with Board approval, within prudential guidelines and exposure norms.

Q:: The seven forms of bank credit, in ICAI's order?
A:: Cash credit, bank overdraft, bills discounting, bills acceptance, line of credit, letter of credit, bank guarantee.

Q:: Define a line of credit.
A:: A commitment by a bank to lend a certain amount of funds on demand, specifying the maximum amount.

Q:: Define a bank guarantee.
A:: A facility commercial banks extend on behalf of their clients in favour of third parties, who are the beneficiaries of the guarantees.

Q:: Effective cost of a loan with a compensating balance?
A:: Interest rate ÷ (1 − minimum balance fraction), counting only a balance the firm would not otherwise maintain; e.g. 16.5% ÷ 90% = 18.33%.

Q:: Who introduced the bill rediscounting scheme, and from when?
A:: The Reserve Bank of India, with effect from 1 November 1970; all licensed scheduled banks may offer eligible bills to RBI for rediscount.

Q:: Who coined the term "core current assets", per the Study Material's MCQ?
A:: The Tandon Committee.
