## In plain words

### Kesar wants to say thank you

Tapri has had its best year. Profit after tax is ₹60 crore, and Kesar wants to thank the shareholders before Diwali.

> **Kesar:** Let's pay out everything. Shareholders love a fat dividend.
> **Dev:** And the 120 new cafés? Every rupee we pay out is a rupee we must borrow or raise again, with issue costs.
> **Ritu:** We could give them bonus shares instead. No cash leaves the company.
> **Dev:** Or buy back some shares. Same cash as a dividend, but only those who sell receive it.

Profit after tax can do only two things: go to shareholders as a **dividend**, or stay in the business as **retained earnings** to finance growth. Deciding the split is the **dividend decision**, the third of the finance manager's long-term decisions after the investment and financing decisions.

The money can reach shareholders in different **forms**. A **cash dividend** pays every share the same amount. A **share buy-back** (repurchase) uses the same cash to buy some shares back, so only the shareholders who sell receive cash, and the rest own a larger slice of the company. **Bonus shares** (a stock dividend) hand out new shares instead of cash: reserves become share capital and nothing leaves the company. A **stock split** is not a distribution at all: one share of ₹500 face value becomes five shares of ₹100.

How much to pay depends on the company's position: its investment opportunities, its cost of capital, the shareholders' expectations, the law, taxes and the share price. A café chain opening 120 outlets and earning more on retained money than its shareholders expect is a different animal from a mature company with spare cash and little to invest in.

::: analogy The corporate pie
A share is a slice of the company's pie. A cash dividend cuts a piece off the pie and hands it out: the pie shrinks, the shareholders hold cash. A buy-back hands cash only to those who give back their slices: the remaining slices are each a little bigger share of a smaller pie. Bonus shares and stock splits cut the same pie into more slices; nobody gets more pie (James Porterfield's "division of corporate pie into a larger number of pieces", SM 8.6).
:::

::: key
Dividend is the part of profit after tax distributed to shareholders; the rest is retained for growth (g = b × r). Cash dividends and buy-backs pay out cash, bonus shares only capitalise reserves, and stock splits only change the number of shares. The right payout depends on investment opportunities, cost of capital, shareholders' expectations, legal and tax constraints, and the share price.
:::

## How it works

::: definition Dividend
Dividend is that part of Profit After Tax (PAT) which is distributed to the shareholders of the company. Profit after tax can be used for (i) distribution of dividend, or (ii) retaining as surplus for future growth. (SM 8.3)
:::

**Ex-dividend** refers to shares that no longer carry the right to dividend; the price at which the shares trade immediately on the next day after declaration of dividend is the **ex-dividend price** (SM 8.3). With nothing else changing, it is the cum-dividend price less the dividend.

1. **Cash dividend**: the most common form. Cash means cash, cheque, warrant, demand draft, pay order or directly through Electronic Clearing Service (ECS), but not in kind.
2. **Share repurchases**: a transaction in which the company buys back its own shares using corporate cash. The shares bought back are either **treasury shares**, kept for re-issuance in future, or **cancelled shares**, retired from issued share capital. Share repurchases are also viewed as one form of dividend distribution: keeping other things the same (such as tax considerations), the effect of a cash dividend and of a share repurchase on shareholders' wealth is the same.
3. **Stock dividend (bonus shares)**: a distribution of shares in lieu of cash dividend. New shares issued to existing shareholders without any consideration are bonus shares. They are distributed proportionately, so proportionate ownership is retained; equity share capital increases and reserves and surplus decrease. The total net worth is not affected, as retained earnings are only capitalised.

**Conditions for a bonus issue** (SEBI, SM 8.5):

- Bonus shares are issued not in lieu of cash dividends.
- The issue should be authorised by the Articles of Association and not be declared unless all partly paid-up shares have been converted into fully paid-up shares.
- The company should not have defaulted in repayment of loan, interest and any statutory dues.
- Bonus shares are issued only from share premium and free reserves, not from capital reserve on account of revaluation of fixed assets.

**Advantages of stock dividend** (SM 8.5–8.6)

| To shareholders | To the company |
|---|---|
| No tax on a stock dividend from a domestic company: it is treated as a capital asset, not as dividend | Conserves cash for profitable investment opportunities |
| If the fixed dividend per share continues after the bonus, total cash dividends rise in future | Suitable when cash is short, or lenders restrict cash dividends |
| Liquidity: lower-priced shares let shareholders sell a few and raise cash | |

**Limitations of stock dividend** (SM 8.6): to shareholders, it does not affect their wealth and so has no value for them: it capitalises past earnings they already own, and their proportionate ownership does not change (though it creates a favourable psychological impact, as a sign of growth). To the company, stock dividends are costlier to administer than cash dividends, and periodic small stock dividends are a disadvantage.

The Study Material adds that bonus shares discourage immediate selling, because the holding period of bonus shares starts from their date of issue and short-term capital gains were taxed more heavily than long-term ones (it quotes 15% and 10%), which supports the share price (SM 8.5).

### Stock splits and share buy-backs (SM 8.33–8.34)

A **stock split** means splitting one share into many, say one share of ₹500 into 5 shares of ₹100. It is a tool to regulate share prices: a price that rises from ₹50 to ₹1,000 over the years may go out of the range of many investors. **Advantages**: it makes the share affordable to small investors; more shares may mean more shareholders, so the potential of investment may increase. **Limitations**: additional expenditure on the process; a low share price may attract speculators or short-term investors, generally not preferred by any company.

A **share buy-back** means buying or repurchasing own shares by the company, resulting in a decrease in share capital: the shares bought back are cancelled, reducing the number of shares outstanding. It is also a form of shareholders' dividend: as the number of shares in the market falls, the dividend per share in future increases. The two main routes:

- **Open market**: the company buys through the secondary market.
- **Tender offer**: the company offers a fixed price at which all shareholders can participate or sell their shares.

| Point | Bonus issue | Stock split |
|---|---|---|
| What changes | More shares, same face value | More shares, lower face value |
| Share capital and reserves | Reserves capitalised into share capital | Unchanged in total |
| A distribution of profits? | Yes, a form of dividend | No |
| Main purpose | Reward shareholders, conserve cash | Keep the price in a tradable range |

### Why dividend policy matters (SM 8.6–8.7)

Dividend policy is governed by two decisions:

1. **Long-term financing decision.** Equity can be raised externally (new shares) or internally (retained earnings), and retained earnings are preferable because they involve no flotation costs. Paying cash dividends reduces the funds for profitable investments. The decision turns on whether the organisation has opportunities to invest the profit if retained, and whether the return on such investment (ROI) will be higher than shareholders' expectation, Ke.
2. **Wealth maximisation decision**: what dividend payout ratio maximises the market price of the share? Because of market imperfections and uncertainty, shareholders give more importance to near dividends than to future dividends and capital gains; higher dividends tend to increase the value of shares. But retained earnings invested profitably raise future earnings per share. Management must divide net earnings between dividends and retained earnings in an optimum way.

### Retained earnings and growth (SM 8.8)

$$g = b \times r$$

g = growth rate of the firm, b = retention ratio, r = rate of return on investment. In the Study Material's Example 1, two companies each earn 20% on ₹50,00,000: A Ltd distributes all its ₹10,00,000 and earns ₹10,00,000 again next year; B Ltd retains ₹5,00,000, employs ₹55,00,000 and earns ₹11,00,000, 10% more (b = 0.5, r = 20%). If Tapri retains 60% of its earnings and earns 18% on them, its earnings grow at 0.6 × 18% = 10.8% a year. How the price reacts is the subject of [[fm.div.models|Walter's and Gordon's models]].

### Determinants of dividend decisions (SM 8.9–8.10)

ICAI's list, in ICAI's order:

1. **Availability of funds**: if the business needs funds, retained earnings are a good source, saving flotation cost and preventing the dilution of control that a public issue of equity shares causes.
2. **Cost of capital**: if financing is through debt (relatively cheaper), it is preferable to distribute more dividend; if through a fresh issue of equity shares, better to use retained earnings as much as possible.
3. **Capital structure**: an optimum debt-equity ratio should be considered.
4. **Stock price**: generally, higher dividends increase the market value of shares and low dividends decrease it.
5. **Investment opportunities in hand**: with opportunities in hand, the company may prefer to retain more earnings.
6. **Trend of industry**: investors depend on some industries for regular dividend income, so firms there must pay dividends to survive in the market.
7. **Expectation of shareholders**: some invest for regular income, others for growth; generally investors prefer current dividend over future growth.
8. **Legal constraints**: under Section 123 of the Companies Act, 2013, dividend shall be declared or paid only (a) out of the profits of the year arrived at after providing for depreciation, (b) out of undistributed profits of previous years after providing for depreciation, (c) out of both, or (d) out of money provided by the Central or a State Government in pursuance of its guarantee. Unrealised or notional gains, revaluation of assets and fair value changes are excluded in computing profits for dividends.
9. **Taxation**: before 1 April 2020, the company paid dividend distribution tax (Section 115-O) and the dividend was exempt for the shareholder (Section 10(34)); after the Finance Act 2020, dividend income from shares held as investment is taxable in the hands of the investor at the applicable slab rate.

### Practical considerations (SM 8.10–8.13)

The policy depends on two questions: should there be a stable pattern of dividends over the years, or should each dividend decision be treated independently?

**(a) Financial needs of the company.** When the company's rate of return exceeds the return shareholders require, retaining earnings benefits shareholders; raising new shares brings flotation costs.

| Mature companies | Growth companies |
|---|---|
| Few investment opportunities: high payout ratios | Need funds for fast-growing fixed assets: low payout ratios |
| Share prices sensitive to any change in payout | Retain earnings and declare bonus shares to offset shareholders' dividend expectations |
| Keep a small portion of earnings for emergent and occasional needs | Increase dividends gradually as profitable opportunities start falling |

**(b) Constraints on paying dividends**: legal (the determinants above); liquidity (dividends are cash outflows; a growth company with good profits may still lack cash); access to the capital market (large dividends may force new issues that dilute control if existing shareholders cannot subscribe); and investment opportunities (if inadequate, pay dividends and raise external funds when needed).

**(c) Payout policies** can be maintained by fixing the amount or rate of dividend irrespective of the earnings of the company (SM 8.12–8.13). The Study Material gives two:

1. **Constant dividend policy**: shareholders are given a fixed amount of dividend irrespective of actual earnings, generally maintained for a considerable period. To keep paying it in poor years, the company creates a reserve such as a **Dividend Equalisation Reserve Fund**, earmarked by marketable securities. The policy treats equity shareholders at par with preference shareholders and is preferred by persons and institutions that depend on dividend income.
2. **Stable dividend policy**: the Study Material describes two forms under this heading.
   - A **constant payout ratio**: a fixed percentage of net earnings every year, so the dividend moves in direct proportion to earnings. At a 40% payout, EPS of ₹2 gives 80 paise and EPS of ₹1.50 gives 60 paise; for losses, no dividend. It has a conservative approach and provides a guarantee against over- or under-payment. Infosys's 2011 policy (up to 30% of consolidated PAT) is the Study Material's example.
   - Dividends based on a **long-term forecast of sustainable earnings**, increased when earnings have risen to a substantially higher level: the target payout ratio of Lintner's model.

### Lintner's model: dividends that follow earnings slowly (SM 8.13–8.14)

John Lintner built his model from interviews with corporate managers in the mid-1950s. It has **two parameters**: (i) the **target payout ratio**, and (ii) the **spread at which current dividends adjust to the target**. Its assumptions:

1. Firms have a long-term dividend payout ratio; mature companies with stable earnings may have high payouts, growth companies low ones.
2. Managers are more concerned with changes in dividends than with the absolute amounts.
3. Dividend changes follow changes in long-run sustainable earnings.
4. Managers are reluctant to effect dividend changes that may have to be reversed.

So this year's dividend depends on this year's earnings and last year's dividend:

$$D_1 = D_0 + [(\text{EPS} \times \text{Target payout}) - D_0] \times A_f$$

D₁ = dividend in year 1, D₀ = last year's dividend, EPS = earnings per share, Af = adjustment factor or speed of adjustment. Its limitations (SM 8.41, MCQ 7): it does not offer a market price for the shares, and the adjustment factor is an arbitrary number, not based on any scientific criterion or method.

## Formats and formulas

*Reproduce these from memory.*

::: formula Payout and retention
$$\text{Payout ratio} = \frac{\text{DPS}}{\text{EPS}} \qquad b = 1 - \text{Payout ratio}$$
DPS = dividend per share; b = retention ratio.
:::

::: formula Growth from retained earnings
$$g = b \times r$$
b = retention ratio, r = rate of return on investment (SM 8.8).
:::

::: formula Lintner's model
$$D_1 = D_0 + [(\text{EPS} \times \text{Target payout}) - D_0] \times A_f$$
Af = adjustment factor (speed of adjustment), between 0 and 1 (SM 8.14).
:::

::: formula Share buy-back
$$\text{Shares bought back} = \frac{\text{Amount}}{\text{Buy-back price}} \qquad \text{BVPS after} = \frac{\text{Book value before} - \text{Amount paid}}{\text{Shares remaining}}$$
Cash paid out reduces net worth; with earnings unchanged, EPS after = earnings ÷ shares remaining (SM 8.54, problem 9).
:::

**Ex-dividend price** = cum-dividend price − dividend per share (SM 8.4; 8.54, problem 8).

::: definition Stock split
Stock split means splitting one share into many, say, one share of ₹500 into 5 shares of ₹100. (SM 8.33)
:::

::: definition Share buyback
Share buyback, in simple terms, means buying/repurchasing own shares by the company resulting into decrease in share capital of the company. Thus, the shares bought back are cancelled leading reduction in outstanding number of shares. (SM 8.33)
:::

## Worked examples

::::: example Thirty crore rupees, three ways to hand them out
Tapri Tea Co. Ltd has 1 crore equity shares quoted at ₹300 (cum-dividend) and ₹30 crore of cash to return to shareholders. The board considers three alternatives:

- Option 1: a cash dividend of ₹30 per share.
- Option 2: buying back ₹30 crore of shares at the market price, every shareholder tendering in proportion.
- Option 3: no cash, but a bonus issue of 1 share for every 4 held.

**Required:** Show the wealth of Ritu, who holds 100 shares, immediately after each option, ignoring taxes and transaction costs. Comment.

:::: answer Show the working
**Option 1: cash dividend.** Ex-dividend price = ₹300 − ₹30 = ₹270.

**Option 2: buy-back.** Shares bought back = ₹30 crore ÷ ₹300 = 10 lakh shares, 10% of the shares.

| Particulars | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Market capitalisation before buy-back | ₹300 crore |
| Less: Shares repurchased | ₹30 crore |
| Market capitalisation after buy-back | ₹270 crore |
| Shares left (1 crore − 10 lakh) | 90 lakh |
| **Market price after buy-back** | **₹300** |

**Option 3: bonus 1:4.** Shares rise to 1.25 crore; net worth is not affected, so the theoretical price is ₹300 crore ÷ 1.25 crore = ₹240.

| Cash dividend | 3,000 | 100 | 27,000 | 30,000 |
| Buy-back | 3,000 | 90 | 27,000 | 30,000 |
| Bonus 1:4 | nil | 125 | 30,000 | 30,000 |

:::: example Tapri's buy-back: tender offer or open market?
Tapri's board has earmarked ₹96 crore for a share buy-back. Its earnings are ₹60 crore, its EPS ₹30, its P/E ratio 16 and its book value per share ₹240. Dev offers two routes:

- Route A: a tender offer at a premium of 25% to the current market price.
- Route B: open-market purchases at the current market price.

Assume the ₹96 crore would otherwise have earned nothing, so total earnings stay at ₹60 crore.

**Required:**

(i) Calculate the number of shares outstanding, the market price and the number of shares bought back under each route.

(ii) Calculate the EPS and the book value per share after the buy-back under each route.

(iii) At what buy-back price would the book value per share not change?

(iv) Comment on the results.

::: answer Show the working
**(i) Shares and prices**

1. Shares outstanding = ₹60 crore ÷ ₹30 = **2 crore**.
2. Market price = EPS × P/E = ₹30 × 16 = **₹480**; tender price = ₹480 × 1.25 = **₹600**.
3. Shares bought back: Route A = ₹96 crore ÷ ₹600 = **16 lakh**; Route B = ₹96 crore ÷ ₹480 = **20 lakh**.

**(ii) After the buy-back**

| Particulars | Route A (₹600) | Route B (₹480) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Book value before (2 crore × ₹240) | ₹480 crore | ₹480 crore |
| Less: Amount paid | ₹96 crore | ₹96 crore |
| Book value after | ₹384 crore | ₹384 crore |
| Shares after | 1.84 crore | 1.80 crore |
| **Book value per share** | **₹208.70** | **₹213.33** |
| **EPS (₹60 crore ÷ shares after)** | **₹32.61** | **₹33.33** |

**(iii) Price that leaves book value per share unchanged.** At the book value itself, ₹240: ₹96 crore buys 40 lakh shares, and ₹384 crore ÷ 1.60 crore shares = ₹240. Any price above book value lowers the book value per share; any price below it raises it.

**(iv) Comment.** Both routes pay more than the book value of ₹240, so book value per share falls, while EPS rises because the same earnings are shared by fewer shares. The 25% premium of the tender offer retires 4 lakh fewer shares for the same ₹96 crore, so its EPS gain is smaller (₹32.61 against ₹33.33). In return, a tender offer lets every shareholder sell at a fixed price, while open-market purchases go through the secondary market (SM 8.34). With fewer shares in circulation, the dividend per share in future can rise.

:::: example Ritu smooths the dividend: Lintner's model
Tapri paid a dividend of ₹8 per share last year. This year's EPS is ₹30. The board's target payout ratio is 40% and its speed of adjustment 0.5.

**Required:**

(i) Calculate this year's dividend per share using Lintner's model.

(ii) If EPS stays at ₹30 next year, what will next year's dividend be?

(iii) What speed of adjustment would have given a dividend of ₹11 this year?

::: answer Show the working
**(i)** Target dividend = ₹30 × 40% = ₹12.

$$D_1 = 8 + (12 - 8) \times 0.5 = 8 + 2 = \text{₹}10$$

**(ii)** D₂ = 10 + (12 − 10) × 0.5 = **₹11**. The dividend closes half the remaining gap each year: ₹8, ₹10, ₹11, ₹11.50 …

**(iii)** 11 = 8 + (12 − 8) × Af, so Af = 3 ÷ 4 = **0.75**. The higher the speed of adjustment, the faster the dividend moves to the target; at Af = 1 it would jump to ₹12 at once, and at Af = 0 it would never move.
:::

:::: example Case scenario: Kesar's Diwali dividend
Tapri's board is setting the dividend for 2025-26. Profit after tax is ₹60 crore. The company plans 120 new cafés next year, needing ₹180 crore, and expects to earn 20% on them against the 14% its shareholders expect. A bank term-loan covenant limits cash dividends to 25% of profit after tax. Many retail shareholders, including Tapri's own staff, depend on a regular dividend, and the share price has risen to ₹2,400, beyond the reach of many small investors. Kesar proposes a cash dividend of 60% of profit "to keep everyone happy".

**Required:** Advise the board, with reasons, on Kesar's proposal and on whether bonus shares or a stock split could help.

::: answer Model answer
**Relevant considerations.** The dividend decision is governed by the long-term financing decision (retained earnings are preferable as they involve no flotation cost, but only if the company has opportunities to invest them at a return above the shareholders' expectation, Ke) and the wealth maximisation decision. The determinants of dividend decisions include availability of funds, cost of capital, capital structure, stock price, investment opportunities in hand, trend of industry, expectation of shareholders, legal constraints and taxation (SM 8.6–8.10).

**Facts of the case.** Tapri needs ₹180 crore for expansion and expects a return of 20% against a Ke of 14%; a lender's covenant restricts cash dividends to 25% of profit after tax (₹15 crore); its shareholders expect regular income; its share price is high.

**Analysis.**

1. *Investment opportunities and availability of funds*: with profitable opportunities in hand (r > Ke), the company should retain more of its earnings; retention avoids the flotation cost and dilution of control of a fresh equity issue.
2. *Legal and contractual constraint*: a 60% payout (₹36 crore) would breach the covenant limit of ₹15 crore.
3. *Expectation of shareholders*: retail shareholders depending on dividend income argue for a regular, stable cash dividend, within what the company can afford.
4. *Stock dividend*: bonus shares conserve cash for the expansion and are suitable when lenders restrict cash dividends; they improve liquidity for shareholders, and if the dividend per share is maintained on the larger number of shares, future cash dividends increase.
5. *Stock price*: a stock split would bring the price within the reach of small investors and may increase the number of shareholders.

**Conclusion.** Kesar's proposal should not be accepted. The board should declare a regular cash dividend within the covenant limit (not exceeding ₹15 crore), retain the balance to finance the expansion, and may consider a bonus issue (subject to SEBI's conditions) or a stock split to reward shareholders and improve the marketability of the shares without parting with cash.
:::

## Under the hood

### Three names, two policies

The Study Material's text on payout policies needs a careful reading. Under "Constant Dividend Policy" it describes a fixed amount per share (with a dividend equalisation reserve); under "Stable Dividend Policy" it describes a constant payout ratio (Infosys, the 40% example, Buffett's remark that a regular 10% or 20% payout has no logic), then closes with a stable dividend based on long-term sustainable earnings, which leads to Lintner's target payout. One line under the constant dividend policy says such dividends "fluctuate with earnings in short term", which does not fit a fixed amount. In an answer, describe each policy by what it fixes: the amount per share, the payout ratio, or a target approached gradually.

### Buy-backs and book value

A buy-back above book value lowers book value per share; below book value, it raises it. In the Study Material's problem 9, DG Ltd (book value ₹400 a share) buys back 25,000 of its 1 lakh shares at ₹500 (EPS 25 × P/E 20): book value falls from ₹4 crore to ₹2.75 crore, or ₹367 per share (SM 8.54).

::: exam The traps ICAI used
- "Options other than cash used for distributing profits": the Study Material's answer is **bonus shares**, not "both bonus shares and stock split" (SM 8.41, MCQ 8; answer 8.47). A split distributes nothing.
- Lintner in reverse (January 2026 MCQ 8): 50 = D₀ + (80 − D₀) × 0.25, so 0.75 D₀ = 30 and D₀ = ₹40.
- Buy-backs at a premium: the price paid, not the market price, reduces book value (MTP Series II for September 2024).
- Reinvesting a dividend: buy at the ex-dividend price (SM problem 8: ₹50 − ₹10 = ₹40, so ₹10,00,000 buys 25,000 shares).
:::

::: trick Lintner closes a fixed share of the gap
Each year the dividend moves Af of the way from last year's dividend to the target (EPS × target payout). With Af = 0.5 it halves the gap; reversed, D₀ = (D₁ − Af × target) ÷ (1 − Af). Condition: Af between 0 and 1.
:::

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: the stock split that "distributed profits"
Bunty lists bonus shares *and* stock splits as ways of distributing profits. **Fix:** bonus shares capitalise reserves and are a form of dividend; a stock split only divides each share into more shares of lower face value.
:::

::: mistake Using the market price instead of the buy-back price
Book value after = book value before − amount *paid*, at the buy-back price including any premium; and the number of shares retired is the amount ÷ the buy-back price. **Fix:** in Tapri's tender offer, ₹96 crore buys 16 lakh shares at ₹600, not 20 lakh at the market price of ₹480.
:::

::: mistake Lintner without the brake
Taking the target dividend (EPS × target payout) as this year's dividend. **Fix:** only the fraction Af of the gap is closed: ₹8 + (₹12 − ₹8) × 0.5 = ₹10, not ₹12.
:::

::: mistake Payout in place of retention in g = br
A company paying out 40% and earning 15% grows at 0.6 × 15% = 9%, not 0.4 × 15% = 6%. **Fix:** b is the retention ratio, 1 − payout.
:::

## Practice

::: quiz Check yourself
Q: Chai Express Ltd has 50 lakh shares with a book value of ₹120 each. It buys back 10 lakh shares at ₹180 each. What is its book value per share after the buy-back (₹)?
= 105
> Book value = ₹60 crore − (10 lakh × ₹180 = ₹18 crore) = ₹42 crore, over 40 lakh shares: ₹105. Buying above book value lowers it.

Q: A company paid ₹6 per share last year. EPS this year is ₹20, the target payout ratio 50% and the adjustment factor 0.4. What is this year's dividend under Lintner's model (₹)?
= 7.6
> D₁ = 6 + (20 × 50% − 6) × 0.4 = 6 + 1.6 = ₹7.60.

Q: Case scenario. Tapri's share price has climbed to ₹2,400. The board wants more small investors to be able to buy its shares, without paying out cash and without capitalising reserves. Kesar asks Dev which tool fits. Which of the following is correct?
- [ ] A bonus issue of 1 for 1
- [x] A stock split, say one ₹10 share into five ₹2 shares
- [ ] A buy-back through a tender offer
- [ ] A cash dividend of ₹100 per share
> A split makes the share affordable to small investors and changes only the face value and number of shares. A bonus issue capitalises reserves; a buy-back and a cash dividend pay out cash.

Q: Which of these are determinants of dividend decisions in ICAI's list? Select all that apply.
- [x] Availability of funds and investment opportunities in hand
- [x] Trend of industry and expectation of shareholders
- [ ] The face value of the shares
- [x] Legal constraints under Section 123 of the Companies Act, 2013
> The nine determinants: availability of funds, cost of capital, capital structure, stock price, investment opportunities, trend of industry, expectation of shareholders, legal constraints and taxation (SM 8.9–8.10).

Q: Kulhad Kraft Ltd, a packaging supplier, earns ₹40 per share and pays a dividend of ₹14 per share. It earns 16% on the funds it retains. What growth rate does g = br give?
- [ ] 5.6%
- [x] 10.4%
- [ ] 16%
- [ ] 35%
> Payout = 14 ÷ 40 = 35%, so b = 0.65 and g = 0.65 × 16% = 10.4%. 5.6% uses the payout ratio in place of the retention ratio; 35% is the payout ratio itself.

## Recap

- Dividend: the part of profit after tax distributed to shareholders; the rest is retained for growth, g = b × r.
- Forms: cash dividend; share repurchase (treasury or cancelled shares), same effect on wealth as a cash dividend; stock dividend (bonus shares), which capitalises reserves and leaves net worth unchanged.
- Bonus conditions (SEBI): not in lieu of cash dividend; authorised by AOA, all shares fully paid; no default on loans, interest or statutory dues; only from share premium and free reserves.
- Stock split: one share into many of lower face value; makes shares affordable; costs money and may attract speculators. Not a distribution of profits.
- Buy-back: open market or tender offer; reduces shares outstanding; BVPS after = (book value − amount paid) ÷ shares remaining.
- Determinants: funds, cost of capital, capital structure, stock price, investment opportunities, industry trend, shareholders' expectations, legal constraints, taxation.
- Payout policies: constant dividend (fixed amount, dividend equalisation reserve); stable dividend (constant payout ratio, or dividends based on long-term sustainable earnings). Lintner: D₁ = D₀ + [(EPS × target payout) − D₀] × Af, two parameters.
- (A bonus issue cuts the pie into more slices: nobody gets more pie.)

## Flashcards

Q:: ICAI's meaning of dividend?
A:: That part of profit after tax which is distributed to the shareholders of the company; the rest is retained as surplus for future growth.

Q:: What is the ex-dividend price?
A:: The price at which shares trade immediately on the next day after declaration of dividend, when they no longer carry the right to the dividend.

Q:: Three forms of dividend (ICAI)?
A:: Cash dividend; share repurchases (treasury or cancelled shares); stock dividend (bonus shares).

Q:: SEBI conditions for a bonus issue (four)?
A:: Not in lieu of cash dividend; authorised by the AOA and all partly paid shares made fully paid; no default in repaying loans, interest or statutory dues; issued only from share premium and free reserves, not from revaluation reserve.

Q:: Two advantages of a stock dividend to the company?
A:: Conserves cash for profitable investment opportunities; suitable in case of cash deficiency and restrictions imposed by lenders on cash dividends.

Q:: Stock split: meaning, two advantages, two limitations?
A:: One share split into many (₹500 into 5 × ₹100). Advantages: affordable to small investors; more shareholders. Limitations: additional expenditure; low price may attract speculators or short-term investors.

Q:: Two routes for a share buy-back?
A:: Open market (through the secondary market) and tender offer (a fixed price at which all shareholders can sell).

Q:: Formula linking retention and growth?
A:: g = b × r, where b = retention ratio and r = rate of return on investment.

Q:: The nine determinants of dividend decisions, in ICAI's order?
A:: Availability of funds, cost of capital, capital structure, stock price, investment opportunities in hand, trend of industry, expectation of shareholders, legal constraints, taxation.

Q:: Lintner's model: formula and its two parameters?
A:: D₁ = D₀ + [(EPS × target payout) − D₀] × Af; parameters: the target payout ratio and the speed at which current dividends adjust to the target.

Q:: Two limitations of Lintner's model?
A:: It does not offer a market price for the shares; the adjustment factor is arbitrary, not based on any scientific criterion or method.

Q:: Mature versus growth companies: payout ratios?
A:: Mature companies with few investment opportunities show high payout ratios; growth companies, needing funds for expansion, show low payout ratios.
