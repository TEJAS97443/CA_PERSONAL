## In plain words

### The distributors want 90 days

BrewBucks has launched an instant coffee and is giving distributors 60 days to pay. Tapri's premix distributors now want 90.

> **Kesar:** Give them 90 days. More credit, more sales. Simple.
> **Dev:** More sales, yes. Also more money stuck with distributors, more bad debts, more phone calls to chase them.
> **Kesar:** So how many days is right?
> **Dev:** The number of days where the extra profit stops paying for the extra cost. I have a table for that. Two, actually. Even my optimism is discounted at 12%.

Selling on credit creates **receivables** (debtors): money customers owe the firm. **Management of receivables** is planning and controlling that debt, also called trade credit management. Credit brings sales, but every rupee waiting with a customer is a rupee the firm has to finance, some customers never pay (**bad debts**), and chasing them costs money. Too little credit, on the other hand, and competitors take the customers.

So the finance manager decides three things: the **credit policy** (to whom, for how long, with what discount for early payment), the **credit analysis** (how risky this particular customer is) and the **control** of receivables (following up, collecting). To compare policies, Dev writes down for each one the profit it earns, less the cost of the money locked up in debtors; the policy with the biggest net figure wins.

When cash is short, the debts themselves can be turned into cash. Tapri can borrow against them (**pledging**), sell them to a specialist who collects them for a fee (**factoring**), or, for export bills, sell the importer's bills to a bank that takes the whole risk of non-payment (**forfaiting**).

::: analogy The kirana shop's udhaar diary
The corner shop keeps a diary of who owes what. The shopkeeper decides whose name goes in the diary (credit analysis), how long they may run up a bill and whether a quick payer gets a few rupees off (credit policy), and when to send the boy round with a reminder (control). If he needs cash for Diwali stock, he can borrow against the diary, or hand it to a recovery agent who pays him most of it now and collects the rest himself for a cut. That agent is a factor.
:::

::: key
Credit is worth giving only up to the point where the extra contribution from extra sales exceeds the extra bad debts, cash discount, collection cost and the opportunity cost of the money locked in receivables. Factoring and forfaiting turn receivables into cash: factoring is worth it when its effective cost is below the firm's cost of borrowing.
:::

## How it works

### Meaning and the three aspects

The basic objective of management of receivables is ==to optimise the return on investment on these assets== (SM 9.70). Large receivables bring bad debts and collection costs; small ones may restrict sales when competitors offer more liberal terms. There are basically three aspects (SM 9.70–9.71):

1. **Credit policy**: a balanced policy covering credit standards, credit terms and collection efforts. It involves a trade-off between the profits on additional sales from extending credit and the cost of carrying those debtors and bad debt losses; it decides the credit period, cash discount and other matters. The credit period is stated in net days: "net 50" means customers repay not later than 50 days. The cash discount policy specifies (a) the rate of cash discount, (b) the cash discount period and (c) the net credit period: "3/15 net 60" means a 3% discount if the customer pays within 15 days; otherwise payment within 60 days.
2. **Credit analysis**: how risky it is to advance credit to a particular party: due diligence or reputation check of the customer's creditworthiness.
3. **Control of receivables**: following up debtors and deciding a suitable collection policy; both laying down credit policies and executing them.

**The cost of maintaining receivables** (SM 9.71): (i) the cost of additional funds blocked in receivables, as interest (loan funds) or opportunity cost (own funds); (ii) administrative costs: record keeping, investigation of creditworthiness; (iii) collection costs; (iv) defaulting costs.

### What decides the size of receivables

The credit policy determines both the quantity and quality of receivables. The factors that determine the size of the investment (SM 9.71–9.72): (i) the effect of credit on the volume of sales; (ii) credit terms; (iii) cash discount; (iv) policies and practices for selecting credit customers; (v) paying practices and habits of the customers; (vi) the firm's policy and practice of collection; (vii) the degree of operating efficiency in billing, record keeping and adjustment. Rising interest, collection and bad debt costs depress the size of the investment.

A **lenient** policy sells on credit on very liberal terms and standards; a **stringent** policy sells on credit only to customers of proper creditworthiness who are financially sound. More credit means more sales but also more financing, investigation, collection effort and bad debts; less credit may mean lost sales to competitors (SM 9.72).

The **finance manager's** involvement (SM 9.72): (a) supervising the administration of credit; (b) contributing to top management decisions on the best credit policies; (c) deciding the criteria for selecting credit applications; (d) speeding up the conversion of receivables into cash by an aggressive collection policy. In summary, a balance between the cost of increased investment in receivables and the profits from higher sales.

### Evaluating credit policies: total and incremental

ICAI gives two methods, the **total approach** and the **incremental approach**, with a format for each (SM 9.73–9.75; reproduced under Formats and formulas):

- **Total approach**: for every policy, expected profit (credit sales − variable costs − fixed costs − bad debts − cash discount, less tax) minus the **opportunity cost of the investment in receivables**. The policy with the highest net benefit is adopted.
- **Incremental approach**: for every proposed policy, the incremental profit over the present policy minus the required return on the incremental investment in receivables.
- A third presentation in Illustration 12: the **expected rate of return** = incremental expected profit ÷ incremental investment in receivables, compared with the required rate (SM 9.78–9.79).

Both approaches give the same ranking. The two working formulas (SM 9.74):

$$\text{Total fixed cost} = (\text{Average cost per unit} - \text{Variable cost per unit}) \qquad \times \text{Units sold on credit under present policy}$$

$$\text{Opportunity cost} = \text{Total cost of credit sales} \qquad \times \frac{\text{Collection period (days)}}{365 \text{ or } 360} \qquad \times \frac{\text{Required rate of return}}{100}$$

::: note Which cost to lock up
ICAI's format uses the **total cost** of credit sales (variable + fixed) for the investment in receivables (Illustration 12, SM 9.77; in Illustration 13 no fixed cost is given, so the variable cost is the whole cost, SM 9.80). When only the contribution ratio is given, ICAI uses the **variable cost**, "only relevant or variable costs are considered" (Illustration 14, SM 9.81). Illustration 16 values the new debtors at new variable cost plus fixed cost at the existing level (SM 9.97). State the basis you use.

### One customer: grant credit or not?

Granting credit to a customer is a cost-benefit decision: if the customer pays, the firm earns its profit; if not, the whole cost of the goods is lost. ### Financing receivables: pledging and factoring

Pledging and factoring have emerged as important sources of financing receivables (SM 9.82):

- **Pledging** is the use of a firm's receivables to secure a short-term loan. After cash, receivables are the most liquid assets and serve as prime collateral. The lender scrutinises them, selects acceptable accounts, creates a lien and fixes the percentage financed, around 50 to 90%. Advantages: ease and flexibility, regular financing. Drawbacks: high cost; being a loan, it raises debt and affects the debt-equity ratio.
- **Factoring** is the outright sale of receivables to a factor, a firm that acquires the receivables of other firms. The factoring agreement lays down the conditions; the factoring agency bears the risk of collection and services the accounts for a fee.

Factoring may be **with recourse** (if the factor cannot collect, it turns the receivable back to the firm, generally to be replaced by new receivables) or **non-recourse** (the factor bears the ultimate risk of loss, and so charges a higher commission) (SM 9.83). Its biggest advantages are the immediate conversion of receivables into cash and a predicted pattern of cash flows. It gives liquidity without creating a net liability, so there is no impact on the debt-equity ratio; it is not considered a loan, involves no debt repayment, and provides timely funds, efficient record keeping and effective management of collection (SM 9.83).

**Advise** (SM 9.84): avail factoring if the rate of effective cost of factoring is less than the existing cost of borrowing, or if it gives positive net annual benefits; do not avail it if the effective cost is more than the cost of borrowing.

### Forfaiting

"Forfait" is a French term meaning "relinquish a right". **Forfaiting** is an arrangement of bill discounting in which a financial institution or bank buys the trade bills or receivables from exporters, the exporter relinquishing the right to receive payment from the importer. The bank provides immediate finance on a **without recourse** basis, so the risks and rewards pass to it. The overseas buyer can open a letter of credit (or other negotiable instrument) in favour of the exporter and import on deferred payment terms (SM 9.86).

**Features** (SM 9.87): it motivates exporters to explore new geographies as payment is assured; the importer can import on deferred payment terms; the exporter enjoys reduced transaction costs and complexities; the exporter can compete internationally and keep its working capital at work to scale up; importers avail of it from international institutions to finance imports at competitive rates. **Example**: Exim Bank of India's **Buyer's Credit** gives credit to overseas buyers to import from India, providing non-recourse finance to Indian exporters by converting a deferred credit contract into a cash contract (SM 9.87–9.88).

### Innovations in receivable management (SM 9.89–9.94)

1. **Re-engineering the receivable process**: (a) centralisation of high-volume receivable and payable transactions; (b) alternative payment strategies: direct debit, Integrated Voice Response (payment over the phone), collection by a third party, lock box processing (an outsourced partner captures cheques and invoice data), internet payments (RTGS, NEFT, UPI, app-based); (c) customer orientation for strategically important customers.
2. **Evaluation of risk**: assess risks, then introduce controls to contain or eliminate them.
3. **Use of latest technology**: e-commerce (EDI, e-mail, EFT, electronic catalogues) and automated accounts receivable management systems.
4. **Receivable collection practices**: issue of invoice; open account or open-end credit; credit terms or time limits; periodic statements and follow-ups; payment incentives and penalties; record keeping and continuous audit; export factoring; business process outsourcing.
5. **Financial tools and techniques**: credit analysis and credit rating (sources: trade references, bank references, credit bureau reports, past experience, published financial statements, salesman's interview and reports; then a credit limit, raised as experience is gained); the credit-granting decision tree; control of receivables; collection policy.

The **collection policy** trades collection expense against lower bad debts and investment in debtors; it must answer how long a balance may run before collection starts, how and how often reminders go, whether personal calls are made, and how doubtful accounts are handled (legal action or an escalation matrix) (SM 9.94). ### Monitoring receivables (SM 9.94–9.96)

1. **Average age of receivables**: the average collection period.
2. **Ageing schedule**: receivables classified by age, for closer control over the quality of individual accounts, compared with earlier schedules of the same firm and with other firms; a tendency for old accounts to accumulate is a warning.
3. **Debt collection programme**: (a) monitoring the state of receivables; (b) intimation to customers when the due date approaches; (c) e-mail and telephonic advice on the due date; (d) reminding the legal recourse on overdue accounts and following an escalation matrix; (e) legal action on overdue accounts.

**Statement showing the evaluation of a factoring proposal** (SM 9.83–9.84)

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---:|
| **A. Annual savings (benefit) on taking factoring service** | |
| Cost of credit administration saved | … |
| Bad debts avoided | … |
| Interest saved due to reduction in average collection period: cost of annual credit sales × rate of interest × (present − new collection period) ÷ 360 | … |
| **B. Annual cost of factoring to the firm** | |
| Factoring commission: annual credit sales × % of commission | … |
| Interest charged by factor on advance: amount available for advance × collection period ÷ 360 × rate of interest (annualised) | … |
| **C. Net annual benefits / cost of factoring (A − B)** | … |

::: formula Effective cost of factoring (SM 9.84)
$$\text{Rate of effective cost} = \frac{\text{Net annual cost of factoring}}{\text{Advances to be paid}} \times 100 \qquad \text{or} \quad \frac{\text{Net annual cost}}{\text{Amount available for advance}} \times 100$$
Amount available for advance = receivables − factoring commission − factoring reserve; advances to be paid = amount available for advance − interest deducted by the factor. A year is taken as 360 days.
:::

::: formula Cash discount and average collection period
$$\text{Cash discount} = \text{Credit sales} \qquad \times \text{Share of customers taking the discount} \qquad \times \text{Rate}$$
Average collection period = the sum, over the groups of customers, of each group's share of credit sales × the days that group takes to pay. From a receivables turnover ratio: collection period in months = 12 ÷ turnover (Illustration 13, SM 9.80).
:::

::: formula Expected rate of return (Illustration 12)
$$\text{Expected rate of return} = \frac{\text{Incremental expected profit}}{\text{Incremental investment}} \times 100$$
:::

::: definition Factoring (SM 9.82, 9.106)
The outright sale of accounts receivables to a factor or financial agency; a continuous arrangement in which the factor purchases the client's trade debts, with or without recourse, exercises control over the credit extended to customers and administers the client's sales ledger.
:::

::: definition Forfaiting (SM 9.86)
An arrangement of bill discounting in which a financial institution or bank buys the trade bills or receivables from exporters, the exporter relinquishing the right to receive payment from the importer, providing immediate finance on a without recourse basis.
:::

## Worked examples

::::: example Premix distributors: longer credit, or a discount?
Tapri sells chai premix to distributors at ₹200 a carton, all on credit. Present terms are net 30: credit sales are ₹1,20,00,000 a year, every distributor pays on the 30th day, and bad debts are 1% of sales. Variable cost is ₹140 a carton; at the present volume the average cost is ₹160 a carton, and fixed costs will not change. Tax is 25%, and Tapri wants a post-tax return of 12% on its investment in receivables. Dev's consultant estimates:

| Policy | Terms | Credit sales (₹) | Distributors pay | Bad debts |
|---|---|---:|---|---:|
| A | net 60 | 1,32,00,000 | on day 60 | 2% |
| B | net 90 | 1,44,00,000 | on day 90 | 3.5% |
| C | 2/10 net 90 | 1,44,00,000 | half on day 10 | 2.25% |

Under C, half the distributors (by value) take the discount and pay on the 10th day; the other half pay on the 90th day.

**Required:** Evaluate the policies by the total approach and recommend one; check the result with the incremental approach. Assume a 360-day year.

:::: answer Show the working
**Working notes**

1. Units at present = ₹1,20,00,000 ÷ ₹200 = 60,000 cartons. Fixed cost = (₹160 − ₹140) × 60,000 = **₹12,00,000**.
2. Variable cost = 70% of sales (₹140 ÷ ₹200).
3. Policy C: average collection period = 50% × 10 + 50% × 90 = **50 days**; cash discount = ₹1,44,00,000 × 50% × 2% = **₹1,44,000**.
4. Opportunity cost = total cost × collection period ÷ 360 × 12%. Present: ₹96,00,000 × 30/360 × 12% = ₹96,000.

:::: example Institutional sales: what is a quick payment worth?
Tapri's institutional division (offices and colleges) has credit sales of ₹60,00,000 a year on terms of net 45, but its customers actually pay, on average, on the 60th day; bad debts are 2.5% of sales. Dev proposes terms of 2/10 net 45. He expects no change in sales; 60% of customers (by value) to take the discount and pay on the 10th day; the rest to keep paying on the 60th day; and bad debts to arise only on the sales to customers who do not take the discount, at 2.5% of those sales. The contribution to sales ratio is 30%, the tax rate 25% and the opportunity cost of funds 16% before tax. Assume a 360-day year.

**Required:**

(i) Advise whether the division should change its credit terms.

(ii) Calculate the highest rate of cash discount at which the change would still pay, if the same customers take it.

::: answer Show the working
**Working notes**

1. New average collection period = 60% × 10 + 40% × 60 = 6 + 24 = **30 days**.
2. Cash discount = ₹60,00,000 × 60% × 2% = **₹72,000**.
3. Bad debts: present 2.5% × ₹60,00,000 = ₹1,50,000; proposed 2.5% × 40% × ₹60,00,000 = **₹60,000**.
4. The statement is after tax, so the pre-tax opportunity cost is converted: 16% × (1 − 0.25) = **12%** (as in Illustration 14, SM 9.81). Investment in receivables at variable cost (only relevant costs): present ₹42,00,000 × 60/360 = ₹7,00,000; proposed ₹42,00,000 × 30/360 = ₹3,50,000.

| Less: opportunity cost @ 12% (WN 4) | 84,000 | 42,000 |
| **Net benefit** | **11,53,500** | **12,09,000** |

**(i) Advice:** The proposed terms should be adopted: the net benefit increases by ₹12,09,000 − ₹11,53,500 = **₹55,500**. The discount costs ₹72,000, but it saves ₹90,000 of bad debts and halves the money locked in receivables.

**(ii) The highest discount.** With the same customers taking it, the discount can cost at most what the change saves before tax: bad debts ₹90,000, plus the opportunity cost saved, ₹42,000 after tax = ₹42,000 ÷ 0.75 = ₹56,000 before tax; ₹1,46,000 in all. The discount is paid on 60% × ₹60,00,000 = ₹36,00,000 of sales, so

$$\text{Highest rate} = \frac{1{,}46{,}000}{36{,}00{,}000} \times 100 = 4.06\%$$

Any discount below about 4.06% leaves the division better off, on the assumption that the same 60% take it up.
:::

:::: example Modern trade receivables: which factoring scheme?
Tapri's credit sales to supermarket chains are ₹4,80,00,000 a year, collected on average in 72 days. Bad debts are 1.5% of credit sales. Credit administration costs ₹9,00,000 a year, of which ₹6,00,000 would be avoided if the receivables were factored.

A factor offers two schemes. Under both it withholds a reserve of 10% and advances the balance at 15% p.a., the interest deducted in advance for the collection period, which stays at 72 days:

- **Scheme R, with recourse**: commission 1%; bad debts remain Tapri's.
- **Scheme N, without recourse**: commission 2%; the factor bears the bad debts.

Tapri's bank cash credit costs 13% p.a.

**Required:**

(i) Calculate the rate of effective cost of each scheme, on the advance to be paid.

(ii) Advise Tapri.

Assume a 360-day year.

::: answer Show the working
**Working notes**

1. Average receivables = ₹4,80,00,000 × 72/360 = **₹96,00,000**; they turn over 360 ÷ 72 = 5 times a year.
2. Advance from the factor:

| Particulars | Scheme R (₹) | Scheme N (₹) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Average receivables | 96,00,000 | 96,00,000 |
| Less: commission @ 1% and 2% | 96,000 | 1,92,000 |
| Less: reserve @ 10% | 9,60,000 | 9,60,000 |
| Amount available for advance | 85,44,000 | 84,48,000 |
| Less: interest @ 15% for 72 days | 2,56,320 | 2,53,440 |
| **Advance to be paid** | **82,87,680** | **81,94,560** |

**(i) Statement showing the evaluation of the factoring proposals**

| Particulars | Scheme R (₹) | Scheme N (₹) |
|---|---:|---:|
| **A. Annual savings** | | |
| Credit administration saved | 6,00,000 | 6,00,000 |
| Bad debts avoided (1.5% of ₹4,80,00,000) | — | 7,20,000 |
| Total | 6,00,000 | 13,20,000 |
| **B. Annual cost of factoring** | | |
| Factoring commission (× 360/72) | 4,80,000 | 9,60,000 |
| Interest charges (× 360/72) | 12,81,600 | 12,67,200 |
| Total | 17,61,600 | 22,27,200 |
| **C. Net annual cost to the firm (B − A)** | **11,61,600** | **9,07,200** |
| **Rate of effective cost (C ÷ advance to be paid)** | **14.02%** | **11.07%** |

On the amount available for advance, the rates are 13.60% and 10.74%.

**(ii) Advice:** Scheme N's effective cost (11.07%) is below the bank's 13%; Scheme R's (14.02%) is above it. Tapri should avail factoring under Scheme N. The cheaper commission is the dearer scheme: with recourse, the ₹7,20,000 of bad debts stays with Tapri, far more than the extra 1% commission (₹4,80,000 a year) that buys them off. Either way the commission works out as a percentage of annual credit sales: 2% of the ₹96,00,000 outstanding, five times a year, is 2% of ₹4,80,00,000.

## Under the hood

### Factoring against bills discounting (SM 9.106)

| Point | Factoring | Bills discounting |
|---|---|---|
| Name | "Invoice factoring" | "Invoice discounting" |
| Parties | Client, factor and debtor | Drawer, drawee and payee |
| Nature | Management of book debts | Borrowing from commercial banks |
| Law | No specific Act | Negotiable Instruments Act |

Factoring as a source of working capital finance, and bills discounting and rediscounting, are in [[fm.wc.banking|financing of working capital and bank credit]].

### Factoring against forfaiting

ICAI's suggested answer to MTP January 2025 (Series II, Q4(a), 4 marks) distinguishes them on these points:

| Point | Factoring | Forfaiting |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Sale of receivables to a factor for immediate cash | Export finance: the exporter sells the rights to trade receivables to a forfaiter for instant cash |
| Recourse | With or without recourse | Always without recourse |
| Amount paid | Generally 80% to 90% upfront | 100% of the value of exported goods |
| Receivables | Domestic or international | International |
| Cost | Commission borne by the seller | Borne by the overseas buyer, if any |
| Secondary market | None: complete once sold | Exists: the receivables can be traded |

### Corners ICAI has used

- **The other side of the cash discount.** "2/10 net 45" costs the customer who does not take it about 23.5% a year on ICAI's compounding formula (Illustration 17, SM 9.100): that analysis is in [[fm.wc.payables|management of payables]].
- **Extra suppliers' credit reduces the investment.** In September 2024 Q3(a), creditors gave 15 days' credit on the extra variable cost, and the suggested answer deducted those average creditors from the average debtors before applying the 20% return.
- **The acceptable risk of non-payment.** TYK Problem 12 solves for the bad debt percentage that just earns the required after-tax return: profit after tax on the extra sales = required return on the investment in debtors, solved for bad debts (SM 9.152–9.153).
- **A factor that guarantees payment.** In May 2024 Q1(c) the factor guaranteed payment in 45 days against the firm's 70: the savings then include interest on the reduction in the collection period, as in ICAI's format.
- **Collection programmes without new sales.** TYK Problem 11 compares spending more on collection against interest saved on lower receivables and lower bad debts, all as increments over the present (SM 9.150–9.151).

::: trick Credit pays when the margin beats the lock-up
A longer credit period pays only if extra contribution − extra bad debts − extra cash discount exceeds the extra investment in debtors × required return. This is just the difference between two policies' net benefits, so it always agrees with the total approach. Conditions: fixed costs unchanged, and profit and the required return on the same (pre- or post-tax) basis. Still work out every policy: a step that fails can be followed by one that passes.
:::

## Answer frame

### "Explain the three aspects of management of receivables"

1. **Credit policy**: credit standards, credit terms and collection efforts; the trade-off between profit on additional sales and the cost of carrying debtors and bad debts; credit period ("net 50") and cash discount policy (rate, discount period, net credit period: "3/15 net 60").
2. **Credit analysis**: how risky it is to advance credit to a party; due diligence or reputation check.
3. **Control of receivables**: follow-up and a suitable collection policy; laying down policies and executing them.

### "Describe factoring" / "Describe the salient features of forfaiting"

**Factoring**: definition (outright sale of receivables to a factor, who bears the risk of collection and services the accounts for a fee); with or without recourse; advantages (immediate cash, predictable cash flows, no impact on debt-equity, not a loan, efficient record keeping and collection); the evaluation (savings against commission and interest; accept if the effective cost is below the cost of borrowing).

**Forfaiting**: meaning ("relinquish a right"; bank buys exporters' receivables without recourse); the four functions in order; the five features; Exim Bank's Buyer's Credit as the example.

### "Explain the ageing schedule"

Receivables classified by age from the date of invoice (0–30, 31–60, 61–90, 91–120 days and more); compared with earlier schedules and with other firms; gives closer control over the quality of individual accounts; shows old accounts accumulating; supplements the average collection period. A two-date table makes the point.

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: the profit locked up in debtors
Computing the investment in receivables as sales × days ÷ 360 when ICAI's format wants the cost of credit sales. In Worked example 1, policy C at sales value gives ₹20,00,000 instead of ₹15,66,667, and an opportunity cost ₹52,000 too high. **Fix:** total cost (or variable cost, when only the contribution ratio is given), and say which; ICAI uses sales only when no cost is given (TYK Problem 11, SM 9.151).
:::

::: mistake The discount rate applied to everybody
Cash discount taken as 2% of all credit sales when only 60% of customers take it: ₹1,20,000 instead of ₹72,000 in Worked example 2. **Fix:** credit sales × share taking the discount × rate.
:::

::: mistake Commission for one period compared with an annual saving
Setting Scheme N's ₹1,92,000 commission for one 72-day collection period against an annual bad-debt saving of ₹7,20,000. **Fix:** put everything on an annual basis: × 360 ÷ 72.
:::

::: mistake Factoring and forfaiting treated as the same thing
Writing "forfaiting may be with or without recourse". **Fix:** forfaiting is always without recourse and for export receivables; factoring may be either way and domestic or international.
:::

Q: A firm's credit sales are ₹36,00,000; 40% of customers (by value) take a discount of 1% by paying on the 10th day, and the rest pay on the 50th day. What is the average collection period in days?

- [ ] Factoring with recourse
- [ ] Pledging the receivable with his bank
- [x] Forfaiting
- [ ] Bills discounting with recourse
> Forfaiting is for export receivables, always without recourse, and pays 100% of the value: the bank buys the letter of credit and takes the risk. Factoring with recourse and pledging leave the risk with him; pledging is also a loan that raises debt.

Q: Under the total approach, a policy shows an expected profit of ₹18,00,000; the total cost of its credit sales is ₹72,00,000, the collection period 45 days and the required return 20%. What is its net benefit, in rupees? (Assume 360 days.)
= 1620000
> Investment in receivables = ₹72,00,000 × 45/360 = ₹9,00,000; opportunity cost @ 20% = ₹1,80,000; net benefit = ₹18,00,000 − ₹1,80,000 = ₹16,20,000.

- [x] avail the factoring service
- [ ] not avail it, because non-recourse factoring carries a higher commission
- [ ] avail it only if the factoring is with recourse
- [ ] not avail it, because factoring is a loan that raises the debt-equity ratio
> ICAI: avail factoring if its rate of effective cost is less than the existing cost of borrowing (11.2% against 12.5%), or if it gives positive net annual benefits. A higher commission for non-recourse factoring is already inside the 11.2%. Factoring is not a loan and does not affect the debt-equity ratio; pledging does.

- [x] Interest or opportunity cost on funds blocked in receivables
- [x] Collection costs
- [ ] Cost of carrying finished goods inventory
- [x] Defaulting costs
> ICAI's four: the cost of additional funds blocked, administrative costs, collection costs and defaulting costs. Inventory carrying cost belongs to inventory management.

## Recap

- Objective: optimise the return on investment in receivables; three aspects: credit policy, credit analysis, control of receivables.
- "3/15 net 60": 3% discount within 15 days, otherwise pay within 60; cash discount = credit sales × share taking it × rate.
- Costs of maintaining receivables: funds blocked (interest or opportunity cost), administration, collection, default.
- Total approach: expected profit − opportunity cost of investment in receivables; incremental approach: incremental profit − required return on incremental investment; both pick the same policy.
- Opportunity cost = total cost of credit sales × collection period ÷ 360 (365) × required rate; fixed cost = (average − variable cost per unit) × present units.
- Factoring: sale of receivables, with or without recourse, no impact on debt-equity; accept if effective cost < cost of borrowing.
- Effective cost of factoring = net annual cost ÷ advances to be paid (or ÷ amount available for advance) × 100.
- Forfaiting: exporters' receivables sold to a bank without recourse; Exim Bank's Buyer's Credit. Ageing schedule: receivables by age, compared over time. (Hand the diary to the agent only if his cut is cheaper than the bank.)

## Flashcards

Q:: The basic objective of management of receivables (ICAI)?
A:: To optimise the return on investment on these assets.

Q:: The three aspects of management of receivables?
A:: Credit policy (credit standards, credit terms, collection efforts), credit analysis, and control of receivables.

Q:: What does "3/15 net 60" mean?
A:: A 3% cash discount if the customer pays within 15 days; if the offer is not availed, payment must be made within 60 days.

Q:: ICAI's four costs of maintaining receivables?
A:: Cost of additional funds blocked (interest on loan funds or opportunity cost of own funds), administrative costs, collection costs and defaulting costs.

Q:: Formula: opportunity cost of investment in receivables (ICAI format)?
A:: Total cost of credit sales × collection period (days) ÷ 365 (or 360) × required rate of return ÷ 100.

Q:: Total fixed cost in a credit policy question, from average cost?
A:: (Average cost per unit − variable cost per unit) × number of units sold on credit under the present policy.

Q:: Pledging vs factoring of receivables?
A:: Pledging uses receivables as security for a short-term loan (50–90% financed; adds debt). Factoring is the outright sale of receivables to a factor, which bears the collection risk and services the accounts for a fee; it is not a loan.

Q:: Factoring with recourse and without recourse?
A:: With recourse: if the factor cannot collect, it turns the receivable back to the firm (generally replaced with new receivables). Without recourse: the factor bears the ultimate risk of default and charges a higher commission.

Q:: Rate of effective cost of factoring?
A:: Net annual cost of factoring ÷ advances to be paid × 100 (or ÷ amount available for advance); advances to be paid = amount available for advance − interest deducted by the factor.

Q:: When should a firm avail factoring (ICAI's advice)?
A:: If the rate of effective cost of factoring is less than the existing cost of borrowing, or if factoring results in positive net annual benefits; not if the effective cost exceeds the cost of borrowing.

Q:: Forfaiting: meaning and recourse?
A:: From the French "relinquish a right": a bank or financial institution buys exporters' trade bills or receivables, the exporter relinquishing the right to receive payment from the importer; always without recourse.

Q:: The five steps of the debt collection programme?
A:: Monitoring the state of receivables; intimation as the due date approaches; e-mail and telephonic advice on the due date; reminding the legal recourse on overdue accounts and following an escalation matrix; legal action on overdue accounts.
