## In plain words

### Tapri goes abroad

Tapri is opening kulhad cafés in Dubai and London, and Kesar wants every new café to run on solar power.

> **Kesar:** I want Tapri on Nasdaq. Imagine the photo.
> **Dev:** I am imagining it, Kesar-ji. The present value of that photo is lower than you think. Also, we can borrow dollars without selling a single share.
> **Ananya:** And the solar cafés can pay for themselves: investors queue up for green bonds.
> **Kesar:** And Rohan says KulhadKart will raise money from "the crowd". Which crowd?
> **Dev:** The internet, Kesar-ji. Thousands of people, a few thousand rupees each.

A company today is not limited to Indian banks and Indian investors. It can **borrow abroad** (a foreign currency loan from a bank, or an **external commercial borrowing** from any non-resident lender), **sell bonds abroad** (a **Euro bond**, in a currency other than that of the country where it is sold, or a **foreign bond**, in that country's own currency), and **sell shares abroad** without listing the shares themselves: a bank abroad issues **depository receipts**, each representing a number of its shares, which trade on a foreign exchange (**ADRs** in the US, **GDRs** mainly in Europe). A foreign company can do the reverse in India through **Indian Depository Receipts**.

At the other end of the scale, a start-up that no bank will lend to can turn to **crowdfunding**: raising money from a large group of people through an online platform, in exchange for shares (**equity crowdfunding**), as loans with interest (**peer-to-peer lending**), or for nothing at all (**donation**).

::: analogy The cloakroom token
At a wedding hall you hand in your coat and get a token. The coat stays in the cloakroom; the token can be handed to anyone, and whoever holds it can claim the coat. A depository receipt is that token: the company's shares stay with a bank, and the receipts, each standing for a fixed number of shares, are bought and sold on an exchange in another country, in that country's money.
:::

::: key
International financing reaches foreign capital three ways: loans (foreign currency loans, ECBs), bonds (Euro bonds, foreign bonds, ESG bonds and their variants) and equity (ADRs, GDRs; IDRs the other way round). Contemporary sources such as crowdfunding raise small sums from many people through online platforms, in exchange for equity, for interest, or for nothing.
:::

## How it works

### 1. International funding and angel financing: chapter 1's view

The scope chapter introduces two of these sources among the ways of procuring funds (SM 1.6; [[fm.intro.role|the finance function]]):

- **International funding.** With liberalisation and globalisation, a business enterprise has options to raise capital from international markets also. **Foreign Direct Investment (FDI)** and **Foreign Institutional Investors (FII)** are two major routes for raising funds from foreign sources, besides **ADRs and GDRs**. The mechanism of procuring funds has to be modified in the light of the requirements of foreign investors.
- **Angel financing.** A form of equity financing where an **angel investor**, a wealthy individual, provides capital for start-up or expansion in exchange for an ownership/equity in the company. Angels have idle cash and look for a higher rate of return than traditional investments give, and typically invest around 25 to 60 per cent to help a company get started. This source is sometimes the last option for start-ups which do not qualify for bank funding and are **too small for venture capital** financing.

### 2. Where external finance comes from abroad

The sources of external financing include (SM 2.35–2.36):

1. **Commercial banks**: like domestic loans, banks all over the world extend **foreign currency (FC) loans** for international operations, and allow overdrawing over and above the loan amount.
2. **Development banks**: long- and medium-term loans, including FC loans. Many national agencies offer concessions to foreign companies to invest in their country and to finance exports from it, e.g. the EXIM Bank of the USA.
3. **Discounting of trade bills**: a short-term financing method, widely used in Europe and Asian countries for domestic and international business.
4. **International agencies**: the International Finance Corporation (IFC), the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (IBRD), the Asian Development Bank (ADB), the International Monetary Fund (IMF), etc.
5. **International capital markets**, such as London, cater to organisations borrowing in rupees and foreign currency. FC is available under **four main systems**: the Euro-currency market, export credit facilities, bonds issues, and financial institutions.

The **Euro-currency market** began with **dollar-denominated bank deposits and loans in Europe**, particularly in London. Euro-dollar deposits are dollar time deposits at foreign branches of US banks and at some foreign banks; banks based in Europe accept dollar deposits and make dollar loans. Funds are made available as loans through syndicated Euro-credit of instruments such as FRNs and FR certificates of deposit (SM 2.36).

### 3. External commercial borrowings

**External commercial borrowings (ECBs)** are commercial loans (bank loans, buyers' credit, suppliers' credit, securitised instruments such as floating rate notes and fixed rate bonds) availed from **non-resident lenders**, with a ==minimum average maturity of 3 years== (SM 2.36).

- Raised from internationally recognised sources: (i) international banks, (ii) international capital markets, (iii) multilateral financial institutions such as the IFC and ADB, (iv) export credit agencies, (v) suppliers of equipment, (vi) foreign collaborators and (vii) foreign equity holders.
- Two routes. Under the **automatic route**, no RBI or Government approval is needed; under the **approval route**, it is. Companies registered under the Companies Act and NGOs engaged in micro finance are eligible for the automatic route; financial institutions and banks dealing exclusively in infrastructure or export finance, and those which participated in the textile and steel sector restructuring packages approved by the government, take the approval route.

::: warning ECB rules change
The maturity and the route conditions are as the Study Material states them (SM 2.36). Verify against the Study Material and Statutory Update for your attempt.
:::

### 4. The other instruments of the international market

In ICAI's order (SM 2.36–2.38):

| Instrument | What it is |
|---|---|
| Euro bonds | Debt instruments **not denominated in the currency of the country in which they are issued**, e.g. a Yen note floated in Germany; generally in bearer form, without the investor's name or country, so attractive to investors seeking privacy |
| Foreign bonds | Issued by **foreign corporations or governments**, **denominated in the currency of the country where issued**, e.g. a British firm placing dollar bonds in the USA; exposed to default risk, and to exchange rate risk for investors whose home currency differs |
| Fully hedged bonds | Foreign bonds whose currency risk is eliminated by selling the entire stream of principal and interest payments in forward markets |
| Medium term notes (MTN) | A programme under which several lots of bonds, with different coupons, currencies, etc., are issued when market opportunities suit; documentation and regulatory approvals are taken once |
| Floating rate notes (FRN) | Issued up to seven years' maturity; interest rates adjusted to reflect the prevailing exchange rates; cheaper money than foreign loans |
| Euro commercial papers (ECP) | Short-term money market instruments, maturity less than one year, usually designated in US dollars |
| Foreign currency option | The right, not the obligation, to buy or sell foreign currency at a specified price on or before a specified date; a hedge against financial and economic risks |
| Foreign currency futures | Obligations, not rights, to buy or sell a specified foreign currency in the present for settlement at a future date |
| Foreign Euro bonds | Local names for such bonds: **Yankee bonds** (US), Swiss Frances (Switzerland), **Samurai bonds** (Tokyo), **Bulldogs** (UK) |
| Euro convertible bonds | Convertible into a pre-determined number of equity shares, usually at a premium; fixed interest; may include a call option (issuer may redeem early) or a put option (holder may sell back at a set date and price) |
| Euro convertible zero bonds | No interest; converted on maturity at a pre-determined price; usually five years; treated as a deferred equity issue |
| Euro bonds with equity warrants | Coupon at market rates; detachable warrants; the pure bonds trade at a discount; suit fixed income funds seeking regular income |
| ESG-linked bonds | Bonds carrying the issuer's responsibility to prioritise optimal environmental, social and governance factors; investing in them is socially responsible investing |

- **Green bonds**: the most popular ESG bonds, issued by a financial, non-financial or public institution, whose proceeds finance **green projects** with a positive environmental and/or climate impact, including eco-friendly technology. India is the second-largest green bond market; Ghaziabad Municipal Corporation became the first municipal corporation to raise ₹150 crore through a green bond, in 2021.
- **Social bonds**: finance socially impactful projects (human rights, equality, animal welfare, etc.); "vaccine bonds" are an example.
- **Sustainability-linked bonds (SLBs)**: a combination of green and social bonds whose proceeds are **not meant for a specific project** but for general corporate purposes, to achieve **key performance indicators (KPIs)**. UltraTech Cement raised US\$400 million through India's first SLB in 2021, aiming to reduce carbon emissions over the bond's 10-year life.

### 5. The foreign bonds table

The long-term sources section has its own table of foreign bonds (SM 2.13–2.15), which RTP September 2026 Q10(b) asked for in full:

| Foreign bond | Salient features |
|---|---|
| Foreign currency convertible bond (FCCB) | A very low rate of interest; the issuer gets foreign currency at a very low cost; the risk is that if the bond has to be redeemed at maturity, the issuer must pay and may not have the money then |
| Plain vanilla bond | The issuer pays the principal along with interest; no options; issued as a discounted bond or a coupon-bearing bond |
| Convertible floating rate note (FRN) | The holder may convert it into a longer-term debt security with a specified coupon; protects the investor against falling interest rates; the long-term security can be sold for a profit; capital gain is not applicable to an FRN |
| Drop lock bond | An FRN with a normal floating rate, automatically converted into a fixed rate bond if interest rates fall below a predetermined level; the fixed rate stays till maturity. A convertible FRN is a long option structure; a drop lock bond a short option structure |
| Variable rate demand obligations | A normal FRN with a nominal maturity, which the holder can sell back to the trustee at par plus accrued interest: an exit option, so more liquid than a normal FRN |
| Yield curve note (YCN) | A structured debt security whose yield rises when prevailing interest rates fall and falls when they rise; used to hedge interest rate risk; works like an inverse floater |
| Euro bond | Issued or traded in a country using a currency other than the one in which it is denominated, outside the jurisdiction of the central bank issuing that currency; issued by multinationals (a British company issuing a dollar Eurobond in Germany). The term has nothing to do with the euro |
| Emerging market bond | Issued by developing countries; higher yields than US corporate and Treasury bonds; credit default swaps protect bondholders against default; higher risk than domestic debt |

The **masala bond**, a rupee-denominated bond sold to overseas investors, is listed among the Indian bonds: see [[fm.sources.long|long-term sources]].

### 6. Euro issues by Indian companies: ADR, GDR, IDR

**Euro issuance** refers to the sources of funding that can be accessed to raise money outside the home country in foreign currency. Indian companies may raise foreign currency through ordinary equity shares via **GDRs/ADRs** and/or **FCCBs**, issued to foreign investors (institutional investors or individuals, including NRIs, residing abroad). Such investment is treated as **Foreign Direct Investment**. The government guidelines on these issues are covered under the Foreign Currency Convertible Bonds and Ordinary Shares (through depositary receipt mechanism) Scheme, 1993 and the notifications issued after it (SM 2.39–2.40).

- **American Depository Receipts (ADRs)**: securities offered by non-US companies who want to list on any US exchange. Each ADR represents a certain number of the company's regular shares, and lets US investors buy them without the costs of investing directly in a foreign stock exchange (SM 2.40).
- **Global Depository Receipts (GDRs)**: negotiable certificates held in the bank of one country representing a specific number of shares of a stock traded on the exchange of another country, used to raise capital in dollars or euros; mainly traded in European countries, particularly London (SM 2.40).
- **Indian Depository Receipts (IDRs)**: the depository receipt mechanism applied in the Indian capital market: **foreign companies** issue IDRs to raise funds from the Indian market on the same lines as an Indian company uses ADRs/GDRs to raise foreign capital. IDRs are listed and traded in India like other Indian securities (SM 2.41).

**Why Indian companies preferred GDRs to ADRs** (SM 2.40): the US market exposes them to a **higher level of responsibility** than a European listing in **disclosure, costs, liabilities and timing**. The regulations are more stringent and onerous, even for companies already listed and held by retail investors at home. The most onerous aspect is providing **full, half-yearly and quarterly accounts in accordance with, or at least reconciled with, US GAAPs**.

**ADRs/GDRs and the Indian scenario** (SM 2.40): Indian companies are shedding their reluctance to tap the US markets. **Infosys** was the first Indian company listed on Nasdaq, in 1999; the first Indian firm to issue a sponsored GDR or ADR was **Reliance Industries**. Others listed overseas include Wipro, MTNL, State Bank of India, Tata Motors and Dr. Reddy's Lab.

### 7. Contemporary sources of funding

**Crowd funding** (SM 2.41): raising money for an individual or organisation from a group of people to fund a project, typically **via the internet** (social media and crowdfunding websites), from family, friends, strangers, corporates and many more, in exchange for **equity** (equity funding), **loans** (P2P lending) or **nothing at all** (donation). It also helps a start-up **substantiate demand for its product before entering production**. **Three parties** are involved: the **fund raiser, the mediator and the fund investor**; the platforms (mediators) may charge processing fees, transaction fees, etc., as a fixed amount, a percentage or both.

- **Equity funding** (SM 2.41): investors invest money in an organisation and receive its **securities** in return, each entitled to a stake depending on the investment. Its digital nature targets a **large number of investors with small contributions**; mostly adopted by start-ups. Platforms: StartEngine, EquityNet, SeedInvest, etc.
- **Peer-to-peer (P2P) lending** (SM 2.41–2.42): the category of crowdfunding where **lenders match with borrowers** to provide **unsecured loans** through an online platform, repaid with interest, with a risk of default (just as banks bear in conventional lending). Investors visit the platforms and choose among borrowers considering risk and return. Platforms: i2iFunding, Lendbox, Faircent, RupeeCircle, etc.
- **Start-up funding** (SM 2.42): a newly formed start-up finds bank loans difficult, leaving crowdfunding as one of its sources: equity funding, P2P lending or both.
- **Donation-based crowdfunding** (SM 2.42): a large group of people donate money as charity for a cause, with **no expectation of ownership or debt**. Platforms: GoFundMe (medical needs, education), Ketto (medical needs), FuelADream (charity projects, new ideas).

## Worked examples

:::: example Dev's world tour: name the instrument
Dev is reviewing six proposals. Identify each instrument, with a reason.

(a) A US\$50 million loan from a Singapore bank to Tapri, average maturity five years, raised without RBI or Government approval.

(b) Certificates, each representing four Tapri shares held by a bank, traded on the London market.

(c) A yen-denominated Tapri bond sold to investors in Germany, in bearer form.

(d) Dollar bonds placed by Tapri with investors in the USA.

(e) A bond whose proceeds Tapri may use for any corporate purpose, issued on a commitment to meet a stated key performance indicator: cutting its delivery fleet's carbon emissions over the bond's life.

(f) A floating rate note that turns into a fixed rate bond, until maturity, if interest rates fall below 6%.

::: answer Model answer
**(a) External commercial borrowing, automatic route.** ECBs are commercial loans availed from non-resident lenders with a minimum average maturity of 3 years; under the automatic route no RBI or Government approval is needed, and companies registered under the Companies Act are eligible for it.

**(b) Global Depository Receipts.** Negotiable certificates held in the bank of one country representing a specific number of shares of a stock traded on the exchange of another country, mainly traded in European countries and particularly in London.

**(c) Euro bond.** A debt instrument not denominated in the currency of the country in which it is issued (yen bonds in Germany), generally in bearer form, attractive to investors seeking privacy.

**(d) Foreign bond (in the US, a Yankee bond).** A debt instrument issued by a foreign corporation and denominated in the currency of the country where it is issued, like ICAI's example of a British firm placing dollar bonds in the USA.

**(e) Sustainability-linked bond (SLB).** A target-based ESG bond, a combination of green and social bonds, whose proceeds are not meant for a specific project but for general corporate purposes, to achieve key performance indicators (KPIs). It is not a green bond: a green bond's proceeds must finance green projects.

**(f) Drop lock bond.** A floating rate note with a normal floating rate, automatically converted into a fixed rate bond if interest rates fall below a predetermined level; the fixed rate stays until maturity.
:::

:::: example Kesar wants Nasdaq; BrewBucks wants India
(i) Kesar wants Tapri to raise US\$200 million by listing on a US exchange "for the photo". Dev prefers a GDR issue in London. Advise, with reasons.

(ii) BrewBucks, the American coffee chain, wants to raise money from Indian investors and have its securities traded in India. Explain the instrument it can use.

::: answer Model answer
**(i)** An ADR is a security offered by a non-US company that wants to list on a US exchange; each ADR represents a certain number of the company's regular shares. A GDR is a negotiable certificate held in the bank of one country representing a specific number of shares traded on the exchange of another country, mainly in Europe and particularly London.

Indian companies have preferred GDRs to ADRs because the US market exposes them to a higher level of responsibility than a European listing in the areas of disclosure, costs, liabilities and timing. The regulations are more stringent and onerous, even for companies already listed and held by retail investors in their home country, and the most onerous aspect is providing full, half-yearly and quarterly accounts in accordance with, or at least reconciled with, US GAAPs.

**Advice:** unless Tapri needs US investors specifically (and Infosys, the first Indian company on Nasdaq, shows it can be done), a **GDR issue in London** raises the same foreign capital with a lighter disclosure and compliance burden. Either way, the investment is treated as Foreign Direct Investment.

**(ii) Indian Depository Receipts.** The depository receipt mechanism has been applied in the Indian capital market through IDRs: a foreign company issues IDRs to raise funds from the Indian market on the same lines as an Indian company uses ADRs/GDRs to raise foreign capital. The IDRs are listed and traded in India in the same way as other Indian securities.

:::: example KulhadKart asks the crowd
Rohan's KulhadKart has three plans: (1) sell a 10% stake to about 2,000 small investors through an online platform; (2) borrow ₹40 lakh, unsecured, from individual lenders matched with it on an online platform, repayable with interest; (3) collect money online for a free chai kitchen for flood-hit families, promising donors nothing in return.

**Required:** Identify the form of each plan, name the parties to the process, and explain why crowdfunding suits a start-up like KulhadKart, which no bank will lend to, and what else it can do for Rohan before he starts production.

::: answer Model answer
**Forms.** (1) is **equity crowdfunding**: investors put money into the organisation and receive its securities, each entitled to a stake depending on the investment, a large number of investors with small contributions. (2) is **peer-to-peer (P2P) lending**: lenders matched with borrowers provide unsecured loans through an online platform, repaid with interest, with a risk of default. (3) is **donation-based crowdfunding**: people donate money as charity for a cause with no expectation of any ownership or debt.

**Parties.** Three parties are involved: the **fund raiser** (KulhadKart), the **mediator** (the platform, which may charge processing or transaction fees, as a fixed amount, a percentage or both) and the **fund investor**.

**Why crowdfunding.** A start-up company, being newly formed, needs funds before starting any project, but finds it difficult to manage loans from banks, leaving crowdfunding as one of its sources of finance: it can raise money from a large group of people, in the form of equity funding, P2P lending or both (SM 2.42). Crowdfunding also helps a start-up to **substantiate demand for its product before entering into production** (SM 2.41): if 2,000 people buy a stake in KulhadKart, Rohan knows the machine-pressed kulhad has buyers before he starts production.
:::

## Under the hood

### Euro bond or foreign bond: one question decides

Ask: **is the bond in the currency of the country where it is sold?** If not, it is a Euro bond (yen in Germany; dollars in Germany). If it is, and the issuer is foreign, it is a foreign bond (dollars in the USA by a British firm), and the local name follows the market: Yankee (US), Samurai (Tokyo), Bulldog (UK). The "Euro" has nothing to do with the euro currency.

### Samurai and Bulldog bonds, as ICAI's answers describe them

The Study Material only names these bonds (SM 2.38), but ICAI has asked for their features (the Bulldog bond: RTP January 2026 Q11(a), old-scheme November 2023 Q6(a), 4 marks; the Samurai bond: old-scheme RTP November 2022; both: old-scheme RTP May 2023). The suggested answers give:

| Feature | Samurai bond | Bulldog bond |
|---|---|---|
| Denominated in | Japanese yen | Pound sterling (GBP) |
| Issued in | Tokyo | London |
| Issuer | A non-Japanese company | A non-UK company |
| Regulations | Japanese | Great Britain |
| Purpose | Access to capital in the Japanese market | Access to capital in the UK market |
| Use of proceeds | Japanese operations, or the company's local opportunities | UK operations, or the company's local opportunities |

The Samurai answers add that it can also be used to hedge foreign exchange risk.

### ECBs, Euro-issues and the maturity classification

The maturity classification places external commercial borrowings, Euro-issues and foreign currency bonds among **medium-term** sources, and "international financing like Euro-issues, foreign currency loans" among long-term ones, so Euro-issues appear in both lists (SM 2.4): see [[fm.sources.long|long-term sources]].

::: exam The wording traps
- A Euro bond is defined by **currency against country**, not by Europe; a foreign bond by a **foreign issuer** in the local currency.
- ADRs are for US exchanges, GDRs mainly Europe and London, IDRs for **foreign companies in India**.
- ECB: **non-resident** lenders, **minimum average maturity of 3 years**, automatic **and** approval routes.
- Green and social bonds are **project-based**; SLB proceeds are for **general corporate purposes**, tied to KPIs.
:::

::: trick ADR, GDR, IDR by the first letter
**A** for America, **G** for global (in practice Europe and London), **I** for India, where the issuer is foreign. Condition: the first two are issued by non-US (for ADRs) or Indian companies raising capital abroad; the IDR runs the other way.
:::

## Answer frame

### "Distinguish between ADRs and GDRs" (the old-scheme May 2022 suggested answer)

::: compare
| Basis | ADR | GDR |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | A negotiable instrument issued by a US bank, representing a non-US company's stock traded on a US stock exchange | A negotiable instrument issued by an international depository bank, representing a foreign company's stock traded worldwide |
| Issued in | The US domestic capital market | The European capital market |
| Listed in | The American stock exchange | Non-US stock exchanges |
| Relevance | Foreign companies trade in the US stock market | Foreign companies trade in any country's stock market other than the US |

### "Discuss the features of international funding and angel financing"

Two headings. International funding: liberalisation and globalisation; FDI and FII as the two major routes, besides ADRs and GDRs; procurement adapted to foreign investors' requirements. Angel financing: equity financing by a wealthy individual for start-up or expansion in exchange for equity; seeks a higher return than traditional investments; typically 25 to 60 per cent; sometimes the last option for start-ups that do not qualify for bank funding and are too small for venture capital.

### "Explain the features of crowd funding"

Definition (from a group of people, typically via the internet) → three forms (equity, P2P loans, donation) → helps substantiate demand before production → three parties (fund raiser, mediator, fund investor) → platform fees.

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: the Euro bond issued in euros
Calling any bond in euros a Euro bond, or a dollar bond sold in the US by a British firm a Euro bond. **Fix:** a Euro bond is not denominated in the currency of the country where it is issued (the term has nothing to do with the euro); the British firm's dollar bond in the USA is a foreign bond (a Yankee bond).
:::

::: mistake IDRs for Indian companies going abroad
**Fix:** Indian companies go abroad with ADRs and GDRs; IDRs are issued in India by foreign companies.
:::

::: mistake Crowdfunding and P2P lending as rivals
**Fix:** P2P lending is a category of crowdfunding (the loan form); crowdfunding also covers equity funding and donations.
:::

::: mistake The green bond that funds anything
**Fix:** green and social bonds finance specific projects; it is the sustainability-linked bond whose proceeds go to general corporate purposes, tied to KPIs.
:::

> Under an MTN programme several lots of bonds, with different coupons, currencies, etc., are issued, the timing of each decided by market opportunities, with the documentation and approvals taken at one point of time (SM 2.37). Fully hedged bonds remove currency risk by selling the payments forward; Euro convertible zero bonds convert into equity at maturity; ECPs are money market instruments of less than one year.

Q: As the Study Material describes the ECB routes, which of these borrowers must take the approval route?

- [ ] An NGO engaged in micro finance activities

> Companies registered under the Companies Act and NGOs engaged in micro finance are eligible for the automatic route (no RBI or Government approval). Financial institutions and banks dealing exclusively in infrastructure or export finance, and those in the approved textile and steel restructuring packages, take the approval route. The 3-year minimum average maturity applies to every ECB; it does not choose the route.

## Recap

- External finance abroad: commercial banks (FC loans), development banks, discounting of trade bills, international agencies (IFC, IBRD, ADB, IMF), international capital markets (Euro-currency market, export credit, bond issues, financial institutions).
- ECB: commercial loans from non-resident lenders, minimum average maturity 3 years, seven recognised sources, automatic route (no approval) or approval route.
- Euro bond: not in the currency of the country of issue (often bearer), while a foreign bond is a foreign issuer's bond in the local currency (Yankee, Samurai, Bulldog).
- MTN: many lots, one documentation; FRN: up to 7 years; ECP: under one year, usually US dollars; fully hedged bonds: forward sale of all payments.
- ESG bonds: green and social (project-based), sustainability-linked (target-based, KPIs, general purposes).
- Foreign bonds table: FCCB, plain vanilla, convertible FRN, drop lock, variable rate demand obligations, yield curve note, Euro bond, emerging market bond.
- ADR (US exchange), GDR (Europe, London), IDR (foreign company in India); FDI; Indian companies preferred GDRs because US disclosure is more onerous (US GAAP quarterly accounts).
- Crowdfunding: equity, P2P (unsecured loans with interest), donation; three parties; platforms charge fees. Angel: wealthy individual's equity, 25–60%, when banks will not lend and VC will not look. (Kesar got his photo, in London.)

## Flashcards

Q:: What are external commercial borrowings (ECBs)?
A:: Commercial loans (bank loans, buyers' credit, suppliers' credit, securitised instruments such as FRNs and fixed rate bonds) availed from non-resident lenders with a minimum average maturity of 3 years.

Q:: Through which routes can ECBs be accessed?
A:: The automatic route (no RBI or Government approval; companies registered under the Companies Act, NGOs in micro finance) and the approval route (approval needed; e.g. FIs and banks dealing exclusively in infrastructure or export finance).

Q:: Euro bond versus foreign bond?
A:: Euro bond: not denominated in the currency of the country where it is issued (a yen note floated in Germany), often bearer. Foreign bond: issued by a foreign corporation or government, denominated in the currency of the country where it is issued (a British firm's dollar bonds in the USA).

Q:: What are fully hedged bonds?
A:: Foreign bonds whose currency fluctuation risk is eliminated by selling the entire stream of principal and interest payments in forward markets.

Q:: What is a medium term note (MTN) programme?
A:: A programme under which several lots of bonds with different features (coupons, currencies) are issued at times chosen for market opportunities, with documentation and regulatory approvals taken at one time.

Q:: Name the three kinds of ESG bonds and how they differ.
A:: Green bonds and social bonds are project-based (proceeds for specific green or social projects); sustainability-linked bonds are target-based (proceeds for general corporate purposes to achieve KPIs).

Q:: What are the features of a drop lock bond?
A:: A floating rate note with a normal floating rate, automatically converted into a fixed rate bond if interest rates fall below a predetermined level; the fixed rate stays until maturity; a short option structure.

Q:: What are American Depository Receipts?
A:: Securities offered by non-US companies that want to list on a US exchange; each ADR represents a certain number of the company's regular shares, letting US investors buy them without investing directly on a foreign exchange.

Q:: What are Global Depository Receipts?
A:: Negotiable certificates held in the bank of one country representing a specific number of shares of a stock traded on the exchange of another country; used to raise dollars or euros; mainly traded in Europe, particularly London.

Q:: What are Indian Depository Receipts?
A:: Depository receipts issued in India by foreign companies to raise funds from the Indian capital market, on the same lines as ADRs/GDRs; listed and traded in India like other Indian securities.

Q:: Why did Indian companies prefer GDRs to ADRs?
A:: A US listing exposes them to a higher level of responsibility in disclosure, costs, liabilities and timing; regulations are more stringent; and they must give full, half-yearly and quarterly accounts in accordance with, or reconciled with, US GAAPs.

Q:: What is crowdfunding, and who are its three parties?
A:: Raising money for an individual or organisation from a group of people, typically via the internet, in exchange for equity, loans (P2P) or nothing (donation). Parties: the fund raiser, the mediator (platform) and the fund investor.
