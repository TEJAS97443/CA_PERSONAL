## In plain words

### A wedding hall with no guests, a stall with a queue

Tapri Tea Co. Ltd wants ₹200 crore for 300 new kulhad cafés, and the board meeting has three opinions.

> **Kesar:** Issue shares. No interest, no bank manager, sab adjust ho jayega.
> **Dev:** Issue enough shares and the family drops below 51%. Then the board adjusts you.
> **Ananya:** And if it is all debt, one bad monsoon and the interest still arrives on the first of the month.

**Capital structure** is the combination of capitals from different sources of finance: equity shareholders' fund, preference share capital and long-term external debts. Choosing it is a balancing act between **control** (the existing owners keep their majority), **risk** (financial risk stays within tolerable limits) and **cost** (the overall cost of capital stays at a minimum). All three cannot be achieved together, so the finance manager balances them, with one prime objective: to maximise the value of the company.

Two things can go wrong with the amount of capital itself. Kesar's cousin raised ₹60 crore for a sweets factory that runs at about a third of its capacity: its earnings cannot pay a decent dividend and interest, and its share price sinks. That is **over-capitalisation**: more capital than the business needs or can earn on. The original Tapri stall in Indore, on the other hand, ran on ₹50,000 of capital and earned like a factory: a dividend rate no one else matched, and competitors queuing to copy it. That is **under-capitalisation**: less capital in the books than its earning capacity warrants.

::: analogy The wedding hall and the chai stall
A wedding hall built for a thousand guests that fills two hundred seats a night has too much capital for what it earns: every rupee invested earns very little, and the owner cuts what he pays his backers. That is over-capitalisation. A roadside chai stall with a queue round the block earns a fortune on a few thousand rupees: high returns attract competitors, the staff ask for a raise, and customers feel overcharged. That is under-capitalisation. One is a problem of earnings; the other only of the books catching up.
:::

::: key
Capital structure is planned to balance control, risk and cost so as to maximise the value of the firm, guided by seven principles (trading on equity, growth and stability of sales, cost, risk, control, flexibility, other considerations). Over-capitalisation (capital more than earnings justify) and under-capitalisation (capital less than earnings warrant) are both bad; over-capitalisation is the more dangerous.
:::

## How it works

### 1. What the capital structure decision decides

**Capital structure** is the combination of capitals from different sources of finance; the capital of a company consists of equity shareholders' fund, preference share capital and long-term external debts. The source and quantum of capital is decided keeping in mind the following factors (SM 5.2):

1. **Control**: capital structure should be designed in such a manner that existing shareholders continue to hold majority stake.
2. **Risk**: capital structure should be designed in such a manner that financial risk of a company does not increase beyond tolerable limit.
3. **Cost**: overall cost of capital remains minimum.

The capital structure decision refers to deciding the **forms of financing** (which sources to be tapped), their **actual requirements** (amount to be funded) and their **relative proportions** (mix) in total capitalisation (SM 5.3). Because value of the firm = EBIT ÷ overall cost of capital, the mix decides the weights in the WACC and so the value: capital structure is relevant in maximising value of the firm and minimising overall cost of capital. Whether it really changes the WACC is the question the [[fm.cs.theories|capital structure theories]] argue about.

### 2. The financing decision process

Whenever funds are to be raised to finance investments, a capital structure decision is involved: the demand for funds generates a new capital structure (SM 5.3–5.4).

### 3. The menu of sources

A firm can raise funds from different sources in different proportions (SM 5.30). It can:

- (a) exclusively use debt (in case of existing company), or
- (b) exclusively use equity capital, or
- (c) exclusively use preference share capital (in case of existing company), or
- (d) use a combination of debt and equity in different proportions, or
- (e) use a combination of debt, equity and preference capital in different proportions, or
- (f) use a combination of debt and preference capital in different proportion (in case of existing company).

The choice of the combination of these sources is called the **capital structure mix**.

### 4. Seven principles for choosing the mix

ICAI's factors affecting capital structure, in ICAI's order (SM 5.30–5.32):

1. **Financial leverage or trading on equity.** The use of long-term fixed interest bearing debt and preference share capital along with equity share capital is called financial leverage or trading on equity. Long-term debt increases EPS if the firm yields a return higher than the cost of debt; preference capital does too, but because interest is deductible for tax, ==the leverage impact of debt is much more==. Leverage can operate adversely if the rate of interest is more than the expected rate of earnings, so it needs caution.
2. **Growth and stability of sales.** Stable sales let a firm raise a higher level of debt, because it will not face difficulty in meeting fixed commitments of interest and repayment. Usually, the greater the rate of growth of sales, the greater the use of debt. If sales are highly fluctuating or declining, debt should be avoided as far as possible.
3. **Cost principle.** The ideal pattern minimises cost of capital and maximises EPS. Debt is cheaper than equity from the point of its cost, and interest is deductible for income tax purpose, whereas dividends are not.
4. **Risk principle.** Reliance is placed more on equity than on excessive use of debt. More debt means a higher commitment of interest, which erodes shareholders' value in unfavourable business situations: as debt increases, financial risk increases.
5. **Control principle.** Existing management control and ownership should remain undisturbed. New equity dilutes the existing control pattern and involves higher cost; more debt causes no dilution in control but a higher degree of financial risk.
6. **Flexibility principle.** Choose a combination that is easier to adjust to future changes in the need for funds. Debt can be interchanged (an 18% loan can be replaced by new debt at 15%); the same option may not be available with equity.
7. **Other considerations.** Nature of industry, timing of issue and competition in the industry. Industries facing severe competition resort to more equity than debt.

A suitable pattern is a satisfactory compromise between these principles, reached by assigning weights to them in terms of the characteristics of the company (SM 5.32).

::: compare
| Principle | Points towards | Because |
|---|---|---|
| Trading on equity | debt, if return > cost of debt | EPS rises; interest is tax-deductible |
| Growth and stability of sales | debt if sales are stable or growing; equity if they fluctuate or decline | fixed interest must be met every year |
| Cost | debt | cheaper; interest tax-deductible |
| Risk | equity | more debt, more financial risk |
| Control | debt | no dilution of control |
| Flexibility | debt | debt can be replaced by cheaper debt; the same option may not exist for equity |
| Other considerations | equity in severely competitive industries | nature of industry, timing, competition |
:::

### 5. The optimal capital structure

The objective of financial management is to maximise wealth, so one should choose the capital structure which maximises wealth (SM 5.32); the optimum is where value of the firm is highest and cost of capital is lowest (SM 5.9). Three analyses are used (SM 5.32–5.33):

1. **EBIT-EPS-MPS analysis**: choose the capital structure which maximises market price per share. Start with the same EBIT for all capital structures, calculate EPS, then multiply EPS by the PE ratio or divide it by the cost of equity to arrive at MPS.
2. **Indifference point analysis**: the first analysis considers value at a given EBIT only; to see whether the decision changes when EBIT changes, do indifference point analysis.
3. **Financial break-even point analysis**: financial risk changes with capital structure; though it is already reflected in the PE ratio or cost of equity, it may be considered separately by calculating the financial BEP.

All three are worked in [[fm.cs.ebiteps|EBIT-EPS analysis]].

### 6. Over-capitalisation: more capital than the business can carry

It is a situation where a firm has more capital than it needs, or in other words assets are worth less than its issued share capital, and earnings are insufficient to pay dividend and interest. It mainly arises when existing capital is not effectively utilised because of a fall in earning capacity, while the company has raised funds more than its requirements. ==The chief sign of over-capitalisation is the fall in payment of dividend and interest== leading to fall in value of the shares (SM 5.45).

**Causes** (SM 5.45):

1. Raising more money through issue of shares or debentures than company can employ profitably.
2. Borrowing huge amount at higher rate than rate at which company can earn.
3. Excessive payment for the acquisition of fictitious assets such as goodwill etc.
4. Improper provision for depreciation, replacement of assets and distribution of dividends at a higher rate.
5. Wrong estimation of earnings and capitalisation.

**Consequences** (SM 5.45):

1. Considerable reduction in the rate of dividend and interest payments.
2. Reduction in the market price of shares.
3. Resorting to "window dressing".
4. Some companies may opt for reorganisation; sometimes the matter gets worse and the company may go into liquidation.

**Remedies** (SM 5.46):

1. Company should go for thorough reorganisation.
2. Buyback of shares.
3. Reduction in claims of debenture-holders and creditors.
4. Value of shares may also be reduced. This will result in sufficient funds for the company to carry out replacement of assets.

### 7. Under-capitalisation: less capital than the earnings warrant

It is just reverse of over-capitalisation: a state when actual capitalisation is lower than proper capitalisation as warranted by its earning capacity. It normally happens with companies which have insufficient capital but large secret reserves in the form of considerable appreciation in the values of fixed assets not brought into the books (SM 5.46).

**Consequences** (SM 5.46):

1. The dividend rate will be higher in comparison to similarly situated companies.
2. Market value of shares will be higher than value of shares of other similar companies, because their earning rate is considerably more than the prevailing rate on such securities.
3. Real value of shares will be higher than their book value.

**Effects** (SM 5.46–5.47):

1. It encourages acute competition: high profitability encourages new entrepreneurs into the same type of business.
2. High rate of dividend encourages the workers' union to demand high wages.
3. Normally common people (consumers) start feeling that they are being exploited.
4. Management may resort to manipulation of share values.
5. It invites more government control and regulation and higher taxation also.

**Remedies** (SM 5.47):

1. The shares should be split up. This will reduce dividend per share, though EPS shall remain unchanged.
2. Issue of bonus shares is the most appropriate measure, as this will reduce both dividend per share and the average rate of earning.
3. Revising upward the par value of shares in exchange of the existing shares held by them.

### 8. Which is worse?

Both are not good. Over-capitalisation is more dangerous to the company, shareholders and the society than under-capitalisation; under-capitalisation can be handled more easily, and it is not an economic problem but a problem of adjusting capital structure. So under-capitalisation is considered less dangerous, but both situations are bad and every company should strive to have a proper capitalisation (SM 5.47).

::: compare
| Point | Over-capitalisation | Under-capitalisation |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | more capital than needed; assets worth less than issued share capital | actual capitalisation lower than proper capitalisation warranted by earning capacity |
| Typical origin | funds raised beyond requirement; fall in earning capacity | large secret reserves (appreciated fixed assets not in the books) |
| Dividend rate | falls considerably | higher than similar companies |
| Market value of shares | falls | higher than similar companies; real value above book value |
| Remedies | reorganisation; buyback; reduce claims of debenture-holders and creditors; reduce value of shares | split shares; bonus shares (most appropriate); revise par value upward |
| Danger | more dangerous to the company, shareholders and society | less dangerous: not an economic problem but one of adjusting capital structure |
:::

## Worked examples

:::: example Mehra Mithai's empty factory
Mehra Mithai Ltd, owned by Kesar's cousin, raised ₹40 crore by issuing equity shares and ₹20 crore by 14% debentures to build a sweets factory and buy the brand of a closed sweet-shop chain, paying ₹12 crore for its goodwill. The factory runs at 35% of capacity and the company earns 8% on its capital employed. The dividend has fallen from 18% to 3% in two years and the share trades below its face value. The directors now propose to skip depreciation this year "to show a healthier profit".

**Required:** Identify the situation the company is in, giving reasons from the facts. State its causes and consequences as seen here, and advise the remedies.

::: answer Model answer
**Meaning.** Over-capitalisation is a situation where a firm has more capital than it needs, or in other words assets are worth less than its issued share capital, and earnings are insufficient to pay dividend and interest. Its chief sign is the fall in payment of dividend and interest, leading to a fall in the value of the shares.

**Facts and analysis.** Mehra Mithai has raised ₹60 crore but employs only 35% of the capacity it built, so the capital is not effectively utilised. It earns 8% on capital employed while paying 14% on debentures. The dividend has fallen from 18% to 3% and the share trades below face value. The company is **over-capitalised**.

**Causes visible in the case:**

1. Raising more money through issue of shares and debentures than the company can employ profitably (a factory at 35% capacity).
2. Borrowing at a higher rate (14%) than the rate at which the company can earn (8%).
3. Excessive payment for the acquisition of a fictitious asset (₹12 crore of goodwill of a closed chain).
4. Wrong estimation of earnings and capitalisation.
5. The proposal to skip depreciation is improper provision for depreciation, which would worsen the position.

**Consequences:** considerable reduction in the rate of dividend (18% to 3%); reduction in the market price of shares (below face value); the depreciation proposal is resorting to window dressing; if not corrected, the company may have to opt for reorganisation or may even go into liquidation.

**Remedies advised:**

1. A thorough reorganisation of the company.
2. Buyback of shares, to bring the capital in line with what the business can employ.
3. Reduction in the claims of debenture-holders and creditors.
4. Reduction in the value of shares, which will leave sufficient funds for replacement of assets.

The directors should not skip depreciation: proper provision is needed, and window dressing does not cure over-capitalisation.
:::
::::

:::: example Indore Kettle Works and its old land
Indore Kettle Works Ltd, which makes Tapri's brewing equipment, has share capital of ₹1 crore and earns a profit after tax of about ₹90 lakh every year. Its factory land, bought in 1985 for ₹20 lakh, is now worth about ₹40 crore but has never been revalued. It pays a dividend of 60% against 15% by similar companies, and its shares trade at many times their book value. This year two new competitors have set up nearby, the workers' union has demanded a 40% wage rise, and a consumer forum is calling the company's prices exploitative.

**Required:** Identify the situation, with reasons. Explain the effects the company is already facing, advise the remedies, and comment on whether this situation is more dangerous than over-capitalisation.

::: answer Model answer
**Meaning.** Under-capitalisation is a state when the actual capitalisation of a company is lower than its proper capitalisation as warranted by its earning capacity. It normally happens with companies which have insufficient capital but large secret reserves in the form of considerable appreciation in the values of fixed assets not brought into the books.

**Facts and analysis.** A capital of ₹1 crore earning ₹90 lakh a year, land worth ₹40 crore carried at ₹20 lakh, a dividend rate of 60% against 15% by similar companies, and a market value of shares far above book value: the company is **under-capitalised**.

**Effects already visible:**

1. Acute competition: high profitability has encouraged two new entrepreneurs into the same business.
2. The high rate of dividend has encouraged the workers' union to demand high wages.
3. Consumers feel they are being exploited.
4. Further effects to guard against: management may be tempted to manipulate share values, and the company may invite more government control and regulation and higher taxation.

**Remedies advised:**

1. Issue of bonus shares, the most appropriate measure, as it reduces both the dividend per share and the average rate of earning.
2. Splitting up the shares, which reduces dividend per share though EPS remains unchanged.
3. Revising upward the par value of shares in exchange for the existing shares.

**Comment.** Under-capitalisation is less dangerous than over-capitalisation: it is not an economic problem but a problem of adjusting the capital structure, and it can be handled more easily. Both are bad, and the company should strive for a proper capitalisation.
:::
::::

:::: example Tapri plans its ₹200 crore
Tapri Tea Co. Ltd needs ₹200 crore for 300 new cafés. The promoter family holds 52% of the equity. Café sales have grown about 15% a year for five years with little fluctuation. Interest rates are high this year but expected to fall within two years. BrewBucks has just entered Tapri's cities and price competition is severe.

**Required:** Advise the board on the principles that should guide the choice between debt and equity for this expansion.

::: answer Model answer
While choosing a suitable financing pattern, the finance manager must reach a satisfactory compromise between the principles of capital structure, assigning weights to them according to the company's characteristics. Applied to Tapri:

1. **Control principle**: the family holds only 52%. A large issue of new equity would dilute the existing control pattern; debt causes no dilution. This favours debt.
2. **Growth and stability of sales**: sales are growing at about 15% and are stable, so Tapri can meet fixed interest commitments; greater growth permits greater use of debt. This favours debt.
3. **Trading on equity and cost principle**: if the cafés earn more than the cost of debt, debt raises EPS, and interest is tax-deductible. This favours debt, provided the expected return exceeds the rate of interest.
4. **Risk principle**: more debt raises financial risk and the fixed interest burden in unfavourable situations. This calls for restraint.
5. **Flexibility principle**: interest rates are expected to fall; debt raised now can later be replaced by cheaper debt, which equity does not allow.
6. **Other considerations**: competition from BrewBucks is severe, and industries facing severe competition resort to more equity than debt.

**Advice.** A mix: debt for a large part of the ₹200 crore (control, stable growing sales, tax-deductible interest), on terms that allow refinancing when rates fall, with an equity component or internal funds to keep financial risk tolerable given the competition. The final proportion should maximise the value of the firm, tested by EBIT-EPS-MPS, indifference point and financial break-even analysis.
:::

## Under the hood

### Capital structure is not the whole balance sheet

The Study Material's own MCQs separate two terms: **financial structure** refers to all financial resources (MCQ 3), while **capital structure** means long-term debt, preference shares and equity shares (MCQ 5) (SM 5.49, 5.59). Current liabilities are part of the financial structure but not of the capital structure. The chapter summary adds retained earnings to the long-term sources in the mix (SM 5.47).

### Two lists that are not the same list

A question asking for the factors deciding the **source and quantum** of capital wants the three of SM 5.2: control, risk, cost (ICAI's answer to the November 2022 paper, old scheme, gave exactly these three). A question on **factors affecting / determining** capital structure or "principles" wants the seven of SM 5.30–5.32. The Study Material answers its own theory question "Explain the principles of trading on equity" by referring to the seven principles (SM 5.59).

### The theories behind the principles

The cost and risk principles weigh the same things as the trade-off theory (the tax benefit of debt against the risk it adds), and a preference for internal funds and debt over new equity is the order the pecking order theory describes: see [[fm.cs.theories|capital structure theories]] and [[fm.sources.long|long-term sources of finance]].

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: the bonus issue for an over-capitalised company
Recommending a bonus issue or a share split to a company that cannot pay its dividends. Those spread earnings over more shares: they cure under-capitalisation. **Fix:** over-capitalisation needs reorganisation, buyback, reduction of debenture and creditor claims, or reduction in the value of shares.
:::

::: mistake The two definitions swapped
Writing "actual capitalisation lower than proper capitalisation warranted by earning capacity" for over-capitalisation: that is under-capitalisation, and the Study Material's own MCQ 11 tests exactly this slip. **Fix:** over = more capital than needed, earnings insufficient; under = capitalisation lower than the earning capacity warrants.
:::

::: mistake Under-capitalisation read as "short of cash"
Explaining under-capitalisation as a company that cannot pay its bills or lacks working capital. **Fix:** in ICAI's sense it is a company whose earnings are high for its small book capital, often because of secret reserves; its symptoms are a high dividend rate and a high market value.
:::

::: mistake Only "cost" in a capital structure answer
Arguing that debt is cheaper and stopping there. **Fix:** a full answer balances cost against risk and control (the three factors) and, for "factors affecting", walks through all seven principles, ending in a compromise.
:::

## Recap

- Capital structure: the combination of equity shareholders' fund, preference share capital and long-term external debts; balance control, risk and cost to maximise value.
- The decision: forms of financing, amounts and proportions in total capitalisation.
- Seven principles in order: trading on equity, growth and stability of sales, cost, risk, control, flexibility, other considerations.
- Optimal structure: highest value, lowest cost of capital; found by EBIT-EPS-MPS, indifference point and financial BEP analysis.
- Over-capitalisation: more capital than needed, earnings insufficient; 5 causes, 4 consequences, 4 remedies; chief sign: falling dividend and interest.
- Under-capitalisation: capitalisation below what earnings warrant (secret reserves); high dividend rate and share value; remedies: split, bonus (most appropriate), higher par value.
- Over-capitalisation is the more dangerous. (The empty wedding hall is the real worry; the busy chai stall only needs bigger books.)

Q:: The three factors keeping in mind which the source and quantum of capital are decided?
A:: Control (existing shareholders keep majority stake), risk (financial risk within tolerable limits), cost (overall cost of capital minimum).

Q:: What does the capital structure decision decide?
A:: The forms of financing (sources), their actual requirements (amounts) and their relative proportions (mix) in total capitalisation.

Q:: List ICAI's seven principles (factors) of capital structure, in order.
A:: Financial leverage or trading on equity; growth and stability of sales; cost principle; risk principle; control principle; flexibility principle; other considerations (nature of industry, timing of issue, competition).

Q:: Define trading on equity.
A:: The use of long-term fixed interest bearing debt and preference share capital along with equity share capital; it raises EPS when the firm earns more than the cost of debt.

Q:: The three analyses for an optimal capital structure?
A:: EBIT-EPS-MPS analysis, indifference point analysis, financial break-even point analysis.

Q:: Define over-capitalisation and give its chief sign.
A:: A firm has more capital than it needs, or assets are worth less than its issued share capital, and earnings are insufficient to pay dividend and interest. Chief sign: fall in payment of dividend and interest, and in the value of shares.

Q:: Five causes of over-capitalisation?
A:: Raising more than can be employed profitably; borrowing at a rate higher than the company earns; excessive payment for fictitious assets such as goodwill; improper provision for depreciation and replacement and high dividends; wrong estimation of earnings and capitalisation.

Q:: Four consequences of over-capitalisation?
A:: Considerable reduction in rate of dividend and interest; reduction in market price of shares; window dressing; reorganisation, or even liquidation.

Q:: Four remedies for over-capitalisation?
A:: Thorough reorganisation; buyback of shares; reduction in claims of debenture-holders and creditors; reduction in the value of shares.

Q:: Define under-capitalisation.
A:: A state when actual capitalisation is lower than proper capitalisation as warranted by earning capacity; typical of companies with large secret reserves not brought into the books.

Q:: Three remedies for under-capitalisation?
A:: Split up the shares; issue bonus shares (the most appropriate measure); revise upward the par value of shares in exchange for existing shares.
