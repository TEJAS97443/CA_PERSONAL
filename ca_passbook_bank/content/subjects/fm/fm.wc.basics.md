## In plain words

### Record Diwali, empty bank account

Tapri's samosa factory in Indore has just had its best Diwali quarter: distributors ordered three times the usual frozen packs, and the quarter's accounts show a fat profit. On Friday, Dev cannot pay the milk and potato suppliers.

> **Kesar:** The accounts say we made a profit of ₹4 crore and the bank says we have nothing? Sab adjust ho jayega, just pay them.
> **Dev:** The profit is sitting in the cold store as samosas and in the distributors' unpaid bills. Cash pays the milkman on Friday, not profit.
> **Kesar:** Then how much money does the factory need just to keep running?
> **Dev:** As much as stays stuck on the wheel at any moment. Let me show you the wheel.

The money a business keeps tied up in its day-to-day running is its **working capital**. It sits in **current assets**, the things that turn into cash within the normal operating cycle: stocks of potatoes and flour, half-made samosas, packed samosas in the cold store, distributors who owe money, and cash itself. Part of it is paid for by other people: suppliers who give 25 days to pay, and staff who are paid at the end of the month. Those amounts owed are **current liabilities**. Current assets minus current liabilities is the **net working capital** the owners and lenders must provide.

The wheel is the **operating cycle**. A rupee leaves as cash to buy potatoes, waits 30 days as raw material, spends 12 days as samosas in the making, 18 days as packed stock, and 45 days as a distributor's unpaid bill before it comes home as cash. Suppliers carry the first 25 days. So Tapri's own money is out for 30 + 12 + 18 + 45 − 25 = 80 days, and the rupee can make the round only 4½ times in a 360-day year. The longer the round, the more money the factory needs to keep the wheel full.

Some of that money is needed all year: even in a quiet June the factory holds stock and waits for distributors. That base is **permanent working capital**. The Diwali bulge on top is **temporary working capital**: it comes and goes with the season. Sensible financing follows the same split: the permanent part from long-term money (shares, long-term loans), the bulge from short-term money (a bank limit used for three months).

::: analogy The giant wheel at the mela
Each rupee boards a cabin as cash, rides past raw material, work-in-progress, finished stock and debtors, and gets off as cash again. Working capital is the number of cabins you must fill to keep the wheel turning. A faster wheel (a shorter cycle) needs fewer cabins for the same number of riders a year; suppliers' credit is a stretch of the ride in someone else's cabin. At Diwali the crowd swells, and you hire extra cabins for the season instead of buying them for the whole year.
:::

::: key
Working capital is the money stuck in the operating cycle: current assets minus current liabilities. The longer the cycle (days in raw material, work-in-progress, finished goods and debtors, less suppliers' credit), the more of it a business needs. Holding more is safer but earns less; holding less earns more but risks running dry. The permanent part is financed long-term, the temporary part short-term.
:::

## How it works

### What counts as current

In accounting terms, working capital is the difference between current assets and current liabilities (SM 9.3).

::: definition Current assets and current liabilities (SM 9.3–9.4)
An **asset** is classified as current when (i) it is expected to be realised, or is intended to be sold or consumed, in the normal operating cycle of the entity or within twelve months after the reporting period, whichever is longer; and (ii) it is held primarily for the purpose of trading in the ordinary course of business.

A **liability** is classified as current when (i) it is expected to be settled in the normal operating cycle of the entity or within twelve months after the reporting period, whichever is longer; and (ii) it is settled either by the use of current assets or by creation of a new current liability.
:::

For working capital management ICAI groups them as follows (SM 9.3–9.4):

| Current assets | Current liabilities |
|---|---|
| (a) Inventory: raw material, work-in-process, finished goods | (a) Payables: trade payables and bills payable |
| (b) Receivables: trade receivables and bills receivable | (b) Outstanding payments: wages and salary, overheads and other expenses |
| (c) Cash or cash equivalents, including short-term marketable securities | Others: short-term borrowings, current portion of long-term debt, short-term provisions such as provision for taxes |
| (d) Prepaid expenses; others: short-term loans or advances, accrued revenue | |

**Working capital management** is a process designed to ensure that an organisation operates efficiently by monitoring and utilising its current assets and current liabilities to the best effect. Its primary objective is to enable a company to maintain ==sufficient cash flows to meet its day-to-day operating expenses and its short-term obligations== (SM 9.4).

- **Gross working capital** is the firm's investment in current assets. **Net working capital** is the difference between current assets and current liabilities. A positive net working capital indicates the company's ability to pay its short-term liabilities; a negative one shows inability to meet its short-term obligations (SM 9.4–9.5).
- **Permanent working capital** is the base: the minimum level of investment in current assets carried at all times to carry on day-to-day activities. It stays invested unless operations are scaled up or down permanently, and it is generally financed by long-term sources. **Temporary** (also called **variable** or **fluctuating**) working capital is the part required in addition to the permanent working capital, because of fluctuations in sales volume: more inventory for seasonal demand, for example. Both are necessary to facilitate production and sales through the operating cycle (SM 9.5).

### Neither too much nor too little

The finance manager must ensure that the working capital available is neither too large nor too small for the requirements (SM 9.6):

- **Too much** means idle funds. Funds have a cost: interest on the money locked up, or the opportunity cost of not investing it long term. Studies by the Bureau of Public Enterprises found that large funds locked up in working capital were one reason for the poor performance of public sector undertakings: **over-capitalisation**, too large funds for the requirements, a low rate of return.
- **Too little** brings the risk of insolvency: liabilities cannot be met, stock runs out, sales and reputation are lost.
- **Growth needs working capital too.** An investment decision must count the additional current assets that come with expansion: more production means more raw material and work-in-progress; more sales mean more debtors and finished goods (SM 9.6–9.7).

**What is optimum?** Traditionally, a **current ratio** (current assets ÷ current liabilities) of 2 for a manufacturing firm, supplemented by a **quick (acid test) ratio** (quick assets ÷ current liabilities, quick assets being current assets less inventory and prepaid expenses) of at least 1, are taken as indicators of a good working capital situation. As a thumb rule this may be adequate, but the optimum can be determined only with reference to the particular circumstances: a firm whose stock sells easily and whose debtors are as good as cash may be sound below 2, a restaurant with perishable stock cannot hold much, and a business with a long production time needs more (SM 9.7–9.8). Both excessive and inadequate working capital positions are dangerous. Ratios themselves are in [[fm.ratio.liquidity|liquidity ratios]].

### Nine things that decide how much you need

Working capital management is concerned with (a) maintaining adequate working capital (the level of each current asset and current liability) and (b) financing it (SM 9.8). The factors to consider while planning the requirement, in ICAI's order (SM 9.9–9.10):

1. **Need for cash**: the cash balance that meets day-to-day expenses while keeping holding costs (interest lost on surplus cash) low.
2. **Desired level of inventory**: enough for uninterrupted production, without excess investment in raw materials; techniques such as JIT and EOQ.
3. **Receivables**: credit terms that attract customers, such that the effect on cash flows and the cash conversion cycle is offset by more revenue and return on capital; tools such as early payment discounts and allowances.
4. **Short-term financing options**: inventory is ideally financed by supplier credit; depending on the cash conversion cycle, a bank loan or overdraft, or "converting debtors to cash" through factoring.
5. **Nature of business**: a restaurant sells mostly for cash and needs little; a pharmacy or bookstore holds more inventory.
6. **Market and demand conditions**: when demand far exceeds production, little finished stock piles up.
7. **Technology and manufacturing policies**: for seasonal demand, steady production all year or production only in the season.
8. **Operating efficiency**: eliminating waste, better coordination, process improvements reduce the need.
9. **Price level changes and exchange rate fluctuations**: rising prices need more funds for the same activity; an adverse exchange rate raises the cost of imported material.

### Liquidity against profitability: the 3 Es

Working capital is compared to the life blood of a living body or the lubricant of an engine. Its management needs the **3 Es**: **economy** in financing, **efficiency** in utilisation and **effectiveness** in achieving the intended objectives. Its scope has two broad areas: (i) **liquidity and profitability**, and (ii) **investment and financing decisions** (SM 9.10–9.11).

Every rupee of capital has a cost. More working capital may raise revenue, but funds tied up in idle assets reduce both liquidity and the chance of a better return elsewhere; so a **trade-off between liquidity and profitability** is needed, one that raises profitability without disturbing day-to-day functioning (SM 9.11). ICAI's table, component by component (SM 9.11–9.12):

| Component | Higher side (profitability) | Trade-off tool | Lower side (liquidity) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inventory | Fewer stock-outs increase profitability | EOQ, JIT to carry the optimum level | Less capital, but stock-outs and loss of goodwill |
| Receivables | Longer credit attracts customers and revenue (but more bad debts) | Evaluate the credit policy; use factoring agencies | Cash sales give liquidity but fail to boost sales |
| Prepaid expenses | Reduce uncertainty; profitable in inflation | Cost-benefit analysis | Improves or maintains liquidity |
| Cash and cash equivalents | Payables paid in time: goodwill, future discounts | Cash budgets and cash management techniques | Cash can be invested elsewhere |
| Payables and expenses | Capital can be used in other investment avenues | Evaluate the credit policy and its cost | Paid in time: goodwill, future discounts |

### How much to invest in current assets

The **investment decision** answers "how much" fund is tied up (effectiveness); the **financing decision** answers "where from", at the lowest possible cost (economy) (SM 9.12). There are no fixed rules for the level of investment; it depends on (SM 9.13): (a) **nature of industry** (construction, breweries: long gestation); (b) **types of products** (consumer durables hold more than perishables); (c) **manufacturing vs trading vs service** (a manufacturer holds raw material, work-in-process and finished goods; a trader only trading stock, a service entity only consumables); (d) **volume of sales** (high sales, high receivables); (e) **credit policy** (a liberal policy means high receivables and, depending on suppliers' credit, more capital for raw material).

Based on policy and the risk-return trade-off, there are three **approaches of working capital investment** (SM 9.13–9.14):

| Approach | What the firm does | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Aggressive** | Minimal current assets: low inventory, strict credit, little cash | Less fund tied up, lower financial cost | Stock-outs; growth held back, lower use of fixed assets and long-term debt; may fall behind competitors |
| **Conservative** | High current assets: more inventory, liberal credit, cash to pay any current liability at once | Higher sales, more demand from liberal credit, suppliers' goodwill | Higher cost of capital, obsolescence, bad debts, liquidity shortage in the long run from longer cycles |
| **Moderate** | In between | Balance of risk and return | |

A conservative policy implies greater liquidity and lower risk; an aggressive policy higher risk and poor liquidity; most firms follow a moderate policy, and a firm may change policy over time. Measured by the **current assets to fixed assets ratio** (with fixed assets constant), a higher ratio indicates a conservative policy and a lower one an aggressive policy (SM 9.14–9.15). ICAI's Illustration 1 shows the inverse relationship between this ratio and the return on total assets: the firm balances the "profitability-solvency tangle" by minimising the total of the cost of liquidity and the cost of illiquidity (SM 9.16). The aggressive approach would better suit a highly integrated organisation with efficient processes (SM 9.14).

### Where the money comes from: the matching principle

After determining the amount of working capital, the finance manager arranges the funds. ICAI advises bifurcating the requirement: ==the permanent working capital should be financed by long-term sources such as debt and equity; the temporary working capital may be financed by short-term sources== (SM 9.102).

Sources are **spontaneous** (arising naturally in the course of business: trade credit, credit from employees and from suppliers of services) or **negotiated** (specifically negotiated with commercial banks, financial institutions, the general public). The parameters that guide the choice (SM 9.102–9.103): (i) cost factor; (ii) impact on credit rating; (iii) feasibility; (iv) reliability; (v) restrictions; (vi) the **hedging approach or matching approach**, i.e. financing of assets with the same maturity as of assets. ICAI's own MCQ puts it this way: under the hedging approach, each asset would be offset with a financing instrument of the same approximate maturity (SM 9.113). The sources themselves are in [[fm.wc.banking|financing of working capital and bank credit]].

ICAI's Mock Test Paper (May 2026, Series II, Q2(a)) grades **financing policies** the same way it grades investment: with the same assets, a **conservative** financing policy uses more long-term debt and less short-term debt, an **aggressive** one the reverse, and a **moderate** one sits between. On Tapri's curve, the level up to which long-term money is used decides the policy:

### The operating cycle

The **operating cycle** analyses the receivable, inventory and payable cycles in days: receivables by the average days to collect, inventory by the average days to turn over, payables by the average days to pay a supplier (SM 9.17).

::: definition Operating (working capital) cycle (SM 9.17)
Working capital cycle indicates ==the length of time between a company's paying for materials, entering into stock and receiving the cash from sales of finished goods==. It can be determined by adding the number of days required for each stage in the cycle.
:::

$$\text{Operating cycle} = R + W + F + D - C$$

R = raw material storage period; W = work-in-progress holding period; F = finished goods storage period; D = receivables (debtors) collection period; C = credit period allowed by suppliers (creditors) (SM 9.19). Then

$$\text{Number of operating cycles in a year} = \frac{360 \text{ or } 365}{\text{Operating cycle}}$$

and the more operating cycles a year, the better: it means a shorter cycle (SM 9.20). Tapri's factory from the opening story, on a timeline:

The length of the operating cycle is an indicator of the performance of management; the **net operating cycle** represents the time interval for which the firm has to negotiate for working capital from its lenders (SM 9.19). Most businesses cannot finance the inventory and receivable days with accounts payable alone; the shortfall is covered by internally generated profits, borrowed funds or both (SM 9.18). Each component has two dimensions, **time and money**: move money faster round the cycle or tie up less of it, and the business generates cash or borrows less (SM 9.18–9.19):

| If you… | Then… |
|---|---|
| Collect receivables (debtors) faster | You release cash from the cycle |
| Collect receivables (debtors) slower | Your receivables soak up cash |
| Get better credit (duration or amount) from suppliers | You increase your cash resources |
| Shift inventory (stocks) faster | You free up cash |
| Move inventory (stocks) slower | You consume more cash |

Turning these days into rupees, the working capital requirement, is the next lesson: [[fm.wc.estimate|estimating working capital requirement]].

## Formats and formulas

*Reproduce the operating cycle formula and the five component formulas from memory: the denominators carry the marks.*

::: formula Working capital
$$\text{Net working capital} = \text{Current assets} - \text{Current liabilities} \qquad \text{Gross working capital} = \text{Current assets}$$
:::

::: formula Operating cycle and number of cycles
$$\text{Operating cycle} = R + W + F + D - C \qquad \text{Number of cycles} = \frac{360 \text{ or } 365}{\text{Operating cycle}}$$
:::

::: formula The five components (SM 9.20)
$$R = \frac{\text{Average stock of raw material}}{\text{Average cost of raw material consumption per day}}$$
$$W = \frac{\text{Average work-in-progress inventory}}{\text{Average cost of production per day}}$$
$$F = \frac{\text{Average stock of finished goods}}{\text{Average cost of goods sold per day}}$$
$$D = \frac{\text{Average receivables}}{\text{Average credit sales per day}} \qquad C = \frac{\text{Average payables}}{\text{Average credit purchases per day}}$$
Average = (opening + closing) ÷ 2. Raw material consumed = opening stock + purchases − closing stock. Work-in-progress inventory may also be termed works cost (SM 9.19).
:::

| Item | Numerator | Denominator (per day) |
|---|---|---|
| R | Average raw material stock | Raw material consumed |
| W | Average WIP | Cost of production |
| F | Average finished goods | Cost of goods sold |
| D | Average receivables | Credit sales |
| C | Average payables | Credit purchases |

::: formula The ratio tests of the optimum (SM 9.7)
$$\text{Current ratio} = \frac{\text{Current assets}}{\text{Current liabilities}} \qquad \text{Quick ratio} = \frac{\text{Current assets} - \text{Inventory} - \text{Prepaid expenses}}{\text{Current liabilities}}$$
Thumb rule: current ratio 2 for a manufacturing firm, quick ratio at least 1; the real optimum depends on the business.
:::

::: formula Investment policy measures (SM 9.15–9.16)
$$\frac{\text{Current assets}}{\text{Fixed assets}} \qquad \text{Return on total assets} = \frac{\text{EBIT}}{\text{Total assets}}$$
Higher CA/FA ratio: conservative; lower: aggressive (fixed assets constant).
:::

::: definition Permanent and temporary working capital (SM 9.5)
**Permanent working capital** refers to the base working capital, which is the minimum level of investment in the current assets that is carried by the entity at all times to carry its day-to-day activities. **Temporary working capital** refers to that part of total working capital which is required by an entity in addition to the permanent working capital; it is also called variable or fluctuating working capital.
:::

## Worked examples

:::: example The samosa factory's cycle, from the balance sheet
Tapri's samosa factory gives you the following information for 2025-26:

| Balances (₹ lakh) | 31 March 2025 | 31 March 2026 |
|---|---:|---:|
| Raw material | 38 | 46 |
| Work-in-progress | 20 | 24 |
| Finished goods | 50 | 58 |
| Receivables | 120 | 140 |
| Payables for raw material | 60 | 70 |

| Annual figures for 2025-26 | ₹ lakh |
|---|---:|
| Purchases of raw material | 480 |
| of which bought for cash at the Indore mandi | 60 |
| Cost of production | 900 |
| Cost of goods sold | 892 |
| Sales | 1,260 |
| of which cash sales at the factory outlet | 165 |

Take a year as 365 days and round each period to the nearest day.

**Required:**

(i) Calculate the raw material storage period, WIP holding period, finished goods storage period, receivables collection period and credit period from suppliers.

(ii) Calculate the gross and net operating cycles and the number of operating cycles in the year.

(iii) Dev expects the new distributor portal to bring the collection period down to 30 days. Calculate the new net operating cycle and number of operating cycles.

::: answer Show the working
**Working notes**

1. Raw material consumed = opening stock + purchases − closing stock = 38 + 480 − 46 = **₹472 lakh**. All purchases count here, cash or credit: the material is consumed either way.
2. Credit purchases = 480 − 60 = **₹420 lakh**; credit sales = 1,260 − 165 = **₹1,095 lakh**.

**(i) The five periods** (averages: raw material 42, WIP 22, finished goods 54, receivables 130, payables 65)

| Period | Working | Days |
|---|---|---:|
| Raw material (R) | 42 ÷ 472 × 365 = 32.48 | 32 |
| WIP (W) | 22 ÷ 900 × 365 = 8.92 | 9 |
| Finished goods (F) | 54 ÷ 892 × 365 = 22.10 | 22 |
| Receivables (D) | 130 ÷ 1,095 × 365 = 43.33 | 43 |
| Payables (C) | 65 ÷ 420 × 365 = 56.49 | 56 |

Each denominator is the flow that empties that stage: consumption for raw material, cost of production for WIP, cost of goods sold for finished goods, credit sales for receivables, credit purchases for payables. Cash sales never become receivables and cash purchases never become payables, so D and C use only the credit flows.

**(ii) Gross operating cycle** = R + W + F + D = 32 + 9 + 22 + 43 = **106 days**.

**Net operating cycle** = R + W + F + D − C = 106 − 56 = **50 days**.

Number of operating cycles = 365 ÷ 50 = **7.30 times**.

**(iii) Collection in 30 days**: net operating cycle = 32 + 9 + 22 + 30 − 56 = **37 days**; number of cycles = 365 ÷ 37 = **9.86 times**. Thirteen days less on the debtors' leg means the same costs need about a quarter less working capital (37 against 50 days of costs: 26% less).
:::

:::: example Premix division: how much to invest, and how to finance it
For 2026-27, Tapri's chai premix division expects fixed assets of ₹300 lakh and spontaneous current liabilities (trade payables and accruals) of ₹150 lakh whatever the policy. Dev states three investment policies as current assets to fixed assets ratios; more stock and book debts support more sales and so more EBIT:

| Investment policy | Current assets ÷ fixed assets | EBIT (₹ lakh) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Conservative | 1.6 | 150 |
| Moderate | 1.3 | 143 |
| Aggressive | 1.0 | 126 |

The board adopts the moderate policy. Kesar wants the cheapest money; Dev wants to sleep soundly on the night the bank's renewal letter arrives. Its current assets are needed at ₹300 lakh all year round, plus ₹90 lakh on average for the season. Equity will provide ₹300 lakh; the rest of the assets not met by spontaneous liabilities will be borrowed, short-term at 10% or long-term at 14%. Tax is 25%; use average balances for the year. The three financing policies:

- **Conservative**: all borrowing long-term.
- **Matching (hedging)**: short-term debt finances exactly the seasonal ₹90 lakh; the rest is long-term.
- **Aggressive**: short-term debt finances the seasonal ₹90 lakh and ₹60 lakh of the permanent need.

**Required:**

## Under the hood

### Gross and net operating cycle

R + W + F + D is the **gross operating cycle**: the whole round from buying material to collecting from the customer. Subtracting suppliers' credit gives the **net operating cycle** (also called the cash conversion cycle), the days for which the firm's own or borrowed money is out. The samosa factory's gross cycle in Worked example 1 is 32 + 9 + 22 + 43 = 106 days; its net cycle 50 days. ICAI's MTP for January 2026 (Series II, Q2) states a "cash-to-cash conversion cycle 30 days shorter than the gross operating cycle": the 30 days are the suppliers' credit.

### A higher current ratio is not a better business

Piling up inventory raises the current ratio but not efficiency: it ties cash up in unsold goods, adds storage, insurance and obsolescence costs, lowers inventory turnover and lengthens the operating cycle (ICAI MTP September 2025, Series I, Q4(a)). Inventory is the least liquid current asset; the quick ratio exposes it.

### Working capital in other chapters

A capital budgeting project's cash flows include the working capital it ties up at the start and releases at the end: [[fm.capbud.cashflows|estimating relevant cash flows]]. Over-capitalisation, its causes and remedies, belongs to capital structure: [[fm.cs.planning|capital structure planning]]. EOQ and inventory turnover come from Paper 4: [[cma.mat.eoq|economic order quantity]].

### 360 or 365 days

The Study Material allows either (SM 9.20, 9.24). Use what the question says; if it is silent, state your choice ("Assumed 360 days in a year") and use it consistently in every period.

::: trick Which flow empties the tank?
Each period = average balance ÷ the daily flow that **leaves** that stage: raw material leaves by consumption, WIP by completion (cost of production), finished goods by sale (cost of goods sold), debtors by collection (credit sales), creditors by payment (credit purchases). Holds whenever the periods are measured in days of cost (or sales, for debtors).
:::

### "Discuss the liquidity vs profitability issue"

1. Each rupee of capital bears a cost; liquidity must be maintained with that cost in mind.
2. Higher working capital may raise revenue and profit, but funds tied up in idle assets reduce liquidity and the opportunity to earn better returns.
3. Hence a trade-off is required that increases profitability without disturbing day-to-day functioning, through the 3 Es: economy in financing, efficiency in utilisation, effectiveness in achieving objectives.
4. The component table: inventory, receivables, prepaid expenses, cash, payables, with the higher side, the trade-off tool and the lower side.

### "Permanent and fluctuating working capital are both necessary. Describe."

Define each in ICAI's words (base level carried at all times, financed long-term; the part in addition to it, due to fluctuations in sales, e.g. seasonal inventory); a sketch of the two diagrams (flat and rising permanent line with the temporary bulge above); conclude that both facilitate production and sales through the operating cycle, and that the permanent part is financed by long-term and the temporary part by short-term sources.

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: the samosas measured against the wrong denominator
Dividing average WIP by cost of goods sold, or average finished goods by cost of production, or raw material by purchases, or creditors by all purchases. In Worked example 1, raw material on purchases gives 42 ÷ 480 × 365 = 31.94 days instead of 32.48; creditors on all purchases, cash ones included, give 49.43 days instead of 56.49, and the net cycle is overstated by a week. **Fix:** each stage over the flow that leaves it (the trick above); raw material consumed = opening + purchases − closing; only credit sales and credit purchases for D and C.
:::

::: mistake The creditors that were added
Writing the operating cycle as R + W + F + D + C. Suppliers' credit shortens the period for which the firm finances the cycle. **Fix:** R + W + F + D − C, and check: the net cycle is always shorter than the gross cycle.
:::

::: mistake "The higher the current ratio, the better"
A current ratio of 3.20 under a conservative policy is safer, not better: its return on total assets is the lowest. **Fix:** in theory answers say "neither too high nor too low"; the thumb rule of 2 (and quick ratio of 1) depends on the business.
:::

::: mistake Swapping aggressive and conservative
"Aggressive" sounds like "lots of stock". It is the opposite: aggressive means minimal current assets and, in financing, more short-term debt. **Fix:** aggressive takes risk to save funds; conservative pays for safety.
:::

= 15

## Recap

- Net working capital = current assets − current liabilities; gross working capital = current assets (SM 9.3–9.4).
- Permanent: the base carried at all times, financed long-term; temporary (variable, fluctuating): the extra due to sales fluctuations, financed short-term.
- Too much: idle funds, low return, over-capitalisation. Too little: insolvency risk, lost sales. Thumb rule: current ratio 2, quick ratio 1.
- Nine determinants: cash, inventory, receivables, short-term financing, nature of business, market and demand, technology and manufacturing policy, operating efficiency, price and exchange rates.
- Investment approaches: aggressive (minimal current assets, high return, high risk), conservative (the reverse), moderate; higher CA ÷ FA = more conservative.
- Operating cycle = R + W + F + D − C; number of cycles = 360 (or 365) ÷ operating cycle.
- Denominators: consumption, cost of production, cost of goods sold, credit sales, credit purchases.
- Hedging (matching) approach: finance each asset with funds of the same maturity. (Diwali cabins are hired, not bought.)

## Flashcards

Q:: ICAI's definition of the working capital (operating) cycle?
A:: The length of time between a company's paying for materials, entering into stock and receiving the cash from sales of finished goods; found by adding the days required for each stage.

Q:: Formula: operating cycle, with each letter named?
A:: R + W + F + D − C: raw material storage period, WIP holding period, finished goods storage period, receivables collection period, minus credit period allowed by suppliers.

Q:: Denominators of R, W, F, D and C?
A:: R: average raw material consumption per day; W: average cost of production per day; F: average cost of goods sold per day; D: average credit sales per day; C: average credit purchases per day.

Q:: Gross working capital and net working capital?
A:: Gross: the firm's investment in current assets. Net: current assets minus current liabilities.

Q:: Permanent working capital (ICAI)?
A:: The base working capital: the minimum level of investment in current assets carried at all times to carry on day-to-day activities; generally financed by long-term sources.

Q:: Temporary working capital (ICAI)?
A:: The part of total working capital required in addition to permanent working capital, due to fluctuation in sales volume (e.g. seasonal inventory); also called variable or fluctuating; may be financed by short-term sources.

Q:: Thumb rules ICAI quotes for a good working capital position?
A:: Current ratio of 2 for a manufacturing firm and quick (acid test) ratio of at least 1; the true optimum depends on the circumstances of the business.

Q:: The 3 Es of working capital management?
A:: Economy in financing, efficiency in utilisation and effectiveness in achieving the intended objectives.

Q:: Aggressive vs conservative working capital investment: liquidity, risk and the CA/FA ratio?
A:: Aggressive: minimal current assets, poor liquidity, higher risk and return, lower CA/FA ratio. Conservative: high current assets, greater liquidity, lower risk and return, higher CA/FA ratio.

Q:: The six parameters that guide the choice of working capital finance?
A:: Cost factor; impact on credit rating; feasibility; reliability; restrictions; hedging or matching approach (financing assets with funds of the same maturity).

Q:: Spontaneous vs negotiated sources of working capital finance?
A:: Spontaneous arise naturally in the course of business (trade credit, credit from employees and service suppliers); negotiated must be specifically negotiated with banks, financial institutions or the public.
