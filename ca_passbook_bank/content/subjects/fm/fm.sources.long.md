## In plain words

### Kesar wants a factory on an overdraft

Tapri Tea Co. Ltd wants a samosa factory at Dewas. It will cost ₹100 crore and run for fifteen years.

> **Kesar:** The bank already gives us an overdraft. Use that. Sab adjust ho jayega.
> **Dev:** An overdraft is repayable on demand, Kesar-ji. The factory pays for itself over fifteen years. If the bank calls the money back in year two, we sell the fryers?
> **Kesar:** Then where does fifteen-year money come from?
> **Dev:** From people who will wait fifteen years. There are only a few kinds of them.

Dev's rule is the first rule of this chapter: money needed for a long time must come from **long-term sources**, money needed for a few months from short-term sources. A business has three kinds of financial need: **long-term** (more than 5–10 years: land, buildings, plant, and the working capital it always needs), **medium-term** (one to five years) and **short-term** (up to one year: stock, debtors, cash).

The long-term money comes from four kinds of people. **Equity shareholders** are the owners: they get whatever is left after everyone else is paid, and they vote. **Preference shareholders** get a fixed dividend before the owners, and usually no vote. **Lenders** (debenture holders, bondholders, financial institutions, banks) get fixed interest on fixed dates, whether the factory makes money or not. And **retained earnings** are the owners' own profits, kept in the business instead of being paid out.

Each source trades the same three things against each other: what it **costs** the company, how much **risk** it puts on the company, and how much **control** it takes from the owners.

::: analogy The family that buys a delivery van
A family shop needs a van. It can bring in a partner, who shares every profit and wants a say (equity). It can take money from an uncle who wants a fixed 10% before the family takes anything, but stays out of the shop's decisions (preference). It can take a bank loan whose instalment is due on the 5th of every month, good month or bad (debt). Or it can use the savings in its own cash box (retained earnings). The partner is the most expensive in a good year and the safest in a bad one; the bank is the cheapest and the least forgiving.
:::

::: key
Long-term needs are financed from long-term sources. Equity is the costliest but carries no obligation to pay or repay, and dilutes control; debt is the cheapest because interest is tax-deductible, but its interest and repayment are obligatory and it raises financial risk; preference capital sits in between, a hybrid; retained earnings dilute nothing and carry almost no risk.
:::

## How it works

### 1. Three needs and the matching rule

ICAI groups all the financial needs of a business into three categories (SM 2.2–2.3):

1. **Long-term financial needs**: funds required for a period exceeding 5–10 years. All investments in plant, machinery, land and buildings; funds for permanent or **hard-core working capital** should also be procured from long-term sources.
2. **Medium-term financial needs**: funds required for more than one year but not more than 5 years, for example stores and spares, critical spares, tools, dies and moulds.
3. **Short-term financial needs**: funds for current assets such as stock, debtors and cash (working capital requirements), for a period not exceeding the accounting period, i.e. one year.

**Basic principle for funding various needs**: short-term needs from short-term sources, medium-term needs from medium-term sources and long-term needs from long-term sources. The method of raising funds is decided with reference to the period for which funds are required (SM 2.3).

The rule can change with the nature of the borrower, its stage of development and the nature of its business. ICAI's general pattern (SM 2.3):

| Stage | Nature of business | Sources of fund |
|---|---|---|
| Early stage | High uncertainty | Equity; mainly angel fund |
| Early stage | High to moderate uncertainty | Equity; venture capital; debt |
| Growth stage | Moderate to low uncertainty | Debt; venture capital; private equity |
| Stable stage | Low uncertainty | Debt |

The less certain the business, the more it relies on owners who can wait; the more stable it is, the more it can carry debt.

### 3. Equity share capital: the owners' money

A public limited company may raise funds from promoters or from the investing public by issuing ordinary equity shares. Their characteristics (SM 2.5–2.6):

- A source of **permanent capital**; the holders are called equity shareholders or ordinary shareholders.
- Equity shareholders are practically the **owners** of the company, as they undertake the highest risk.
- They are entitled to dividends **after the income claims of other stakeholders** are satisfied; the dividend is an **appropriation of profits, not a charge** against profits.
- In a winding up, they claim on assets after the claims of the other suppliers of capital have been met.
- The **cost of ordinary shares is usually the highest**, because these shareholders expect a higher return for the highest risk.
- Equity provides a **security to other suppliers of funds**: a lender checks that the debt-equity ratio is comfortable. Types include a new issue, rights issue, bonus shares and sweat equity.

**Advantages of raising funds by issue of equity shares** (SM 2.6–2.7):

1. It is a **permanent source of finance**. The shares are not redeemable, so the company has no liability for cash outflows on redemption; investors who want to leave sell their shares to others.
2. Equity **increases the company's financial base** and so its borrowing powers.
3. The company is **not legally obliged to pay dividends**: in uncertain times dividends can be reduced or even suspended.
4. The company can further increase its share capital by a **rights issue**.

**Disadvantages** (SM 2.7):

1. Investors find ordinary shares riskier because of uncertain dividend payments and capital gains.
2. A new issue **reduces the earning per share** of the existing shareholders until profits rise proportionately.
3. A new issue can **reduce the ownership and control** of the existing shareholders.

### 4. Preference share capital: the hybrid

Preference shares are a special kind of share whose holders enjoy **priority both as regards the payment of a fixed amount of dividend and towards repayment of capital** on winding up (SM 2.7). Their characteristics (SM 2.7–2.8):

- Long-term funds from preference shares can be raised through a public issue.
- They are normally **cumulative**: a dividend unpaid in a year of loss is carried over until there are adequate profits.
- The dividend rate is normally **higher than the rate of interest** on debentures, loans, etc.
- Most carry a stipulated period, at the end of which the funds are repaid.
- ==Preference share capital is a hybrid form of financing==: like equity, its dividend is **not a tax-deductible payment**; like debt, its **rate is fixed**.
- **Cumulative convertible preference shares (CCPs)** carry a cumulative dividend of a specified limit for a period of, say, three years, after which they convert into equity shares: attractive for projects with a long gestation period.
- Preference capital may be redeemed at a pre-decided future date or earlier, out of profits, which lets promoters withdraw capital from a company that has become self-sufficient.

| Type of preference share | Salient feature (SM 2.8) |
|---|---|
| Cumulative | Arrear dividend will accumulate |
| Non-cumulative | No right to arrear dividend |
| Redeemable | Redemption should be done |
| Participating | Can participate in the surplus remaining after payment to equity shareholders |
| Non-participating | Cannot participate in the surplus after payment of the fixed rate of dividend |
| Convertible | Option of converting into equity shares |

**Advantages of raising funds by issue of preference shares** (SM 2.8–2.9):

1. **No dilution in EPS** on an enlarged capital base (an equity issue reduces EPS and affects market perception).
2. The **advantage of leverage**, as it bears a fixed charge; non-payment of preference dividend does not force the company into liquidation (the Study Material prints "liquidity"; liquidation is the sense).
3. **No risk of takeover**: preference shareholders have no voting rights except where dividends are in arrears.
4. The dividends are fixed and pre-decided, so preference shareholders cannot share surplus profits as ordinary shareholders can, except participating preference shareholders.
5. Preference capital can be **redeemed** after a specified period.

**Disadvantages** (SM 2.9):

1. Preference dividend is **not tax-deductible**, so it gives no tax shield: preference shares cost the company more than debt such as debentures.
2. Preference dividends are **cumulative**: unpaid dividends accumulate and must be paid later, and until they are, no dividend can be paid to ordinary shareholders, which could seriously impair the company's reputation.

### 5. Retained earnings: the money already in the till

Long-term funds can be provided by accumulating the company's profits and **ploughing them back** into the business. These funds belong to the ordinary shareholders and increase the net worth of the company. A public limited company must plough back a reasonable amount of profit every year, keeping in view the legal requirements and its own expansion plans. Such funds **entail almost no risk**, and the control of present owners is **not diluted** (SM 2.10).

Whether to plough back depends on the **rate of return the company generates against the expected cost of equity** (SM 2.10): the subject of [[fm.div.models|the dividend theories]].

### 6. Debentures: the borrowed money with a trust deed

Public limited companies can raise loans from the public by issuing debentures or bonds. Their characteristics (SM 2.10):

- Normally issued in denominations from ₹100 to ₹1,000, at different rates of interest.
- Normally issued on the basis of a **debenture trust deed**, which lists the terms and conditions of the issue.
- Basically instruments for raising **long-term debt capital**.
- Maturity normally varies from **3 to 10 years**, longer for projects with a high gestation period.
- Either **secured or unsecured**; they may or may not be listed on a stock exchange.
- The **cost is quite low**, since interest is charged as an expense before tax.
- For investors, more attractive than preference shares, since **interest is payable whether or not the company makes profits**.

**By convertibility** (SM 2.11):

1. **Non-convertible debentures**: no conversion feature; repayable on maturity.
2. **Fully convertible debentures**: converted into equity shares on the terms of issue as to price and time; interest is generally lower than on non-convertible debentures because of the attractive conversion feature.
3. **Partly convertible debentures**: carry features of both, in one debenture.

| Other type of debenture | Salient feature (SM 2.11) |
|---|---|
| Bearer | Transferable like negotiable instruments |
| Registered | Interest payable to the registered person |
| Mortgage | Secured by a charge on asset(s) |
| Naked or simple | Unsecured |
| Redeemable | Repaid after a certain period |
| Non-redeemable | Not repayable |

**Advantages of raising finance by issue of debentures** (SM 2.11):

1. The **cost is much lower** than the cost of preference or equity capital, as the interest is tax-deductible; investors also consider debentures safer and may accept a lower return.
2. Debenture financing **does not result in dilution of control**.
3. In a **period of rising prices**, a debenture issue is advantageous: the fixed monetary outgo decreases in real terms as the price level increases.

**Disadvantages of debenture financing** (SM 2.12):

1. Debenture interest and the repayment of principal are **obligatory payments**.
2. The **protective covenants** of a debenture issue may be restrictive.
3. Debenture financing **enhances the financial risk** of the firm, because of point 1.
4. Repayment at maturity needs a **large cash outflow** at that time.

A public issue of debentures, and private placement to mutual funds, must be **rated by a credit rating agency** such as CRISIL. The rating weighs the company's track record, profitability, debt servicing capacity, creditworthiness and the perceived risk of lending (SM 2.12).

### 7. Bonds

A **bond** is a fixed income security created to raise funds, through a public issue or a private placement (SM 2.13). Based on call:

- **Callable bonds**: a call option gives the **issuer** the right to redeem the bond before maturity at a predetermined price, the **call price** (generally at a premium).
- **Puttable bonds**: a put option gives the **investor** the right to sell the bond back to the company before maturity.

**Indian bonds** (SM 2.15–2.16):

| Bond | Salient feature |
|---|---|
| Masala bond | "Masala" means spice: an Indian name for a **rupee-denominated** bond that Indian corporate borrowers sell to investors in overseas markets; issued outside India but denominated in Indian rupees. NTPC raised ₹2,000 crore through masala bonds in 2016 |
| Municipal bonds | Used to finance urban infrastructure. Ahmedabad Municipal Corporation issued a first historical municipal bond in Asia, raising ₹100 crore for part financing a water supply project |
| Government or treasury bonds | Issued by the Government of India, the Reserve Bank of India, any state government or any other government department |

The foreign bonds of SM 2.13–2.15 (FCCB, plain vanilla, drop lock and the rest) are in [[fm.sources.modern|contemporary and international sources]]; deep discount, zero coupon, inflation and junk bonds in [[fm.sources.other|other sources of finance]].

### 8. Loans from financial institutions and commercial banks

**Financial institutions** (SM 2.16–2.17). National: IFCI (converted into a public company), the State Financial Corporations, IDBI (converted into a bank), NIDC, ICICI (converted into a bank and privatised), LIC, UTI and IRBI. International: the World Bank/International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (IBRD, 1944), the International Finance Corporation (IFC, 1956) and the Asian Development Bank (ADB, 1966).

**Commercial banks** primarily cater to the short-term requirements of industry, but have taken an interest in long-term financing in several ways (SM 2.17):

- (a) **Long-term loans** for expansion or setting up new units, repayable over a long period; their liquidity depends on the anticipated income of the borrowers.
- (b) A **working capital term loan (WCTL)**: funding of the portion of working capital that is always required (the minimum level) and is not affected by seasonal requirements.

**Bridge finance** refers to loans taken by a company, normally from commercial banks, for a short period because of **pending disbursement of loans sanctioned by financial institutions**. Though short-term, it is discussed with long-term sources because it is an important step in the facilitation of long-term loans: once term-lending institutions approve a loan, companies arrange short-term bank loans so as not to lose time in starting their projects (SM 2.17–2.18).

## Worked examples

:::: example The samosa factory: shares, preference or debentures?
Tapri Tea Co. Ltd needs ₹100 crore for its Dewas samosa factory, which will run for about fifteen years. It has 10 crore equity shares, of which the Mehra family holds 5.20 crore. Its cash flows have been stable for five years, its debt-equity ratio is low, and the tax rate is 25%. Dev has three offers:

(a) a public issue of equity shares at ₹250 each;

(b) 10.5% cumulative redeemable preference shares;

(c) 10% secured non-convertible debentures, redeemable after 7 years, rated by a credit rating agency.

Kesar's only conditions: "The family keeps control, and the taxman helps pay for it." Advise Dev, with reasons, on the most suitable source.

::: answer Model answer
**Relevant points.** Equity is a permanent source with no obligation to pay dividends, but it is usually the costliest source and a new issue may reduce the EPS and the ownership and control of existing shareholders. Preference shares cause no dilution in EPS and no risk of takeover, since their holders have no voting rights unless dividends are in arrears, but their dividend is not tax-deductible, so they are costlier than debt. Debentures cost the least because interest is tax-deductible and do not dilute control, but their interest and principal are obligatory, they enhance financial risk, and repayment needs a large cash outflow at maturity.

**Facts of the case.**

| Source | Net cost a year (₹ crore) | Family holding |
|---|---:|---:|
| Equity | Highest | 50% |
| Preference | 10.50 | 52% |
| Debentures | 10.00 − 2.50 = **7.50** | 52% |

Working: the equity dividend is not obligatory, but equity carries the highest expected return. New shares = ₹100 crore ÷ ₹250 = 40 lakh; family holding = 5.20 crore ÷ 10.40 crore = 50%. Tax saved on interest = ₹10 crore × 25% = ₹2.50 crore.

**Analysis.**

1. *Control*: the equity issue takes the family from 52% to exactly 50%, no longer a majority. Preference shares and debentures leave it at 52%.
2. *Cost*: the debentures cost 10% × (1 − 0.25) = 7.5% after tax, against 10.5% for preference shares, whose dividend gets no tax shield.
3. *Risk*: stable cash flows and a low debt-equity ratio mean Tapri can carry the obligatory interest and plan for the large cash outflow at redemption in year 7.

**Conclusion.** Tapri should issue the **10% secured debentures**: they meet both conditions, being the cheapest source after tax without diluting control. If the board wanted to avoid an obligatory payment altogether, the preference shares would be the next choice; the equity issue fails the control condition.
:::
::::

:::: example Dev's term sheets: name the instrument
Dev's file holds five term sheets. Identify the instrument or source in each, with a reason.

(a) Tapri may redeem the bond after four years at ₹102 for every ₹100 of face value.

(b) The investor may sell the bond back to Tapri at par at the end of year 3.

(c) A ₹500 crore issue in London and Singapore, denominated in Indian rupees.

(d) A 9% dividend, arrears carried forward, converted into equity shares after three years, for the slow-to-mature tea plantation in Assam.

(e) A ₹30 crore loan from Tapri's bank for four months, because the term loan sanctioned by a financial institution has not yet been disbursed; secured by hypothecation of the delivery vans and Kesar's personal guarantee.

::: answer Model answer
**(a) Callable bond.** The call option gives the issuer the right to redeem the bond before maturity at a predetermined call price, generally at a premium (here ₹102).

**(b) Puttable bond.** The put option gives the investor the right to sell the bond back to the company before maturity.

**(c) Masala bond.** A rupee-denominated bond issued outside India by an Indian corporate borrower to investors in overseas markets.

**(d) Cumulative convertible preference shares (CCPs).** They carry a cumulative dividend of a specified limit for a period, say three years, after which they convert into equity shares; they suit projects with a long gestation period.

**(e) Bridge finance.** A short-period loan, normally from a commercial bank, taken because the disbursement of a loan sanctioned by a financial institution is pending. It is normally secured by hypothecating movable assets, personal guarantees and demand promissory notes, carries a higher interest rate than the term loan, and is repaid or adjusted out of the term loan when disbursed.
:::

## Under the hood

### The features of a debenture, from the cost of capital chapter

ICAI's suggested answer to "Explain any four features of debentures or bonds" (MTP May 2025 Series II Q4(b)) took its points from the cost of capital chapter: **face value** (interest is calculated on it), **interest (coupon) rate** (fixed, except for zero coupon and deep discount bonds), **maturity period** (fixed for redemption; infinite for irredeemable debentures), **redemption value** (may differ from face value) and the **benefit of tax shield** (SM 4.5–4.6). They are used in [[fm.coc.debt|the cost of debt]].

### A dividend is an appropriation, interest is a charge

This one line explains most of the chapter. Interest is deducted before tax, so each rupee of it costs the company only (1 − tax rate). A dividend, equity or preference, is paid out of profit after tax, so it saves no tax. That is why debt is the cheapest source and equity the dearest, and why a preference share, with a fixed rate like debt and no tax shield like equity, is called a hybrid.

### The years in the institutions table

The Study Material's table of national financial institutions also gives a year of establishment for each (SM 2.16–2.17). None of the ICAI questions read for this lesson asked for them: learn the names and the remarks (converted into a public company, converted into a bank, privatised), and leave the years alone.

::: exam The wording traps
- "Most expensive" is equity, even though it has no obligatory payment; "most risky to the company" is debt.
- "Hybrid" is preference capital, and ICAI gives the reasons: dividend not tax-deductible (like equity), fixed rate (like debt).
- A callable bond's option belongs to the **issuer**, a puttable bond's to the **investor**.
- Bridge finance is short-term but is taught with long-term sources: write why.
:::

::: trick C-R-C for any source
For any "advantages and disadvantages" question, run the source through **Cost** (tax-deductible or not), **Risk** (obligatory payment and repayment, or not) and **Control** (voting dilution, or not), then add its own special points (rights issue for equity; covenants and rising prices for debentures). It recovers ICAI's lists for equity, preference and debentures; it does not replace them.
:::

## Answer frame

### "Distinguish between preference shares and debentures" (SM 2.12)

::: compare
| Basis | Preference shares | Debentures |
|---|---|---|

| Payment of dividend / interest | Priority as regards a fixed dividend and repayment of capital on winding up | Carries a fixed percentage of interest |
| Nature | A hybrid form of financing, with some characteristics of equity and some of debt | An instrument for raising long-term capital with a fixed period of maturity |

| Dividend payment | Paid after preference dividend | Preferred over equity dividend |
| Rate of dividend | Fluctuating | Fixed |
| Convertibility | Not convertible | Convertible |

### "Discuss the advantages of equity shares" and its cousins

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: the factory on an overdraft
Funding plant, buildings or hard-core working capital from short-term borrowings because they are quick to arrange. **Fix:** the basic principle matches the period: long-term needs from long-term sources. An overdraft is repayable on demand.
:::

::: mistake "Retained earnings are free money"
They carry almost no risk and dilute no control, but they belong to the equity shareholders, who could have had them as dividends. **Fix:** the decision to plough back depends on the return the company generates against the expected cost of equity (SM 2.10).
:::

::: mistake Callable and puttable swapped
Writing that a callable bond lets the investor demand early repayment. **Fix:** call = the issuer redeems early (at the call price, generally a premium); put = the investor sells it back.
:::

::: mistake Preference dividend shown as a tax saving
Treating preference dividend like interest. **Fix:** preference dividend, like equity dividend, is not tax-deductible; only debt interest gives a tax shield.
:::

Q: Case scenario. Chai Express Logistics Ltd, Tapri's subsidiary, plans ₹40 crore of 9% cumulative redeemable preference shares for its refrigerated trucks. Its finance manager's slide makes four claims. Which one is correct?
- [ ] The preference dividend will cut the tax bill, just as debenture interest would
- [ ] If the dividend is skipped in a loss year, an equity dividend can still be paid next year without clearing the arrears
- [ ] Preference shares can never be redeemed, so the money is permanent
- [x] The preference shareholders will have no vote, except where their dividend is in arrears
> No voting rights except where dividends are in arrears (SM 2.9). The dividend is not tax-deductible, so there is no tax shield; cumulative arrears must be paid before any equity dividend; and these shares are redeemable after a specified period.

Q: Which of the following is NOT a disadvantage of debenture financing listed by ICAI?
- [ ] Interest and repayment of principal are obligatory payments
- [ ] Protective covenants may be restrictive
- [x] It dilutes the control of existing shareholders
- [ ] A large cash outflow is needed at maturity
> Debentures do not dilute control: that is one of their advantages. The other three are on ICAI's list, with the enhanced financial risk.

Q: Tapri issues 50,000 debentures of ₹1,000 each at 8% interest. The tax rate is 25%. What is the annual interest cost to Tapri after tax, in rupees?
= 3000000
> Interest = 50,000 × ₹1,000 × 8% = ₹40,00,000. It is tax-deductible, so the cost after tax is ₹40,00,000 × (1 − 0.25) = ₹30,00,000.

- [ ] Callable bond
- [x] Puttable bond
- [ ] Masala bond
- [ ] Municipal bond
> The put option (the right to sell) belongs to the investor. A callable bond's option belongs to the issuer.

- [x] It is normally secured by hypothecation of movable assets, personal guarantees and demand promissory notes

- [x] It is repaid or adjusted out of the term loan when disbursed

## Recap

- Fund each need from a source of matching period: long-term (more than 5–10 years), medium-term (1–5 years), short-term (up to one year).
- Three classifications: basic sources (external, internal), maturity of payment, ownership and control (owner's fund, borrowed fund).
- Equity: permanent, owners, residual claim, costliest; advantages: permanent, financial base, no legal obligation to pay dividends, rights issue; disadvantages: risky to investors, EPS dilution, control dilution.
- Preference: priority in fixed dividend and capital; hybrid (dividend not tax-deductible, rate fixed); usually cumulative; six types; no EPS dilution, leverage, no takeover risk.
- Retained earnings: ploughed-back profits of equity shareholders; almost no risk, no dilution; retain if the company's return beats the cost of equity.
- Debentures: trust deed, 3–10 years, secured or unsecured, rated; cheapest (interest tax-deductible), no dilution, good in rising prices; but obligatory, covenants, financial risk, lump-sum repayment.
- Callable: issuer may redeem early at the call price; puttable: investor may sell back. Masala: rupee bond sold abroad.
- Bridge finance: short bank loan pending disbursement of a sanctioned term loan; secured by hypothecation, guarantees, promissory notes; costlier; repaid out of the term loan. (No fryers were sold.)

## Flashcards

Q:: What is the basic principle for funding various financial needs?
A:: Short-term needs from short-term sources, medium-term needs from medium-term sources and long-term needs from long-term sources; the method of raising funds is decided by the period for which the funds are required.

Q:: State four advantages of raising funds by issue of equity shares.
A:: (i) A permanent source: no liability for redemption; (ii) increases the financial base and borrowing powers; (iii) no legal obligation to pay dividends; (iv) share capital can be further increased by a rights issue.

Q:: State three disadvantages of raising funds by equity shares.
A:: (i) Investors find them riskier (uncertain dividends and capital gains); (ii) a new issue reduces the EPS of existing shareholders until profits rise proportionately; (iii) a new issue can reduce the ownership and control of existing shareholders.

Q:: Why is preference share capital called a hybrid form of financing?
A:: Like equity, its dividend is not a tax-deductible payment; like debt, its rate of dividend is fixed.

Q:: Name the six types of preference shares in the Study Material.
A:: Cumulative, non-cumulative, redeemable, participating, non-participating and convertible.

Q:: State the two disadvantages of preference shares.
A:: (i) The dividend is not tax-deductible, so no tax shield: costlier than debt; (ii) dividends are cumulative, and until arrears are paid no dividend can be paid to equity shareholders, which could impair the company's reputation.

Q:: State three advantages of raising finance by debentures.
A:: (i) Cost much lower than preference or equity, as interest is tax-deductible and investors accept a lower return; (ii) no dilution of control; (iii) advantageous in a period of rising prices, as the fixed monetary outgo falls in real terms.

Q:: State four disadvantages of debenture financing.
A:: (i) Interest and principal are obligatory payments; (ii) protective covenants may be restrictive; (iii) it enhances the financial risk of the firm; (iv) a large cash outflow is needed at maturity.

Q:: Callable bond versus puttable bond?
A:: Callable: the issuer has the right to redeem before maturity at a predetermined call price (generally at a premium). Puttable: the investor has the right to sell the bond back to the company before maturity.

Q:: What is a masala bond?
A:: A rupee-denominated bond that Indian corporate borrowers sell to investors in overseas markets: issued outside India but denominated in Indian rupees.

Q:: State the key features of bridge finance.
A:: (i) A short-period loan, normally from commercial banks, pending disbursement of loans sanctioned by financial institutions; (ii) repaid or adjusted out of the term loans when disbursed; (iii) normally secured by hypothecating movable assets, personal guarantees and demand promissory notes; (iv) interest generally higher than on term loans.

Q:: What is a working capital term loan (WCTL)?
A:: Long-term bank funding of the portion of working capital that is always required (the minimum level) and is not affected by seasonal requirements.
