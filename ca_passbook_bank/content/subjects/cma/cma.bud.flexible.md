## In plain words

### The café manager who "saved" ₹18,000

Tapri's Pune café budgeted ₹1,70,000 of costs for March, for 20,000 cups. Then a water pipe burst, the café shut for ten days, and it sold only 15,000 cups. March's costs came to ₹1,52,000.

> **Kesar:** ₹18,000 under budget! Give the manager a bonus.
> **Ritu:** Per unit, please. She sold 5,000 fewer cups. The milk, tea and kulhads for those cups were never bought.
> **Kesar:** So did she save money or not?
> **Ritu:** Ask the budget she should have had for 15,000 cups.

Ritu splits the café's costs by behaviour. Rent and salaries are **fixed**: ₹60,000 whatever the cups. Milk, tea and kulhads are **variable**: ₹4 a cup. Electricity is **semi-variable**: ₹10,000 for the lights and fridges plus ₹1 a cup for the boilers. For 15,000 cups the café should have spent ₹60,000 + ₹10,000 + 15,000 × ₹5 = ₹1,45,000. It spent ₹1,52,000: ₹7,000 **more** than it should have.

The ₹1,70,000 was a **fixed budget**: prepared for one level of activity and left unchanged whatever level is actually reached. The ₹1,45,000 is a **flexible budget**: the same plan recast for the activity actually attained, because it knows which costs move with volume and which do not. Compared with the fixed budget the manager looks thrifty; compared with the flexible budget she overspent.

::: analogy The road-trip fuel budget
You budget ₹5,000 for fuel and ₹500 for tolls for a 500-km trip, then turn back after 300 km having spent ₹3,500 on fuel. "₹1,500 saved"? Fuel at ₹10 a km allowed ₹3,000 for 300 km: you overspent ₹500, perhaps on the hills. The toll was the same either way. A flexible budget is the fuel allowance for the distance you actually drove.
:::

::: key
A fixed budget is for one level of activity and does not change; a flexible budget recognises fixed, semi-variable and variable costs and changes with the level of activity attained. Judge actual costs only against the flexible budget for the actual level.
:::

## How it works

### Fixed budget: one level, no change

A **fixed budget** is prepared on the basis of a standard or fixed level of activity and does not change with a change in the level of activities; it shows the expected results of a responsibility centre for only one activity level, and is also known as a **static budget** (SM 15.19–15.20). Fixed budgeting is used by many service companies and for some administrative functions of manufacturing companies, such as purchasing, engineering and accounting. It is suitable for fixed expenses; when the level attained differs from the budgeted level, it becomes ineffective as a tool of cost control.

**Essential conditions** for a fixed budget (SM 15.20): (1) the nature of business is not seasonal; (2) there is no impact of external factors on business activities; (3) demand for the product is certain and stable; (4) supply orders are received and issued regularly; (5) the market is normally domestic (or regular service exports); (6) no special labour or material is needed in production; (7) the supply of production inputs is regular; (8) there is a trend of price stability. These conditions are generally not all found in practice, hence a fixed budget is not suitable in business concerns.

| Fixed budget: merits | Fixed budget: demerits |
|---|---|
| 1. Very simple to understand | 1. Does not suit a dynamic organisation and may give misleading results: a poor or good performance may remain unnoticed |
| 2. Less time consuming | 2. Not suitable for a long period |
| | 3. Unsuitable when business conditions are changing constantly |
| | 4. Accurate estimates are not possible |

### Flexible budget: a series of fixed budgets

::: definition Flexible budget
A budget which, by recognising the difference in behaviour between fixed and variable costs in relation to fluctuations in output, turnover, or other variable factors, is designed to change appropriately with such fluctuations. CIMA: "a budget which, by recognizing the difference between fixed, semi-variable and variable costs is designed to change in relation to the level of activity attained." (SM 15.21)
:::

Unlike a static budget, a flexible budget shows the expected results of a responsibility centre for different activity levels: it can be viewed as ==a series of static budgets for different levels of activity==. It is especially useful in estimating and controlling factory costs and operating expenses, and more realistic because it considers how revenue and cost behave at different levels (SM 15.21). To prepare one, expenses are classified as (i) fixed, (ii) variable and (iii) semi-variable, and the semi-variable expenses are further segregated into fixed and variable parts ([[cma.intro.behaviour|cost behaviour]]).

Flexible budgeting may be resorted to (SM 15.21): (i) in a new business venture, where demand is hard to forecast accurately; (ii) where the business depends on the fluctuations of nature (a wool trader sells plenty below freezing point and much less in warm weather); (iii) in a labour-intensive industry where production depends on the availability of labour.

| Flexible budget: merits (SM 15.22) | Flexible budget: demerits (SM 15.22) |
|---|---|
| 1. Sales, costs and profit can be calculated easily at various levels of capacity | 1. Possible only with a proper accounting system and perfect knowledge of the factors of production and business circumstances |
| 2. Adjustment to changed business conditions is very simple | 2. Requires a system of standard costing |
| 3. Helps decide the production level, showing budgeted costs and sales at each level, so management can pick the level that earns the profit predetermined by the owners | 3. Very expensive and labour oriented |
| 4. Shows the quantity to be produced to earn a determined profit | |

The split of a semi-variable cost uses the **high and low points method** (SM 15.28): the variable cost per unit is the change in the expense divided by the change in output, and the fixed part is what remains of the total at either level. ## Formats and formulas

*Reproduce the format from memory; ICAI's suggested answers show every level side by side, the fixed portions worked out in notes.*

::: formula Flexible budget allowance
$$\text{Allowance} = F + v \times A$$
F = fixed cost (including the fixed part of semi-variable costs); v = variable cost per unit of activity; A = activity attained, in units, hours, or sales value (for costs given as a percentage of sales).
:::

::: formula High and low points method
$$v = \frac{\text{Change in expense}}{\text{Change in output}} \qquad F = \text{Total expense} - v \times \text{Output}$$
v = variable cost per unit; F = fixed cost. Use two levels at which the fixed cost is the same; the fixed part comes out equal at both.
:::

::: definition Fixed budget
CIMA: "a budget designed to remain unchanged irrespective of the level of activity actually attained." (SM 15.19)
:::

| Flexible budget for … | 60% | 80% | 100% |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Output (units) | xxx | xxx | xxx |
| **Sales (A)** | **xxx** | **xxx** | **xxx** |
| Variable costs, item by item | xxx | xxx | xxx |
| Variable part of semi-variable costs | xxx | xxx | xxx |
| **Total variable cost (B)** | **xxx** | **xxx** | **xxx** |
| **Contribution (A − B)** | **xxx** | **xxx** | **xxx** |
| Fixed costs, item by item | xxx | xxx | xxx |
| Fixed part of semi-variable costs | xxx | xxx | xxx |
| Step costs at the level | xxx | xxx | xxx |
| **Total fixed cost (C)** | **xxx** | **xxx** | **xxx** |
| **Profit (A − B − C)** | **xxx** | **xxx** | **xxx** |

When only costs are asked ("flexible budget showing the total cost"), stop at total cost (B + C); ICAI often adds the cost per unit or per hour at each level.

## Worked examples

:::: example Cold brew at 60%, 80% or 100%
Tapri's cold-brew plant can bottle 1,00,000 bottles a year. It works at 60% (60,000 bottles), selling at ₹120 a bottle. Cost per bottle at 60%:

| Item | ₹ per bottle |
|---|---:|
| Direct material | 40 |
| Direct labour | 15 |
| Factory overhead (40% fixed) | 20 |
| Administration and selling overhead (60% fixed) | 15 |

Bottles go out in hired trucks at ₹9,000 a trip, 3,000 bottles a truck; a part load costs a full trip. Kesar wants to run at 80% or 100%, and all output can be sold, but:

- at 80%, the price falls by 4% and material costs rise by 3% (spot purchases);
- at 100%, the price falls by 6% and an annual contract cuts material costs by 5%;
- above 90%, a night-shift supervisor costing ₹1,50,000 a year is needed.

**Required:**

(i) Prepare a flexible budget showing the profit at 60%, 80% and 100% capacity.

(ii) Comment on the capacity at which the plant should work.

::: answer Show the working
**Working notes**

1. Fixed factory overhead = ₹20 × 40% × 60,000 = **₹4,80,000**; variable part ₹12 a bottle.
2. Fixed administration and selling overhead = ₹15 × 60% × 60,000 = **₹5,40,000**; variable part ₹6 a bottle.
3. Trucks (semi-variable, in steps): 60,000 ÷ 3,000 = 20 trips; 80,000 ÷ 3,000 = 26.67, so 27 trips; 1,00,000 ÷ 3,000 = 33.33, so 34 trips.
4. Price: ₹120; ₹120 × 96% = ₹115.20; ₹120 × 94% = ₹112.80. Material: ₹40; ₹40 × 103% = ₹41.20; ₹40 × 95% = ₹38.

**(i) Flexible budget**

| Particulars | 60% (₹) | 80% (₹) | 100% (₹) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Bottles | 60,000 | 80,000 | 1,00,000 |
| **Sales (A)** | **72,00,000** | **92,16,000** | **1,12,80,000** |
| Direct material | 24,00,000 | 32,96,000 | 38,00,000 |
| Direct labour (₹15) | 9,00,000 | 12,00,000 | 15,00,000 |
| Variable factory overhead (₹12) | 7,20,000 | 9,60,000 | 12,00,000 |
| Variable admin and selling (₹6) | 3,60,000 | 4,80,000 | 6,00,000 |
| **Total variable cost (B)** | **43,80,000** | **59,36,000** | **71,00,000** |
| **Contribution (A − B)** | **28,20,000** | **32,80,000** | **41,80,000** |
| Fixed factory overhead (WN 1) | 4,80,000 | 4,80,000 | 4,80,000 |
| Fixed admin and selling (WN 2) | 5,40,000 | 5,40,000 | 5,40,000 |
| Trucks (WN 3) | 1,80,000 | 2,43,000 | 3,06,000 |
| Night-shift supervisor | – | – | 1,50,000 |
| **Fixed and step costs (C)** | **12,00,000** | **12,63,000** | **14,76,000** |
| **Profit (A − B − C)** | **16,20,000** | **20,17,000** | **27,04,000** |
| Total cost per bottle (B + C) | 93.00 | 89.99 | 85.76 |

**(ii) Comment.** Profit is highest at **100%**: ₹27,04,000, against ₹16,20,000 at present. The price cut costs ₹7.20 a bottle, but the material contract saves ₹2 a bottle and the extra 40,000 bottles add contribution far above the extra ₹2,76,000 of trucks and supervision. 80% is better than 60% but worse than 100%, because spot buying raises material cost exactly when the price falls. The plant should work at 100%, provided the market really takes 1,00,000 bottles at ₹112.80.

The fixed portions are taken at the 60% level and held constant, as in ICAI's suggested answer to May 2024 Q3(a); a fixed cost that changes with capacity is shown only where the question says so.

## Under the hood

### The flexible budget at standard cost

A flexible budget can also be drawn up at **standard cost** for the actual output and set beside the master budget and the actual results, item by item: the master budget for the planned output, the flexible budget for the actual output, the actual costs, and the variance between the last two (the chapter's Practical Problem 6, SM 15.79 and 15.92). The fixed overhead expenditure variance is then budgeted fixed overhead minus actual fixed overhead. The variance analysis itself is in [[cma.std.voh|variable overhead variances]], [[cma.std.foh|fixed overhead variances]] and [[cma.std.recon|reconciling budgeted and actual profit]].

### Step costs: the bus problem

ICAI's favourite flexible budget outside the factory is an outing: students or trainees, buses or batches with a maximum capacity, and costs per student, per bus and per trip. Costs per head are variable; costs per bus are semi-variable in steps (buses = students ÷ seats, rounded **up**); a lump sum is fixed. The average cost per head falls as numbers rise, but jumps whenever a new bus is hired. May 2025 Q5(c) (seats reserved for teachers, and block fees that change with the group size), MTP May 2024 Series II Q3(a) and the case scenario in MTP May 2026 Series II all use it.

### Revenue budgets are flexible too

The chapter's case scenario prepares a public sector undertaking's **revenue budget** as a flexible budget: sales volume grown by a percentage, raw material, power and maintenance moved with volume, wages linked to a wage agreement and an index, prices raised only if a price index crosses its trigger (SM 15.81–15.83, 15.96–15.98). The method is the same: decide what each item moves with, then move it.

### "In the absence of information"

When the capacity rises beyond the present level and the question says nothing, ICAI assumes the fixed costs stay the same, and says so in a note: "However, in practice some of these costs may change if present capacity is exceeded" (Illustration 2, SM 15.26). Write the same assumption in your answer.

::: exam The traps ICAI sets
- A transport, bus or batch cost per vehicle is a step cost: round the number of vehicles **up** (unless the loads divide exactly, as in September 2024, where it became a variable ₹1 a unit).
- A fixed cost that starts "above 80%" or "beyond 90%" is added only at the levels above it.
- The fixed portion of a semi-variable cost is worked out at the base level and held constant; percentage increases are applied to the variable rate and to the fixed total separately.
- "Profit 20% on sales" is 25% on cost.
- Compare actual cost with the flexible budget for the **actual** level; a fixed budget shows "savings" that are only lower volume.
:::

::: trick Fixed cost survives, variable cost travels
Whatever the level: fixed cost stays as a total, variable cost moves as a rate × level. So total cost per unit always falls as the level rises (until a step cost or a price change intervenes). Condition: costs linear within the range, with step and price changes added separately.
:::

## Answer frame

### "Distinguish between fixed and flexible budgets"

ICAI's five points, in order (SM 15.22–15.23):

| Point | Fixed budget | Flexible budget |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Change | Does not change with the actual volume achieved: rigid or inflexible | Can be re-cast for the activity level achieved: not rigid |
| 2. Levels | One level of activity, one set of conditions; assumes no change, which is unrealistic | Various budgets for different levels of activity |
| 3. Variance analysis | All costs related to one level, so it does not give useful information | Each cost analysed by behaviour, so it gives useful information |
| 4. Cost and price | Cost ascertainment and price fixation mislead when budgeted and actual levels differ significantly | Facilitates cost ascertainment, price fixation and quotations |
| 5. Comparison | Comparing actual with budget is meaningless when activity levels differ | A meaningful basis of comparison of actual with budget |

### "Explain briefly the concept of flexible budget"

1. The definition (the behaviour of fixed and variable costs; CIMA's wording with semi-variable costs).
2. Unlike a static budget: results for different activity levels; a series of static budgets.
3. Costs classified as fixed, variable and semi-variable, the last split into fixed and variable.
4. Where it is used: new ventures, businesses dependent on nature, labour-intensive industries.
5. A three-line numerical illustration (the café).

### "Which budgeting system would you recommend?" (actuals far above a fixed budget)

ICAI's suggested answer (MTP September 2025 Series II Q6(b)): recommend a **flexible budget**; define it (both definitions); state that unlike static budgets it shows expected results for different activity levels; then list its advantages: sales, costs and profit calculated easily at various levels; simple adjustment to changes in business conditions; helps select the production level that earns the predetermined profit; shows the quantity to be produced to earn a determined profit.

### "Suitability for flexible budget" (SM 15.21–15.22)

1. Seasonal fluctuations in sales and/or production, for example the soft drinks industry.
2. A company which keeps introducing new products or changes the design of its products frequently.
3. Industries engaged in make-to-order business, like ship building.
4. An industry influenced by changes in fashion.
5. General changes in sales.

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: the incentive lost to a festival rush
Comparing April's actual ₹1,94,300 with the ₹1,78,000 provided for 20,000 meals and calling the ₹16,300 difference an overspend. **Fix:** flex the budget to the 26,000 meals actually made (₹1,96,600); the kitchen stayed ₹2,300 within it. The same error works the other way: when output falls, a fixed budget shows "savings" that are only lower volume.
:::

::: mistake The budget rate per unit carried to another level
₹8.50 a cup at 20,000 cups applied to 15,000 cups gives ₹1,27,500. **Fix:** fixed costs do not shrink: allowance = fixed ₹70,000 + ₹5 × 15,000 = ₹1,45,000.
:::

::: mistake Semi-variable costs treated as wholly variable or wholly fixed
Treating ₹20 of factory overhead as all variable (or all fixed) at every level. **Fix:** split it first, by the percentage the question gives or by the high and low points method; flex only the variable part.
:::

::: mistake 26.67 trucks
A fraction of a truck, a bus or a batch. **Fix:** vehicles and batches are hired whole: round up, and cost the step.
:::

## Recap

- Fixed (static) budget: one level of activity, unchanged whatever is attained; for stable, non-seasonal businesses and fixed expenses.
- Flexible budget (CIMA): recognises fixed, semi-variable and variable costs and changes with the level of activity attained; a series of static budgets.
- Budgeted cost = fixed cost + variable cost per unit × activity; split semi-variables first (high and low points: change in expense ÷ change in output).
- Add the question's steps: price changes at a level, extra fixed costs beyond a capacity, vehicles or batches rounded up.
- Judge performance against the flexible budget for the actual level: a fixed budget's "saving" may be only lower volume.
- Never carry a cost per unit from one level to another: fixed costs do not shrink.
- Fixed vs flexible: change, levels, variance analysis, cost and price, comparison.
- Suitable where sales are seasonal, products or designs keep changing, work is made to order, fashion rules, or sales change generally. (Fuel for the distance you actually drove.)

## Flashcards

Q:: CIMA's definition of a fixed budget?
A:: A budget designed to remain unchanged irrespective of the level of activity actually attained.

Q:: CIMA's definition of a flexible budget?
A:: A budget which, by recognizing the difference between fixed, semi-variable and variable costs, is designed to change in relation to the level of activity attained.

Q:: Flexible budget allowance at the actual level?
A:: Fixed cost + variable cost per unit × actual activity (semi-variable costs split into their fixed and variable parts first).

Q:: High and low points method: the two formulas?
A:: Variable cost per unit = change in expense ÷ change in output; fixed cost = total expense − variable cost per unit × output.

Q:: In which three situations may flexible budgeting be resorted to?
A:: A new business venture whose demand is hard to forecast; a business dependent on the fluctuations of nature; a labour-intensive industry whose production depends on the availability of labour.

Q:: Five cases ICAI lists as suitable for a flexible budget?
A:: Seasonal fluctuations in sales or production; frequent new products or design changes; make-to-order industries like ship building; industries influenced by fashion; general changes in sales.

Q:: Three demerits of a flexible budget?
A:: It needs a proper accounting system and perfect knowledge of the factors of production and business circumstances; it requires a standard costing system; it is very expensive and labour oriented.

Q:: Merits and demerits of a fixed budget?
A:: Merits: very simple to understand, less time consuming. Demerits: misleading in a dynamic organisation; unsuitable for a long period; unsuitable when conditions change constantly; accurate estimates not possible.

Q:: Fixed vs flexible budget: the five points of difference?
A:: Change (rigid vs re-cast); levels (one vs several); variance analysis (not useful vs useful); cost ascertainment and pricing (misleading vs facilitated); comparison of actual with budget (meaningless when levels differ vs meaningful).

Q:: Why is applying the budgeted cost per unit to a different activity level wrong?
A:: Because fixed costs do not change with activity: the cost per unit falls as activity rises, so the allowance must be fixed cost + variable rate × actual activity.

Q:: How are buses, trucks or batches treated in a flexible budget?
A:: As step (semi-variable) costs: the number needed is rounded up at each level and costed whole.
