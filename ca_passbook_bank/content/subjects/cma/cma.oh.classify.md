## In plain words

### The ₹15.78 lakh heap called "miscellaneous"

Tapri's samosa factory in Indore spent ₹15.78 lakh last month on things that are not potatoes, flour or the wages of the people shaping samosas.

> **Kesar:** Rent, gas, the supervisor, the canteen... put it all under "miscellaneous". Sab adjust ho jayega.
> **Ritu:** Per unit, please. How much of this heap belongs to one pack of samosas? "Miscellaneous" cannot tell me.

The potatoes in a pack can be weighed and charged to that pack. The factory rent cannot: no pack "uses" ₹3 of rent that anyone can point to. Costs like the rent, the supervisor's salary and the fryer lubricant are **overheads**: costs that cannot be conveniently traced to any one unit of output, because they are incurred for output in general.

Ritu sorts the heap twice.

First, **by kind**: what the cost is for (the factory, the office, selling or delivery), how it moves with output (fixed, variable or in between), what it is made of (indirect material, indirect employee cost, indirect expenses) and whether a manager can control it.

Second, **by place**: she gives each cost to a department. A cost that belongs wholly to one department goes there in full: the gas read from the Frying department's own meter is **allocated** to Frying. A cost that several departments share is split in fair proportions: the factory rent is **apportioned** by the floor area each department occupies. Then the costs of the departments that only help (Stores, Maintenance) are passed on to the departments that make the product (**re-apportionment**), and finally each production department's total is charged to the packs it makes (**absorption**).

::: analogy The flat-share bills
Four friends share a flat. Each one's mobile bill is that person's alone: allocation. The rent is split by the size of each room, and the cook's salary by how many meals each eats: apportionment, each on its own fair basis. A bill nobody can trace gets split anyway, because someone has to pay it; the only question is how fairly.
:::

::: key
A cost is **allocated** when the whole of it belongs to one cost centre, and **apportioned** when it is shared and must be split on a fair basis. The link between the expense and the cost centre decides which, not the kind of expense: power on its own meter is allocated, power on a common meter is apportioned.
:::

## How it works

### What counts as an overhead

Overheads are the expenditure which cannot be conveniently traced to or identified with any particular cost unit; they are incurred for output generally and not for a particular work order, e.g. wages of watch and ward staff, heating and lighting of the factory (SM 4.2). They are the aggregate of **indirect material**, **indirect employee cost** and **indirect expenses** (SM 1.26), and they arise in the factory, in administration and in selling and distribution. In a manufacturing concern they often exceed direct wages or direct materials, and at times both together, so ignoring them is "a grave mistake" (SM 4.2).

### Why split fixed from variable

The primary objective of splitting semi-variable expenses into fixed and variable is to ascertain **marginal costs**. It also helps in (SM 4.6–4.7):

1. **Controlling expenses**: fixed costs are policy costs, hard to reduce; variable costs are controlled by allowances related to the output achieved.
2. **Preparation of budget estimates**: the flexible budget estimates costs at each level of activity (worked in Example 2 below).
3. **Decision making**: pricing in a recession or for export, make or buy, shut down or continue. Marginal costing, CVP and break-even analysis all rest on this split ([[cma.mc.basics|marginal cost and contribution]]).

### Four steps, five stages, from estimate to unit

Actual overheads are known only after the accounts close, but cost sheets are needed now. So overheads are estimated in advance and charged to products at **pre-determined rates**, and the difference is adjusted at year end (SM 4.7–4.8).

The Study Material tells this journey twice. Its procedure for manufacturing overheads has **four steps**: estimation and collection, assignment, absorption, and treatment of under- or over-absorption (SM 4.8–4.10). Its chart of the steps for the distribution of overheads has **five stages**: estimation, allocation, apportionment, re-apportionment and absorption (SM 4.11). Same road: the chart splits assignment into its three parts and stops before the year-end adjustment.

::: steps
1. **Estimation and collection.** Estimate overheads from past figures adjusted for known future changes. Sources: (a) invoices, (b) stores requisitions, (c) the wage analysis book, (d) journal entries (SM 4.8). Collection is usually through **standing orders**: a sanction for indirect expenses under various heads, each head given a number that continues from year to year (SM 4.11–4.12).
2. **Assignment.** The guiding principle is the traceability of the overhead in an economically feasible manner, on one of two principles: **cause and effect** (the cause is the process, operation or activity, the effect is the incurrence of cost) or **benefit received** (overheads apportioned in proportion to the benefit each cost object receives) (SM 4.8). Assignment has three parts: (a) **allocation**, (b) **apportionment**, (c) **re-apportionment** ([[cma.oh.primary|primary distribution]] does the first two, [[cma.oh.secondary|secondary distribution]] the third).
3. **Absorption.** Each production department's overheads are recovered from its output. Variable manufacturing overheads are absorbed on actual production; fixed manufacturing overheads on ==normal capacity== (SM 4.10). Rates and methods: [[cma.oh.absorb|absorption rates and the machine hour rate]].
4. **Under- or over-absorption.** There is bound to be some difference between the actual and the absorbed overheads; it is adjusted keeping in view its reasons (SM 4.10) ([[cma.oh.underover|under- and over-absorption]]).
:::

The whole exercise serves two purposes: (a) to charge products with an **equitable portion** of factory overheads, and (b) to charge them **immediately** as a job is completed, without waiting for actual figures (SM 4.10).

### Departments first: departmentalisation

A factory has **production departments**, which make the product, and **service departments**, which do not take part in production directly but provide auxiliary services to other cost centres: engineering, quality control, laboratory, canteen, stores, time office, dispensary (SM 4.9). Collecting overheads department by department, or by smaller **cost centres** (machine or personnel), is **departmentalisation** ([[cma.intro.terms|cost centres]]). Its advantages (SM 4.12–4.13):

1. **Better estimation of expenses**: departmental expenses can be estimated almost exactly.
2. **Better control**: you can see which department overspent.
3. **Ascertainment of cost for each department**: a job is charged only for the departments it passes through.
4. **Suitable method of costing**: e.g. batch costing in a parts department, single output costing in assembly.

## Formats and formulas

*Write the definitions in ICAI's words; they are asked as MCQs and as "distinguish" questions.*

::: definition Overheads
Overheads represent expenses that have been incurred in providing certain ancillary facilities or services which facilitate or make possible the carrying out of the production process; by themselves these services are not of any use. (SM 4.63)
:::

::: definition Cost allocation
The term "allocation" refers to assignment or allotment of an entire item of cost to a particular cost center or cost unit. (SM 4.63)
:::

::: definition Cost apportionment
Apportionment implies "the allotment of proportions of items of cost to cost centres or departments". (SM 4.9)
:::

::: definition Re-apportionment
The process of assigning service department overheads to production departments is called reassignment or re-apportionment. (SM 4.9)
:::

::: definition Absorption
The process of recovering overheads of a department or any other cost center from its output is called recovery or absorption. (SM 4.10)
:::

::: formula Budgeted overhead at a new output
$$\text{Budget} = F + SF + (V + SV) \times \frac{Q_1}{Q_0}$$
F = fixed overheads; SF = fixed part of the semi-variable overheads; V = variable overheads and SV = variable part of the semi-variable overheads, both at the old output; $Q_0$ = old output, $Q_1$ = new output. Split the semi-variable cost first; only the variable parts move with output (SM 4.6–4.7). Splitting methods are in [[cma.intro.behaviour|cost behaviour]].
:::

## Under the hood

### Fine print: packing, canteens, welfare and training

The Study Material's "treatment of certain items" settles several classification questions (SM 4.60–4.62):

| Item | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Primary packing (protects the product, eases handling) | Production cost |
| Packing for transport to the customer | Distribution cost |
| Special packing at the customer's request | Charged to that work order or job |
| Fancy packing to attract customers | Selling overhead (advertising) |
| Fringe benefits (overtime, shift allowance, holiday pay, pension) | Production overhead; if large, a supplementary wage rate |
| Removal and re-erection of machines | Production overhead; costing P&L if due to faulty planning |
| Canteen subsidy | Production overhead, by number of workers; a share to office overhead if office staff use it |
| Welfare activities | General overhead, split between factory, office, selling and distribution by number of persons |
| Training of factory workers | Cost of production; office or sales staff training to those overheads |

Administration, selling, distribution and R&D overheads have their own lesson: [[cma.oh.ads|administration, selling, distribution and R&D overheads]].

### Allocation is "the wider term"

The Study Material closes its comparison with two points: the relationship between the expense and the cost centre decides whether it is allocated or apportioned, and "allocation is a much wider term than apportionment" (SM 4.16). Its summary defines allocation as the allotment of an entire item to a cost centre **or a cost unit**, not only to departments.

### Controllable for whom?

"Uncontrollable" is always relative to a person. The tool room's expenditure is controllable by its foreman, but the share of it apportioned to the machine shop is not controllable by the machine-shop foreman (SM 1.33). In fact, says the Study Material, no cost is uncontrollable; it is only in relation to a particular individual that a cost is controllable or not.

## Answer frame

**Distinguish between allocation and apportionment** (SM 4.16): write it as a table, then the two points below it.

| Point | Allocation | Apportionment |
|---|---|---|
| What is charged | Whole items of cost identifiable with one department (e.g. indirect wages of each department obtained separately) | Proportions of an item of cost (e.g. the cost of a service shared by the departments that avail it) |
| Nature of the process | A direct process of charging expenses to cost centres | An indirect process: the appropriate portion borne by each department must be identified |

- The relationship between the expense and the cost centre, not its nature, decides whether an expense is allocated or apportioned.
- Allocation is a much wider term than apportionment.

**Distinguish between cost allocation and cost absorption**: allocation is the allotment of a whole item of cost to a cost centre or cost unit; absorption is the process of absorbing all overheads allocated to or apportioned over a cost centre or production department into the units produced. Allocation gets costs **into** cost centres; absorption gets them **out**, into products.

**Advantages of departmentalisation** (four points, SM 4.12–4.13) and **advantages of separating fixed and variable overheads** (the objective plus three points, SM 4.6–4.7) are written as numbered points with one line each, in ICAI's order.

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: "power is always apportioned"
Students learn "power: horse power × machine hours" and apply it even when a department has its own meter. **Fix:** check the facts first; a metered or exclusively used cost is allocated in full, and only the shared remainder is apportioned.
:::

::: mistake The 25% that ate the budget
Increasing every overhead in proportion to output, as Kesar did, overstated April by ₹1,72,500. **Fix:** split semi-variable costs, keep the fixed parts fixed, and move only the variable parts.
:::

::: mistake Cartons in the factory, pouches on the truck
Primary and secondary packing swapped. **Fix:** ask what the packing does. Protects or holds the product: production. Carries it to the customer: distribution. Attracts the customer: selling.
:::

## Recap

- Overheads: costs not conveniently traceable to a cost unit; the aggregate of indirect material, indirect employee cost and indirect expenses.
- Four classifications, in ICAI's order: function (factory, administration, selling and distribution), nature (fixed, variable, semi-variable), element, control.
- Splitting fixed and variable serves marginal costing, then control, budget estimates and decisions; budget only the variable parts in proportion to output.
- Stages: estimation and collection (standing orders) → allocation → apportionment → re-apportionment → absorption → under- or over-absorption.
- Allocation: whole item to one cost centre; apportionment: proportions of an item on a fair basis (cause and effect or benefit received).
- The relationship with the cost centre decides allocation or apportionment; allocation is the wider term.
- Variable manufacturing overheads are absorbed on actual production, fixed ones on normal capacity.
- Primary packing: production; secondary: distribution; fancy: selling. ("Miscellaneous" is not a function.)

Q:: Define cost apportionment.
A:: The allotment of proportions of items of cost to cost centres or departments.

Q:: What decides whether an expense is allocated or apportioned?
A:: The relationship between the expense and the cost centre, not the nature of the expense.

Q:: The two principles for assigning manufacturing overheads?
A:: Cause and effect, and benefit received.

Q:: Four sources for collecting factory overheads?
A:: Invoices, stores requisitions, the wage analysis book and journal entries.

Q:: What is a standing order?
A:: A sanction for indirect expenses under various heads of expenditure; each head gets a number that continues from year to year.

Q:: On what are variable and fixed manufacturing overheads absorbed?
A:: Variable on actual production; fixed on normal capacity.

Q:: Four advantages of departmentalisation?
A:: Better estimation of expenses, better control, ascertainment of cost for each department, a suitable method of costing for each department.

Q:: Primary, secondary and fancy packing: which cost does each belong to?
A:: Primary packing: production cost. Packing for transport to the customer: distribution cost. Fancy packing: selling overhead.
