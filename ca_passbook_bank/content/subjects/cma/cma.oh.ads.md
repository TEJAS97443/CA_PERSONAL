## In plain words

### Who pays for the boardroom chai?

Tapri's head office in Indore costs lakhs every month: the board, finance, legal, the audit fee. The sales team, the Diwali advertising and the dealers' commission cost more. The delivery vans and godowns cost more again. None of it fries a samosa.

> **Kesar:** The factory makes the samosas. Why should a samosa pay for the board's chai?
> **Ritu:** Because without the board there is no factory, no price list and no samosa.
> **Kesar:** Then add 20% to every pack and be done. Or put it all in miscellaneous.
> **Ritu:** The first is one of ICAI's three methods. The second is not.

**Administration overheads** are the costs of general management and of secretarial, accounting and administrative services, which cannot be related directly to production, marketing, research or development. **Selling overheads** are incurred to promote marketing and sales (salesmen, advertising), and **distribution overheads** to deliver and despatch goods sold (vans, transit insurance, godowns). They sit below works cost in the [[cma.sheet.mfg|cost sheet]], and each has its own question: how much of it each product should carry, and how to keep it from growing.

Administration costs are mostly fixed and arise from management's policy; selling and distribution costs depend on markets, competitors and customers. Both are hard to tie to a unit and hard to control. ICAI's answers are a choice of methods and bases for absorbing them, and three tools for control: comparison with the past, budgets and standards. **Research and development** costs go wherever the research points: to production, to administration, to selling, to one product, or to Costing P&L if the research fails.

::: analogy The school principal's salary
A school's teachers are its production cost. Who bears the principal's salary? The school can split it between the classrooms and the admissions office (apportion between production and sales), treat it as a school-wide expense charged to no class (Costing P&L), or add it to each class as a separate charge on its fees or its number of students (a separate addition to cost). The admission brochures are selling costs, and the school bus is distribution.
:::

::: key
Administration overheads are apportioned between production and sales, charged to Costing P&L, or added to cost as a separate charge on a base such as works cost; selling and distribution overheads are best absorbed as an estimated amount per unit (fixed costs by benefit, plus variable costs). Both are controlled by comparison, budgets and standards. R&D follows the purpose of the research.
:::

## How it works

### Administration overheads

::: definition Administrative overhead
"The sum of those costs of general management and of secretarial accounting and administrative services, which cannot be directly related to the production, marketing, research or development functions of the enterprise." (CIMA Terminology, SM 4.46)
:::

They are the expenses of formulating policy, directing the organisation and controlling its operations, and are collected and classified like factory overheads. ICAI gives three methods of accounting for them, in this order (SM 4.47–4.48):

 "caption": "Under (a) they merge into production and selling and distribution overheads; under (b) they never reach product cost; under (c) they stay a separate function with their own rate."}

| Method (SM 4.47–4.48) | Why | Disadvantages, or bases |
|---|---|---|
| **(a) Apportion between production and sales departments** | Incurred for the benefit of both, so each bears a share; they lose their identity and merge with production and selling and distribution overheads | (1) Suitable bases are difficult to find; (2) a lot of clerical work; (3) not justified to apportion only over production and sales when equally important departments, like finance, exist |
| **(b) Charge to Costing Profit and Loss Account** | They concern policy formulation, not production or selling directly; a suitable basis is difficult to find; they are fixed costs | (1) Cost of products is understated; (2) excluding them from product cost is against sound accounting principle |
| **(c) A separate addition to cost of production or sales** | Administration is a separate function like production and sales, charged to each job or product with the cost of the other functions | Bases used: (i) works cost; (ii) sales value or quantity; (iii) gross profit on sales; (iv) quantity produced; (v) conversion cost, etc. |

**Control.** Mostly fixed, arising from management policy and generally non-controllable, they should still not be allowed to grow disproportionately. Three methods (SM 4.48–4.49):

1. **Classification and analysis by administrative departments according to their functions, compared with the results accomplished.** Each department's expenses are collected under standing orders and compared with the previous period in relation to accomplishment. This gives limited control when activity is not constant, so absorption rates may also be compared from period to period: the extent of over- or under-absorption reveals efficiency. A service department's cost may be compared with the price of the same service from outside, to decide whether to continue it or entrust the work to outsiders.
2. **Control through budgets**: monthly or annual administration budgets for each department, compared with actuals; variances analysed and responsibility assigned.
3. **Control through standards**: standards of performance for each administrative activity, compared with actual performance.

### Selling and distribution overheads

Selling overheads are the expenses of promoting the marketing and sales of different products; distribution expenses relate to delivery and despatch of goods sold. Many concerns group them as one class, **selling and distribution overheads**, collected through standing orders (SM 4.53). The usual absorption bases, with ICAI's comments (SM 4.53–4.54):

| Basis | ICAI's comment |
|---|---|
| (a) Sales value of goods | Ordinarily ==the most logical basis==: there is some connection between sales and the expenses incurred to achieve them |
| (b) Cost of goods sold | Not as satisfactory: it may have no direct relationship with selling and distribution cost |
| (c) Gross profit on sales | Gives a larger share to goods with a large margin: follows the principle of "ability to pay"; may not reflect costs or efforts incurred |
| (d) Number of orders or units sold | One of the usual bases |

**The best method, an estimated amount per unit.** Separate fixed expenses from variable ones. Apportion the fixed expenses according to the benefit each product derives, then divide by the units sold or likely to be sold; add the variable expenses per unit, worked out for each product: packaging, freight outwards, insurance in transit, commission to salesmen, rebate to customers (SM 4.54). ICAI's bases for the fixed ones:

| Fixed expense | Basis of apportionment (SM 4.54) |
|---|---|
| Salaries of the sales department and of salesmen | Estimated time devoted to the sale of each product |
| Advertisement | Actual amount for each product; common expenses (an exhibition) on each product's advertisement expenditure |
| Show room expenses | Average space occupied by each product |
| Rent of finished goods godowns; expenses on own delivery vans | Average quantities delivered during a period |
| No suitable basis | Proportion of sales of the products |

**Control is difficult**, for three reasons (SM 4.55):

1. Their incidence depends mainly on **external factors** beyond management's control: distance of the market, extent and nature of competition, terms of sale.
2. They depend on **customers' behaviour**, likes, dislikes and tastes, so controlling them may lose customers.
3. Being **policy costs**, they are not amenable to control.

In spite of this, three methods may be used: **(a) comparison with past performance** (the expenses, or the expenses as a percentage of sales, against the previous period; suitable for small concerns); **(b) budgetary control** (a budget split into fixed and variable, if necessary a [[cma.bud.flexible|flexible budget]] for different levels of sales, actuals compared and variances acted on); **(c) standard costing** (standards for salesmen, territories, products, set in relation to the standard sales volume; actuals compared and variances enquired into).

### Other items: what ICAI says

| Item (SM 4.59–4.62) | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Interest and financing charges**: interest for the use of non-equity funds and the incidental cost of arranging them (interest on borrowings, financing charges of finance leases, cash discount allowed to customers); not imputed costs | Presented in the cost statement as a **separate item of cost of sales** |
| **Depreciation**: "the diminution in the intrinsic value of an asset due to use and/or the lapse of time" | Traced to the cost object to the extent economically feasible; otherwise assigned on (i) cause and effect or (ii) benefit received |
| **Bad debts** | No unanimity. One view: excluded from cost, being financial losses. Another: part of selling and distribution overheads when they arise in the normal course of trading. Extraordinarily large bad debts are not included in cost accounts |
| **Carriage and cartage** | On direct material: cost of direct material; on indirect material (stores): factory overheads; on finished goods: distribution overhead |
| **Night shift allowance** | Usually caused by pressure of work beyond normal capacity: a production overhead, recovered as such (as direct wages it would make night jobs costlier than day jobs). For a specific customer's order: charged to that order. Due to abnormal circumstances: Costing P&L |

Packing (primary, secondary, special and fancy), fringe benefits, removal and re-erection of machines, canteen, welfare and training expenses are tabulated in [[cma.oh.classify|classifying overheads]].

## Formats and formulas

*Reproduce the reverse-calculation steps and the product-wise statement from memory.*

::: formula Administration and selling rates on a base
$$\text{Admin. rate} = \frac{\text{Admin. overheads}}{\text{Works cost}} \times 100 \qquad \text{S\&D rate} = \frac{\text{S\&D overheads}}{\text{Sales value}} \times 100$$
Works cost is factory cost. The base is the one the question names: works cost, cost of production, sales value, units.
:::

::: formula Estimated selling and distribution expense per unit (SM 4.54)
$$\text{S\&D per unit} = \frac{\text{Fixed S\&D of the product}}{\text{Units sold}} \qquad + \text{Variable per unit}$$
Fixed S&D of the product: the fixed expenses apportioned to it on their bases. Units sold: sold or likely to be sold. Variable per unit: packaging, freight outwards, insurance in transit, commission, rebate.
:::

::: formula Working back from a selling price
$$\text{Total cost} = \frac{\text{Price}}{1 + p_c} \qquad \text{Total cost} = \text{Price} \times (1 - p_s)$$
$p_c$ = profit as a fraction of cost; $p_s$ = profit as a fraction of the selling price. With factory overheads at F% of direct wages and administration at A% of factory cost: factory cost = prime cost + F × direct wages; total cost = factory cost × (1 + A).
:::

::: answer Show the working
**(i) Recovery rates.** Let F = factory overheads as a fraction of direct wages and A = administrative overheads as a fraction of factory cost.

Working note: total cost = selling price ÷ (100% + profit %): K-1 = ₹2,10,600 ÷ 1.125 = **₹1,87,200**; K-2 = ₹1,55,520 ÷ 1.20 = **₹1,29,600**.

$$(1{,}20{,}000 + 48{,}000F)(1 + A) = 1{,}87{,}200 \qquad (81{,}000 + 36{,}000F)(1 + A) = 1{,}29{,}600$$

Dividing the first equation by the second removes (1 + A):

$$\frac{1{,}20{,}000 + 48{,}000F}{81{,}000 + 36{,}000F} = \frac{1{,}87{,}200}{1{,}29{,}600} = \frac{13}{9}$$

So 10,80,000 + 4,32,000F = 10,53,000 + 4,68,000F, giving 36,000F = 27,000 and **F = 0.75**. Then 1 + A = ₹1,87,200 ÷ (₹1,20,000 + ₹36,000) = 1.20, so **A = 0.20**.

## Under the hood

### Reverse questions are three equations in disguise

Every "find the recovery rates" question uses the same three identities: total cost from the selling price and the profit percentage; factory cost from total cost and the administration percentage; factory overheads as the gap between factory cost and prime cost. With one job and one unknown rate (PYQ January 2026 MCQ 12) it is two divisions and a subtraction; with two jobs and two unknown rates (Illustration 9), divide one job's equation by the other's.

### Where these overheads sit in the cost sheet

Administration related to production is added before cost of production; general administration, selling and distribution after cost of goods sold, with interest and financing charges as a separate item of cost of sales ([[cma.sheet.mfg|cost sheet for a manufacturer]]). When they are absorbed at pre-determined rates, their over- or under-absorption is a reconciling item between cost and financial profit: the reconciliations in PYQ May 2024 and MTP January 2026 Series I both list administration overheads over-absorbed ([[cma.sys.recon|reconciling cost and financial profit]]).

### Comparing absorption rates as control

Administration's first control method ends in a comparison of absorption rates and of over- or under-absorption from period to period (SM 4.48–4.49): the same tool that judges factory overheads ([[cma.oh.underover|under- and over-absorption]]).

## Answer frame

**"Discuss the problems of controlling selling and distribution overheads"** (SM question 3), then **"methods"** (PYQ January 2025 Q6(c), 4 marks):

1. Problems: (i) external factors (distance of market, competition, terms of sale) beyond management's control; (ii) dependence on customers' behaviour and tastes, so control may lose customers; (iii) policy costs, not amenable to control.
2. Methods: (a) comparison with past performance, also as a percentage of sales; suitable for small concerns; (b) budgetary control, fixed and variable, a flexible budget if needed, variances acted on; (c) standard costing, standards for salesmen, territories, products, variances enquired into.

**"Explain the methods of accounting administrative overheads"**: the three methods in ICAI's order, each with its reason and its disadvantages (the table in How it works); for (c), the five bases.

**"Explain research and development expenses and their treatment in costing"** (MTP September 2025, 4 marks): the two Terminology definitions; production-method research in cost of production; administration research in administration overhead; market research in selling and distribution; development of a particular product charged to it as deferred revenue expenditure, recovered per unit when production is fully established; routine general research to general overhead (deferred if substantial); fundamental research to administration; failed research to Costing P&L.

**"Treatment of bad debts in cost accounts"**: no unanimity; view 1, excluded as financial losses; view 2, part of selling and distribution overheads in the normal course of trading; extraordinarily large bad debts excluded.

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: the margin that moved
Taking "20% on selling price" as 20% on cost prices Job K-3 at ₹1,38,240 instead of ₹1,44,000. **Fix:** profit on price means cost is 80% of price: price = cost ÷ 0.80.
:::

::: mistake Administration on the wrong base
Charging 20% administration on prime cost (₹1,20,000 for K-1) instead of on factory cost (₹1,56,000) understates K-1's total cost by ₹7,200. **Fix:** write the base next to every percentage in the statement, as ICAI does.
:::

::: mistake Dropping the product that "makes a loss"
The kulhad line's loss is after apportioned fixed costs that stay if the line goes. **Fix:** look at contribution and at which fixed costs would really be saved.
:::

::: mistake Interest inside the factory
Adding interest on borrowings to works cost or cost of production. **Fix:** interest and financing charges are a separate item of cost of sales (SM 4.60).
:::

## Practice

::: quiz Check yourself
Q: A job's direct materials are ₹60,000 and direct wages ₹40,000. Administrative overheads are 20% of factory cost and profit is 25% of total cost. The selling price is ₹1,95,000. What is the factory overhead rate as a percentage of direct wages?
= 75
> Total cost = ₹1,95,000 ÷ 1.25 = ₹1,56,000; factory cost = ₹1,56,000 ÷ 1.20 = ₹1,30,000; factory overheads = ₹1,30,000 − ₹1,00,000 = ₹30,000, which is 75% of ₹40,000.

Q: Case scenario. Tapri's lab spends ₹18 lakh this year developing a sugar-free chai premix, a single new product whose production will be fully established next year. Bunty, on an audit visit from Iyer & Associates, suggests charging it to the Costing Profit and Loss Account. Which treatment is correct?
- [ ] Charge it to the Costing Profit and Loss Account this year
- [ ] Add it to administration overheads
- [x] Charge it to the premix as deferred revenue expenditure, recovered per unit once production is established
- [ ] Add it to selling and distribution overheads
> SM 4.63: development costs for a particular product are charged directly to that product, usually as deferred revenue expenses recovered as a cost per unit when production is fully established. Costing P&L is for research that fails.

Q: Which basis does ICAI call ordinarily the most logical for absorbing selling overheads?
- [ ] Cost of goods sold
- [x] Sales value of goods
- [ ] Gross profit on sales
- [ ] Units produced
> SM 4.53: sales value, because there is some connection between sales and the expenses incurred to achieve them. Cost of production may have no direct relationship; gross profit follows "ability to pay".

Q: Why is control of selling and distribution overheads difficult? Select all that apply.
- [x] Their incidence depends on external factors such as competition and distance of markets
- [x] Controlling them may lose customers, whose tastes they depend on
- [x] They are policy costs, not amenable to control
- [ ] They are mostly variable with production volume
> ICAI's three reasons (SM 4.55). They vary with sales and with policy, not with production volume.

Q: How does ICAI treat a night shift allowance paid because work is beyond normal capacity, and one paid to finish a particular customer's order?
- [ ] Both as direct wages of the jobs worked at night
- [x] The first as production overhead; the second charged to that order
- [ ] Both to the Costing Profit and Loss Account
- [ ] The first to Costing P&L; the second as production overhead
> SM 4.62: general pressure beyond normal capacity makes it a production overhead; a specific customer's order bears its own night shift cost; abnormal circumstances send it to Costing P&L.

## Recap

- Administration overhead (CIMA): costs of general management and secretarial, accounting and administrative services not directly related to production, marketing, research or development.
- Three methods: apportion to production and sales; charge to Costing P&L; separate addition on works cost, sales, gross profit, quantity produced or conversion cost.
- Control of administration: departmental analysis against results (and absorption rates), budgets, standards.
- S&D bases: sales value (most logical), cost of goods sold, gross profit, orders or units. Best: estimated amount per unit (fixed by benefit + variable).
- S&D control is hard (external factors, customers, policy costs); use past comparison, budgets, standard costing.
- R&D: production methods, cost of production; administration or fundamental, administration overhead; market research, S&D; one product, deferred and charged to it; failure, Costing P&L.
- Interest: separate item of cost of sales. Bad debts: two views; very large ones excluded. Carriage follows what it carries.
- Reverse questions: Price → Cost → Factory cost → factory overheads. (The principal's salary still has to land somewhere.)

## Flashcards

Q:: CIMA's definition of administrative overhead?
A:: The sum of those costs of general management and of secretarial accounting and administrative services, which cannot be directly related to the production, marketing, research or development functions of the enterprise.

Q:: Three methods of accounting for administrative overheads, in ICAI's order?
A:: (a) Apportioning between production and sales departments; (b) charging to Costing Profit and Loss Account; (c) treating them as a separate addition to cost of production or sales.

Q:: Bases for absorbing administrative overheads as a separate addition to cost?
A:: Works cost; sales value or quantity; gross profit on sales; quantity produced; conversion cost, etc.

Q:: Two disadvantages of charging administrative overheads to Costing P&L?
A:: Cost of products is understated; excluding them from product cost is against sound accounting principle.

Q:: Three methods of controlling administrative overheads?
A:: Classification and analysis by departments according to functions compared with results accomplished; control through budgets; control through standards.

Q:: Four bases for absorbing selling and distribution overheads, and the most logical?
A:: Sales value of goods, cost of goods sold, gross profit on sales, number of orders or units sold; sales value is ordinarily the most logical.

Q:: Three reasons controlling selling and distribution overheads is difficult?
A:: Incidence depends on external factors beyond management's control; dependence on customers' behaviour and tastes; they are policy costs not amenable to control.

Q:: Three methods of controlling selling and distribution overheads?
A:: Comparison with past performance; budgetary control; standard costing.

Q:: Treatment of development costs of a particular product?
A:: Charged directly to that product, usually as deferred revenue expenses, recovered as a cost per unit when production is fully established.

Q:: Treatment of research that proves a failure?
A:: Excluded from costs and charged to the Costing Profit and Loss Account.

Q:: Treatment of interest and financing charges in a cost statement?
A:: Presented as a separate item of cost of sales; imputed costs are not included.

Q:: Treatment of carriage and cartage?
A:: On direct material: cost of direct material; on indirect material (stores): factory overheads; on finished goods: distribution overhead.
