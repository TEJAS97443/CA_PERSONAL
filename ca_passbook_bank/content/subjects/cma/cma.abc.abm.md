## In plain words

### Kesar finds the "useless" department

Ritu's first ABC report for Tapri's cold-chain and café business lists what each activity costs: brewing, delivering, moving milk crates from the receiving dock to the back store and then to the front store, re-delivering orders that came back, setting up the kulhad kiln for every new design.

> **Kesar:** Ritu, this is brilliant. Which activity adds no value? Delete it. Start with the accounts department; it has never made a single cup of chai.
> **Ritu:** The customer will not pay for accounts, but the law will not let you skip them. The crate moving and the re-deliveries, though: nobody pays for those, and they cost us ₹4 lakh a month.

That is **activity based management (ABM)**: using the activity costs that ABC produces to manage the activities themselves. An activity the customer is willing to pay for, like brewing chai to order, is **value-added**. One that does not improve the product or service but adds cost, like moving materials around or setting up machines for each run, is **non-value-added**: the first target for cutting, streamlining or elimination. ABM also redesigns whole processes, compares one café's activity costs with another's, and measures how efficiently each activity is performed.

**Activity based budgeting (ABB)** runs ABC backwards. ABC starts from the money spent and traces it to products. ABB starts from next year's planned work (how many deliveries, how many orders, how many invoices), works out the activities that work will need, and then the resources and money those activities will cost.

ABC itself has a price. It gives more accurate costs, a logical basis for overheads, better pricing and a spotlight on waste. But it is more expensive than the traditional system, is not much help to a small organisation or one with few products, and choosing the right driver is not always easy.

::: analogy The fitness tracker
A fitness tracker tells you what each activity burns: 300 calories for the walk, 40 for the meeting you could have skipped (ABC). The diet and exercise plan you build from those numbers is ABM: keep the walk, drop the pointless meeting. Planning next month's meals from next month's training schedule, work first and food second, is ABB. And a tracker costs money: a person who sits all day at one desk may not need one.
:::

::: key
ABC measures what activities cost; ABM manages the activities (keep value-added, remove or reduce non-value-added); ABB budgets by working back from the planned quantity of work to the resources it needs.
:::

## How it works

### Benefits and limits of ABC

The main **advantages** of ABC (SM 5.17–5.18), in ICAI's order:

1. More accurate costing of products/services.
2. Overhead allocation is done on a logical basis.
3. It enables better pricing policies by supplying accurate cost information.
4. It utilizes unit cost rather than just total cost.
5. It helps to identify non-value added activities, which facilitates cost reduction.
6. It is helpful to organizations with multiple products.
7. It highlights problem areas which require the attention of management.

The main **limitations** (SM 5.18):

1. It is more expensive, particularly in comparison with the traditional costing system.
2. It is not helpful to small organizations.
3. It may not be applied to organizations with limited products.
4. Selection of the most suitable cost driver may not be easy, or may be difficult or complicated.

### Requirements in ABC implementation

"A number of distinct practical stages are required in the ABC implementation" (SM 5.18):

::: steps
1. **Staff training**: the co-operation of the workforce is critical to successful implementation; staff training should create awareness of the purpose of ABC.
2. **Process specification**: informal but structured interviews with key members of personnel identify the different stages of the production process, the commitment of resources to each, processing times and bottlenecks.
3. **Activity definition**: activities must be defined clearly at an early stage to manage problems effectively. There might be overloading of information from the new data, but it is needed in codification.
4. **Activity driver selection**: a cost driver is selected for each activity.
5. **Assigning cost**: a single representative activity driver can be used to assign costs from the activity pools to the cost objects.
:::

These are the stages of *installing* ABC in an organisation. Do not confuse them with the five calculation stages of [[cma.abc.concept|identifying activities, cost pools and driver rates]].

### ABC as a decision-making tool

ICAI's five ways (SM 5.19):

1. ABC along with some other cost management technique can be used to improve the performance and profitability of an organisation.
2. Wholesale distributors gain in decision-making by correlating costs to activities; the introduction of a new product or vendor can be better decided.
3. Decisions on facility and resource expansion, such as relocating or opening a new distribution centre: the ABC model identifies the specific cost elements targeted (savings in freight or logistics may offset the new facility).
4. Decision support for human resources: activity costs can be associated with an individual, so new levels of financial performance can be determined (branch management, sales).
5. Companies that price on a cost plus markup basis find ABC very relevant for determining competitive prices.

### Activity based management

::: definition Activity Based Management (ABM)
The term Activity Based Management (ABM) is used to describe the cost management application of ABC. The use of ABC as a costing tool to manage costs at activity level is known as Activity Based Management (ABM). ABM is a discipline that focuses on ==the efficient and effective management of activities as the route to continuously improving the value received by customers==. ABM utilizes cost information gathered through ABC. (SM 5.19)
:::

The types of analysis involved in ABM (SM 5.20):

::: diagram The three analyses in ABM (SM 5.20)

:::

1. **Cost driver analysis**: the factors that cause activities to be performed need to be identified in order to manage activity costs; cost driver analysis identifies these causal factors.
2. **Activity analysis**: (a) **value-added activities (VA)** are indispensable in order to complete the process, and customers are usually willing to pay (in some way) for them, e.g. polishing furniture by a furniture manufacturer; (b) **non-value-added activities (NVA)** represent work that is not valued by the external or internal customer; they do not improve the quality or function of a product or service, but they can adversely affect costs and prices, e.g. moving materials and machine set-up for a production run.
3. **Performance analysis**: identifying appropriate measures to report the performance of activity centres or other organisational units, consistent with each unit's goals and objectives.

ABM can be used in business in four ways (SM 5.20–5.21):

1. **Cost reduction**: ABM identifies costs against activities and finds opportunities to streamline or reduce the costs or eliminate the entire activity, especially if there is no value added.
2. **Business process re-engineering**: examining business processes and making substantial changes to how the organisation currently operates. ABM is a powerful tool for measuring business performance, determining the cost of business output, and identifying opportunities to improve process efficiency and effectiveness.
3. **Benchmarking**: comparing ABC-derived activity costs of one segment of the company with those of other segments. It requires uniformity in the definition of activities and the measurement of their costs.
4. **Performance measurement**: focusing on activity performance to face competitors and manage costs, by monitoring the efficiency and effectiveness of activities.

ICAI's examples of performance measures (SM 5.21):

| Area | Measure |
|---|---|
| Quality of purchased component | Zero defects |
| Quality of output | % yield |
| Customer awareness | Orders; number of complaints |

### Activity based budgeting

::: definition Activity Based Budgeting (ABB)
Activity based budgeting analyses the resource input or cost for each activity. It provides a framework for estimating the amount of resources required in accordance with the budgeted level of activity. Actual results can be compared with budgeted results to highlight, in financial and non-financial terms, those activities with major discrepancies from budget, for potential reduction in the supply of resources. It is a planning and control system which seeks to support the objectives of continuous improvement. It means planning and controlling the expected activities of the organisation to derive a cost-effective budget that meets forecast workload and agreed strategic goals. ==ABB is the reversing of the ABC process to produce financial plans and budgets==. (SM 5.21)
:::

:::

The three **key elements** of ABB (SM 5.21): type of work to be done; quantity of work to be done; cost of work to be done.

Its **benefits** (SM 5.22):

1. ABB can enhance the accuracy of financial forecasts and increase management understanding.
2. When automated, ABB can rapidly and accurately produce financial plans and models based on varying levels of volume assumptions.
3. ABB eliminates much of the needless rework created by traditional budgeting techniques.

## Formats and formulas

*Reproduce ICAI's budget statement layout (Illustration 4, SM 5.28–5.30): working note of budgeted activity costs first, then rates, then the cost per unit of each product.*

::: formula Budgeted activity cost
$$\text{Budgeted activity cost} \qquad = \text{Fixed part} \qquad + \text{Present variable part} \times \text{Growth factor}$$
The growth factor is given ("to double": 2; "to rise by 60%": 1.6), or is budgeted level ÷ present level when the variable part moves with the driver. Stepped costs are added step by step ("₹1 lakh more for every one lakh more statements").
:::

::: formula Budgeted activity rate and product cost
$$\text{Budgeted activity rate} \qquad = \frac{\text{Budgeted activity cost}}{\text{Budgeted driver quantity}} \qquad \text{Budgeted cost per unit} \qquad = \frac{\sum \text{Rate} \times \text{Driver quantity}}{\text{Budgeted units of product}}$$
:::

## Worked examples

:::: example Tapri Express: an activity based budget
Tapri Express, Tapri's delivery app, offers three services: home delivery, office subscriptions and café pre-orders (collected at the counter, so never delivered). It has selected four activities for an activity based budget, so that the cost per customer account can guide next year's pricing.

|---|---:|---|

| Rider fleet: insurance | 1,50,000 | Fixed, no change |
| Rider fleet: fuel and rider incentives | 2,50,000 | To rise by 60% (the fleet is driven by number of deliveries) |
| Order processing | 8,00,000 | 40% fixed, no change; the variable part to rise to 2.5 times (driven by app orders) |
| Invoicing | 9,00,000 | 3 lakh invoices now, 4.5 lakh budgeted; cost rises ₹60,000 for every 50,000 more invoices (driven by invoices) |
| Customer support | 2,50,000 | To rise by 40% (driven by support minutes) |

| Budgeted drivers | Home | Office | Café |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Deliveries | 1,80,000 | 50,000 | – |
| App orders | 1,80,000 | 10,000 | 1,14,000 |

**Required:**

(i) Calculate the budgeted cost and the budgeted rate of each activity.

(ii) Prepare the budgeted cost statement activity-wise for each service.

(iii) Compute the budgeted cost per customer account of each service.

## Under the hood

### ZBB is also "activity-based"

ICAI's budgets chapter says "ZBB is an activity-based budgeting system where budgets are prepared for each activity rather than functional department" (SM 15.59), and old-scheme suggested answers repeat it (November 2019 Q6(e)). There the point is that each activity must be justified from zero; in ABB the point is that each activity's resources follow its planned workload. Both budget by activity, not by department. See [[cma.bud.zbb|zero based budgeting]] and [[cma.bud.basics|budgets and budgetary control]].

### Fixed, variable and stepped activity costs

ICAI's Illustration 4 mixes all three behaviours (SM 5.27–5.30): ATM machine maintenance and rent "all fixed, no change", currency replenishment "expected to double", computer processing "half fixed, the variable portion to three times", statements "one lakh rupees for every one lakh more statements". It is the same cost behaviour that drives a [[cma.bud.flexible|flexible budget]], applied activity by activity.

### "Activity Based Cost Management"

The chapter summary calls ABM "Activity Based Cost Management (ABM)" (SM 5.30), and ICAI's suggested answer to old-scheme May 2023 Q6(c) uses both names. They are the same thing.

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: the accounts department declared non-value-added
Kesar's logic: the café customer does not pay for it, so delete it. **Fix:** classify with ICAI's definition and examples. NVA is work "not valued by the external or internal customer" that does not improve the quality or function of the product but adds cost, such as moving materials or set-ups; the answer then says reduce, streamline or eliminate, not "delete everything the customer does not see". The accounts department serves internal users (management, lenders, the board) and the law requires it, so it is not NVA merely because no chai drinker sees it; ABM can still make it cheaper.
:::

::: mistake Mixing up the three lists
Writing "staff training, process specification" when asked how ABC is used in business, or "benchmarking" when asked for the stages of implementation. **Fix:** implementation has five stages (5.18); ABM in business has four uses (5.20–5.21); ABM analysis has three types (5.20).
:::

::: mistake Budgeting the variable part on the whole present cost
"Half fixed, the variable portion to three times": taking ₹5,00,000 × 3. **Fix:** fixed ₹2,50,000 + variable ₹2,50,000 × 3 = ₹10,00,000 (SM 5.29).
:::

## Recap

- ABC advantages: more accurate costing; logical overhead allocation; better pricing; unit cost; identifies NVA activities; helps multi-product firms; highlights problem areas.
- ABC limitations: more expensive; not helpful to small organisations; not for limited products; driver selection difficult.
- Implementing ABC: staff training, process specification, activity definition, activity driver selection, assigning cost.
- ABM: the cost management application of ABC; analyses: cost driver, activity (VA/NVA), performance.
- ABM in business: cost reduction, business process re-engineering, benchmarking, performance measurement.
- VA example: polishing furniture. NVA examples: moving materials, machine set-up for a production run.
- ABB: the reversing of the ABC process; key elements: type, quantity and cost of work; benefits: accurate forecasts, rapid plans at varying volumes, less needless rework. (The accounts department stays.)

A:: Enhances the accuracy of financial forecasts and management understanding; when automated, rapidly produces plans for varying volume assumptions; eliminates much of the needless rework of traditional budgeting.

Q:: Name four of ICAI's seven advantages of ABC.
A:: Any four of: more accurate costing; logical overhead allocation; better pricing policies; utilizes unit cost rather than total cost; identifies non-value added activities; helpful to multi-product organisations; highlights problem areas.
