## In plain words

### "Buy the software, sab adjust ho jayega"

Kesar has just bought his cousin's namkeen business, renamed it Tapri Namkeen, and wants a costing system there by Monday.

> **Kesar:** Ritu, buy the best costing software. Install it on Monday. Sab adjust ho jayega.
> **Ritu:** Software is the easy part. First I need to know why we want it, what we make, how we make it, and who will read the reports.
> **Kesar:** Everyone will read the reports!
> **Ritu:** Then they must be short enough to read. Per unit, please, not per kilogram of paper.

Ritu is right in the way ICAI is right. A costing system costs money, so the first question is whether it will pay for itself. Then, before installing anything, the business must be studied: the **objective** of the system (fixing prices? controlling costs?), the industry, the levels of management and what each needs, the product, the production process, what other departments need, how the cost books will tie up with the financial books, the law, and the qualities the information must have.

A system built that way has six qualities: it is **informative and simple**, **accurate and authentic**, **uniform and consistent**, **integrated** with the other systems, **flexible**, and **trusted** by management.

Once it runs, the **cost and management accounting department** collects costs by element, assigns them to products and services, compares them with budgets and standards, gives managers the information they need to decide, and measures how each department performs.

And installing it is a people problem as much as a software problem: busy managers call it interference, the accounts staff fear for their jobs, supervisors hate the extra forms, and trained cost staff are hard to find.

::: analogy The tailor-made suit
A costing system is a suit, not a T-shirt off the rack. The tailor first measures you: what the suit is for, your build, how you move. A good suit is comfortable (simple), fits exactly (accurate), uses one size chart (uniform), goes with your shoes (integrated), has seams that can be let out (flexible), and, most important, you actually wear it (trust). ICAI's own word for the system is "tailor-made".
:::

::: key
Study the business before installing the system, build the system so that people can and do use it, and remember that it must be worth its cost. A costing system that management does not trust produces reports that nobody reads.
:::

"As in the case of every other form of activity, it should be considered whether it would be profitable to have a Cost Accounting System." Management needs complete and accurate information to decide, and a well-established costing system should provide all relevant information as and when required by management and other stakeholders (SM 1.15).

### What the department is for: role and functions (SM 1.11–1.12)

The role of a cost and management accounting system is to:

- provide relevant information to management for decision making;
- assist management in planning, measurement, evaluation and controlling of business activities;
- help in allocation of cost to products and inventories for both external and internal users.

Its **functions** are:

1. **Collection and accumulation of cost** for each element of cost.
2. **Assigning costs to cost objects** to ascertain cost.
3. **Budgets and standards**: the Cost and Management Accounting Department (whatever it is called) sets budgets and standards for a period or activity beforehand; they are compared with the assigned and ascertained cost, and deviations are analysed and reported, to control costs.
4. **Information for decisions**, the main function: an information system environment, popularly known as the **Management Information System (MIS)**, gives management at all levels relevant and timely information, internal and external, for decisions on cost optimisation, price fixation and plans for products, processes and marketing.
5. **Performance evaluation**: the performance of each responsibility centre is measured against the set standards; the department gathers data such as time taken, wastages and process idleness, analyses it, prepares reports and takes the necessary action.

### Before installing: the nine factors (SM 1.15–1.17)

"Before setting up a system of cost accounting the factors mentioned below should be studied":

- (a) **Objective**: the objective of setting up the costing system, for example whether it is being introduced for fixing prices or for establishing a system of cost control.
- (b) **Nature of business or industry**: every business or industry has its own uniqueness and objectives, and cost accounting methods follow its cost information requirement; an oil refinery, for example, maintains process-wise cost accounts to find the cost of a process such as crude refinement.
- (c) **Organisational hierarchy**: the system should fulfil the information requirements of the different levels of management (the diagram below).
- (d) **Knowing the product**: the nature of the product determines the type of costing system. A product with by-products needs a system that accounts for by-products; for perishable or short shelf-life products, **marginal costing** is appropriate, to know the contribution and the minimum price at which the products could be sold.
- (e) **Knowing the production process**: a good costing system can never be established without complete knowledge of the production process; costs can be apportioned on the most appropriate and scientific basis only if the cost accountant can identify the effort or resources each process consumes. This includes basic technical know-how and the peculiarities of the process.
- (f) **Information synchronisation**: a department or system takes substantial organisational resources, so the information needs of other departments must be taken into account; for example, the accounts department submits a monthly stock statement to the lender bank and quantity-wise stock details when filing returns with the tax authorities.
- (g) **Method of maintenance of cost records**: decide beforehand how the cost and financial accounts could be inter-locked into a single integral accounting system, and how the results of separate cost and financial accounts could be reconciled by means of control accounts.
- (h) **Statutory compliances and audit**: records are maintained to comply with statutory requirements, and the applicable cost accounting standards are followed.
- (i) **Information attributes**: the information should have all the attributes of useful information, i.e. complete, accurate, timely and relevant, for an effective MIS.

### The six essential features of a good system (SM 1.14–1.15)

1. **Informative and simple**: tailor-made, practical, simple and capable of meeting the requirements of the business; ==it should not sacrifice utility by introducing inaccurate and unnecessary details==.
2. **Accurate and authentic**: the data should be accurate and authenticated; otherwise it may distort the output and a wrong decision may be taken.
3. **Uniformity and consistency** in the classification, treatment and reporting of cost data, for benchmarking and comparability of results in both horizontal and vertical analysis.
4. **Integrated and inclusive**: integrated with other systems such as financial accounting, taxation, statistics and operational research, for a complete overview and clarity in results.
5. **Flexible and adaptive**: flexible enough to be amended for changes in technological, reporting, regulatory and other requirements.
6. **Trust on the system**: management should trust the system and its output; this needs an active role of management in developing a system that reflects a strong conviction in using information for decision making.

### The price of a costing system: its limitations (SM 1.23–1.24)

1. **Expensive**: analysis, allocation and absorption of overheads require considerable additional work, and hence additional money.
2. **Requirement of reconciliation**: the results shown by the cost accounts differ from those shown by the financial accounts, so reconciliation statements are necessary to verify their accuracy.
3. **Duplication of work**: the organisation has to maintain two sets of accounts, financial accounts and cost accounts.

These three are why the first question is whether the system will be profitable to have, and why factor (g) asks how the two sets of accounts will be inter-locked or reconciled.

### The practical difficulties, and how to overcome them

The current Study Material chapter does not list these, but ICAI's suggested answers do (RTP September 2026 Q16(a); MTP Series II for September 2024 Q6(a)). The difficulties:

1. **Lack of top management support**: the installation does not receive the support of top management. They consider it interference in their work, believe it will involve additional paperwork, and wrongly think it is meant for keeping a check on their activities.
2. **Resistance from the cost accounting department's staff**, who fear losing their jobs and importance after the new system is implemented.
3. **Non-co-operation from user departments**: foremen, supervisors and other staff may not co-operate in providing the data, as it adds to their responsibilities and to the paperwork of the whole team.
4. **Shortage of trained staff**: installation is specialised work, and trained staff may be short.

To overcome them:

- sell the idea to top management and convince them of the utility of the system;
- overcome resistance and non-co-operation by a behavioural approach, dealing with the staff concerned effectively;
- give proper training to the staff at each level;
- hold regular meetings with the cost accounting staff, user departments and top management to clarify their doubts and misgivings.

::: note Where this list comes from

:::

## Worked examples

:::: example Tapri Namkeen: what to study first
Tapri Namkeen Pvt Ltd makes bhujia and sev in four stages (mixing, extruding, frying, packing), and fresh sweets with a shelf life of three days. The frying stage leaves spent oil and fried crumbs, sold to an animal-feed maker. The company has a cash-credit limit from its bank against stock and book debts. Kesar wants to price the sweets for the Diwali rush; the plant supervisors complain that nobody tells them their material and idle-time targets. The financial accounts are kept on accounting software that the plant does not use.

As the consultant, Ritu must decide what to study before installing a cost accounting system.

**Required:** Advise Ritu on the factors to be studied before installing a cost accounting system, applying each to Tapri Namkeen.

::: answer Model answer
Before setting up a system of cost accounting, the following factors should be studied (SM 1.15–1.17):

1. **Objective**: whether the system is introduced for fixing prices or for establishing cost control. Here both: Diwali pricing of sweets and material and idle-time control in the plant.
2. **Nature of business or industry**: cost accounting methods follow the industry's cost information requirement. Bhujia and sev pass through four stages, so process-wise cost accounts are needed (mixing, extruding, frying, packing).
3. **Organisational hierarchy**: top management needs corporate strategy information; strategic level management, marketing strategy and product pricing (Kesar's Diwali prices); operational level management, standard quantities to be consumed and idle-time reports (the supervisors' complaint).
4. **Knowing the product**: spent oil and crumbs are by-products, so the system must account for by-products; the sweets are perishable, so marginal costing is appropriate to know the contribution and the minimum price at which they could be sold.
5. **Knowing the production process**: the cost accountant must know the effort and resources each of the four stages consumes, to apportion costs on the most appropriate and scientific basis.
6. **Information synchronisation**: the needs of other departments must be met, such as the monthly stock statement for the bank under the cash-credit limit, and quantity-wise stock details for tax returns.
7. **Method of maintenance of cost records**: decide beforehand whether cost and financial accounts will be inter-locked into a single integral system or kept separately and reconciled through control accounts, since the financial accounts are on software the plant does not use.
8. **Statutory compliances and audit**: cost records must comply with statutory requirements and applicable cost accounting standards.
9. **Information attributes**: the information must be complete, accurate, timely and relevant, for an effective MIS.
:::

:::: example The cold-chain system nobody liked
Tapri Cold Chain Pvt Ltd installed a costing system three years ago. Ritu's review finds:

1. The monthly cost report runs to 46 pages, with tyre costs analysed by tread pattern; depot managers do not read it.
2. Diesel issued at depots is entered from memory at the end of the week.
3. The Indore depot treats tyre replacement as maintenance; the Nagpur depot treats it as running cost, so the two depots' cost per km cannot be compared.
4. Cost data is re-typed into the financial accounting software every month, and the two never agree.
5. When a new reporting requirement for e-way bills arrived, the system could not add the field for five months.
6. The managing director decides routes by instinct and ignores the reports: "My gut is cheaper than your software."

**Required:** Identify, for each finding, the essential feature of a good cost accounting system that is missing, and suggest a remedy.

::: answer Model answer
| Finding | Essential feature missing | Remedy |
|---|---|---|
| 1. 46 pages, tyre treads | Informative and simple: utility sacrificed by unnecessary details | A short, tailor-made report of what each depot manager needs |
| 2. Diesel from memory | Accurate and authentic data | Record issues when made, on authorised documents |
| 3. Tyres treated differently | Uniformity and consistency in classification and treatment | One classification for all depots, so results are comparable |
| 4. Re-typing, no agreement | Integrated and inclusive | Integrate with financial accounting (or reconcile through control accounts) |
| 5. No new field for five months | Flexible and adaptive | A system that can be amended for regulatory and reporting changes |
| 6. Gut over reports | Trust on the system | Management's active role in developing the system and a conviction to use its information |
:::

## Under the hood

### The computer in the cost department

The Study Material follows installation with the impact of information technology: with an ERP, a single entry gives custom-made reports for every purpose, documents such as material requisition notes are no longer made in multiple copies, and reconciliation of cost and financial results "becomes simpler and less cumbersome" (SM 1.17). Two of the limitations above, reconciliation and duplication of work, are exactly what integration reduces. The full list, and digital costing, are in [[cma.intro.methods|methods and techniques of costing, and digital costing]].

### Which method the factors point to

Factors (b), (d) and (e) decide the costing method: process costing for a refinery or a namkeen line, by-product accounting where by-products arise, marginal costing for perishables ([[cma.intro.methods|methods of costing]], [[cma.joint.byprod|treatment of by-products]], [[cma.mc.basics|marginal costing]]).

## Answer frame

### "Discuss the essential features of a good cost accounting system" (5 marks)

Six headings, each with one line of ICAI's explanation: informative and simple (tailor-made, no unnecessary details); accurate and authentic; uniformity and consistency (benchmarking, comparability); integrated and inclusive (financial accounting, taxation, statistics, operational research); flexible and adaptive (technological, reporting, regulatory changes); trust on the system (active role of management).

### "Factors to be considered before installing a costing system" (5 marks)

| Factor | The line that earns the mark |
|---|---|
| (a) Objective | Fixing prices or establishing cost control |
| (b) Nature of business or industry | Methods follow the industry's needs (refinery: process-wise accounts) |
| (c) Organisational hierarchy | Top: corporate strategy; strategic: pricing, diversification; operational: standard quantities, idle time |
| (d) Knowing the product | By-products; perishables: marginal costing |
| (e) Knowing the production process | Scientific apportionment needs knowledge of the process |
| (f) Information synchronisation | Other departments' needs (stock statement to the bank, tax returns) |
| (g) Method of maintenance of cost records | Inter-locking or reconciliation through control accounts |
| (h) Statutory compliances and audit | Statutory records, cost accounting standards |
| (i) Information attributes | Complete, accurate, timely, relevant |

In a case ("as a consultant"), apply each factor to the facts in one more line.

### Three answers to "practical difficulties and how to overcome them"

::: cols
**Loses marks**

"Staff do not like change, so train them."

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: "Install it first, study the business later"
Writing the essential features when the question asks for the factors to be considered *before installing*, or the other way round. **Fix:** factors (nine) come before installation: objective, nature of business, hierarchy, product, process, synchronisation, cost records, statutory compliances and audit, information attributes. Essential features (six) are qualities of the finished system.
:::

::: mistake Marginal costing for everything
Saying the nature of the product decides "marginal or absorption costing" in general. **Fix:** ICAI's point is specific: products with by-products need by-product accounting; for perishable or short shelf-life products, marginal costing is appropriate, to know the contribution and minimum selling price.
:::

::: mistake Limitations that are not ICAI's
Listing "cost accounting is inaccurate" or "it is not compulsory" as limitations. **Fix:** the Study Material's three are expensive, requirement of reconciliation, and duplication of work (SM 1.24).
:::

## Recap

- Nine factors to study before installing: objective, nature of business or industry, organisational hierarchy, knowing the product, knowing the production process, information synchronisation, method of maintenance of cost records, statutory compliances and audit, and information attributes.
- Six essential features of a good system: informative and simple, accurate and authentic, uniformity and consistency, integrated and inclusive, flexible and adaptive, and trust on the system.
- Three limitations of cost accounting: it is expensive, it requires reconciliation with the financial accounts, and it duplicates work. So the first question is whether the system will be profitable to have.
- Five functions: collect cost by element; assign to cost objects; set budgets and standards, compare, report; information for decisions (MIS, the main function); evaluate responsibility centres.
- Perishables: marginal costing (contribution, minimum price). By-products: a system that accounts for them.
- Practical difficulties (ICAI's RTP and MTP answers): no top management support, staff resistance, user departments' non-co-operation, shortage of trained staff; remedies: sell the idea, behavioural approach, training, regular meetings.
- A system nobody trusts is a 46-page report nobody reads.
