## In plain words

### The budget that starts in the sales office

Budget week at Tapri. Ritu refuses to touch the factory's numbers until the sales team hands in theirs.

> **Kesar:** The factory is sitting idle while the sales people argue. Start with the samosas!
> **Ritu:** I cannot plan to fry samosas nobody will buy, or buy potatoes for samosas I will not fry.
> **Dev:** And I cannot arrange cash for potatoes nobody will fry.
> **Kesar:** So the whole company waits for one spreadsheet?
> **Ritu:** Per unit, please: yes. Sales first.

That is how a budget is built. The **sales budget** comes first: how many packs, at what price, in which channel. The **production budget** follows: sales, plus the stock Tapri wants left at the end, minus the stock it already has. Production decides how much material is used (the **material usage budget**), and usage, adjusted for the material stocks wanted at the start and end, decides what must be bought (the **purchase budget**). The same production decides the hours of labour, the machine hours and the overheads. Costed and added up, they give the cost of production and the cost of goods sold.

Each of these is a **functional budget**: a budget for one function of the business (selling, producing, buying, administering). When all of them are ready, the budget officer summarises them into a budgeted profit and loss account and a budgeted balance sheet: the **master budget**, which the board approves.

::: analogy The halwai's order book
Before Diwali a sweet shop works backwards. Orders for 500 kg of barfi, plus 50 kg to keep on the shelf, minus 30 kg left from yesterday: make 520 kg. Each kilo needs 600 g of khoya, so 312 kg of khoya will be used; keep 20 kg in the fridge for tomorrow, and 12 kg is already there, so buy 320 kg. The halwais needed, the gas cylinders burnt, the boxes ordered all follow from the 520 kg. The owner's final sheet of rupees in and rupees out is the master budget.
:::

::: key
Every functional budget is built from the one before it: sales, then production (sales + closing stock − opening stock), then materials (usage + closing stock − opening stock = purchases), labour and overheads. The master budget is their summary, approved and adopted as the standard to work to.
:::

## How it works

### Fifteen functional budgets, one chain

A **functional budget** is one related to a function of the business, for example the production budget for the manufacturing function. Functional budgets are prepared for each function and are subsidiary to the master budget; which ones are prepared varies with the size and nature of the business (SM 15.29). ICAI's list:

(i) Sales; (ii) Production; (iii) Plant utilisation; (iv) Direct-material usage; (v) Direct-material purchase; (vi) Direct labour (personnel); (vii) Production or factory overhead; (viii) Production cost; (ix) Ending inventory; (x) Cost of goods sold; (xi) Selling and distribution cost; (xii) Administration expenses; (xiii) Research and development cost; (xiv) Capital expenditure; (xv) Cash budget.

### Sales budget: the first domino

Sales forecast is the commencement of budgeting, so the sales budget assumes primary importance: the quantity which can be sold may be the **principal budget factor** in many business undertakings (SM 15.30). It is prepared for each product, with (1) the quantity of estimated sales and (2) the expected unit selling price, often by region or sales representative. Past sales volumes are the starting point, adjusted for (SM 15.30):

(i) backlog of unfulfilled sales orders; (ii) planned advertising and promotion; (iii) expected industry and general economic conditions; (iv) productive capacity; (v) projected pricing; (vi) findings of market research studies; (vii) relative product profitability; (viii) competition.

Kesar's favourite is (ii), planned advertising. Ritu's is (iv), productive capacity, which is why she keeps being left out of the ad shoots: a campaign that sells more than the fryers can make only sells disappointment.

The sales budget is not merely a sales forecast: a forecast projects the available customer demand, while the budget shows how management intends to react to it. It is an ==active rather than passive document== (SM 15.31). It may be classified by (1) products or groups of products; (2) areas, towns, salesmen and agents; (3) types of customers, for example Government, export, home sales and retail depots; (4) period: months, weeks (SM 15.31).

### Production budget: sales, adjusted for stock

The production budget is prepared in two parts: a **production volume budget** in physical units and a **cost of production (manufacturing) budget** under material, labour and factory overhead (SM 15.32). The volume is based on (1) the sales budget, (2) the production capacity of the factory, (3) the planned increase or decrease in finished stocks and (4) the policy governing outside purchase.

When the sales budget asks for more than the facilities can make, ICAI's remedies are overtime, shift working, sub-contracting or buying more plant; when there is surplus capacity, more advertising, lower prices to increase sales, or sub-contracting the surplus (SM 15.32–15.33).

The **level of stocks** depends on three factors (SM 15.33): (1) seasonal industries, where stocks are built up in the off season for the peak; (2) a steady and uniform level of production, to use the plant fully and avoid retrenchment or lay-off of workers; (3) producing so that minimum stocks are held, to avoid locking up funds. So the budget can show:

| Policy | Capacity | Inventory carrying cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Stabilised production** (the same, often the maximum, every month) | Fully utilised | Varies with the stocks held |
| **Stabilised stocks** (minimum stock every month) | May be under-utilised | Lowest |

Kesar prefers stabilised production (the fryers never rest); Dev prefers stabilised stocks (no cash asleep in the cold store). The production budget is where the two are traded off.

The **plant utilisation budget** states, in working hours, weight or other convenient units, the plant facilities needed to carry out the production budget. Its purposes (SM 15.34): (1) to determine the load on each process, cost centre or group of machines; (2) to show the overloaded ones, so that corrective action (overtime, sub-contracting, expansion) can be taken; (3) to dovetail the sales and production budgets where an overloaded process cannot be expanded; (4) where capacity is surplus, to boost sales to use it.

### Materials: what is used, then what is bought

The **direct material usage budget** is compiled in three steps (SM 15.34–15.35): (1) specify the quality standards for each item of material; (2) set the standard quantity of each item, with an allowance for normal loss in process from past performance, test runs or technical estimates; (3) set standard prices, considering stock and contracts entered into. Usage cost = quantity required × standard price.

The **direct material purchase budget** starts from the production budget: quantities to be bought × expected unit purchase price. Two considerations govern it: (i) the **economic order quantity** and (ii) the **re-order point with safety stocks** to cover fluctuations in demand ([[cma.mat.levels|stock levels]], [[cma.mat.eoq|EOQ]]). Its timing is coordinated between the purchasing and production departments (SM 15.35). A purchases budget can be wider than the materials budget: it may include purchases of items other than materials (SM 15.73, MCQ 7).

### Labour and overheads

The **direct labour (personnel) budget** turns the production and plant utilisation budgets into standard hours for each grade of labour, divided into direct and indirect, and multiplies them by standard wage rates (SM 15.36). Its merits: (1) it defines the direct and indirect labour force required; (2) it lets the personnel department plan recruitment and training ahead, so that labour turnover is kept to the minimum; (3) it reveals the labour cost for the manufacturing cost budget and the cash budget for financing the wage bill.

When workers are not fully efficient and some time is unproductive, the hours to be **paid** exceed the standard hours: standard hours ÷ efficiency (productivity) ratio gives the productive hours, and non-productive down-time is added on top: the hours paid while the fryer is being cleaned or the gas cylinder is late, real wages for zero samosas. Hours beyond the normal week are overtime, paid at a premium.

The **factory overhead budget** gives the total estimated cost of each item of indirect material, indirect labour and indirect expenses (power, fuel, fringe benefits, depreciation). It is useful for working out the **pre-determined overhead recovery rates**, and its departmental schedules split each cost into fixed and variable elements (SM 15.37). Fixed expenses are policy costs; indirect labour is estimated by work study (for example one supervisor for every twenty direct workers); indirect materials from the age and condition of plant (SM 15.37–15.38).

The **production cost budget** brings direct material, direct labour and factory overhead together. The **ending inventory budget** values the closing stocks of raw material and finished goods. The **cost of goods sold budget** adds the opening finished goods and deducts the closing ones; without those stock adjustments the same statement is called the production cost budget (SM 15.38–15.40).

The remaining budgets, in one line each (SM 15.40–15.46): **selling and distribution cost** (selling cost is "the cost of seeking to create and stimulate demand and of securing orders"; split into fixed and variable); **administration expenses** (mostly policy costs, fixed in nature, estimated from past experience adjusted for anticipated changes); **research and development** (a limit on spending is set); **capital expenditure** (the planned outlay on fixed assets, under strict management control, often over several years); **cash** (receipts and payments by month: [[cma.bud.cash|the cash budget]]). Their detailed lists are in Under the hood.

### The master budget: the summary the board signs

::: definition Master budget
CIMA, London: "the summary budget, incorporating its component functional budgets, which is finally approved, adopted and employed." When all the necessary functional budgets have been prepared, the budget officer prepares the master budget, which may consist of a budgeted profit and loss account and a budgeted balance sheet: these are the budget summaries. Once approved by the board of directors, it represents a standard for the achievement of which all the departments will work. (SM 15.55)
:::

## Formats and formulas

*Reproduce every format from memory: ICAI's suggested answers lay the statements out exactly like this, with a working note for every figure brought in.*

::: formula Production budget (units)
$$P = S + C - O$$
P = units to be produced; S = budgeted sales; C = desired closing stock; O = opening stock, all in units of finished goods. If good output is less than output (post-production rejects), divide the good units needed by (1 − rejection rate).
:::

::: formula Material usage and purchase budgets
$$U = P \times q \qquad B = U + C - O$$
U = usage; P = units to be produced; q = material per unit; B = quantity to be bought; C and O = closing and opening stocks of the material. Purchase cost = units to be purchased × unit price. A closing stock given as "N days' consumption" is usage × N ÷ days in the period.
:::

::: formula Hours to be paid in a labour budget
$$\text{Productive} = \frac{\text{Standard hours}}{\text{Efficiency ratio}}$$
$$\text{Paid} = \text{Productive} + \text{Down-time}$$
All in hours. Standard hours = units to be produced × standard hours per unit. Hours paid beyond the normal hours (workers × normal hours each) are overtime.
:::

## Under the hood

### The cost budgets in full (SM 15.40–15.46)

**Selling and distribution.** Problems in budgeting selling costs: (1) heavy selling and promotion expenditure may be needed when sales are falling, raising its percentage of sales; (2) the benefit of intensive promotion in one year may come in later years, so a proportion of selling cost to sales is hard to fix; (3) even so, the Budget Controller must establish a relationship between selling cost and sales volume. **Distribution cost** is the cost of the sequence of operations which begins with making the packed product available for dispatch and ends with making the re-conditioned returned empty package available for re-use (transport, storage, warehousing).

The **advertising budget** (the sales or advertisement manager's responsibility) considers: (1) the best method of advertisement; (2) the maximum amount for the period; (3) co-ordinating advertising with sales, spending only when enough product is ready for sale; (4) control over the expenditure and measurement of its effectiveness; (5) choosing the method by its effectiveness in increasing or maintaining sales. The amount may be fixed (1) as a percentage of sales or expected profit, from past experience; (2) to match what competitors are expected to spend; (3) as a fixed sum per unit of output; (4) by the company's ability to spend; (5) from an advertisement plan.

**Research and development.** Pure research increases knowledge; applied research aims at definite results such as improved methods. Methods of allocating R&D expenses: (1) a percentage of total sales value (good if sales are steady); (2) a percentage of net profit; (3) a total sum based on past experience and future R&D plans; (4) a sum fixed by the cash resources available.

**Capital expenditure.** Considerations: (1) it covers capital receipts and expenditure, such as land, building, plant, loans and share issues; (2) future development plans to increase output by expanding plant; (3) replacement requests from departments; (4) sales potential to absorb the extra output, possible price reductions and extra advertising; (5) "overhead on production facilities of certain departments as indicated by the plant utilisation budget", as the Study Material prints it; read with the plant utilisation budget, whose job is to show the overloaded processes, it points to the departments that need more capacity. Merits: (1) it outlines the capital development programme; (2) it sets priorities, with capital rationing when funds are short; (3) it controls expenditure; (4) it gives the figures for estimated return on capital employed; (5) it lets the cash budget be completed; (6) it helps cost reduction where modernisation is covered.

### Sales mix from a profit target

When a profit target drives the budget, as in ICAI's Illustration 5 (SM 15.50–15.54), the sales budget comes from contribution: desired contribution = fixed overheads + target profit; a "batch" is one set of the sales ratio (3 : 4 : 2); batches needed = desired contribution ÷ contribution per batch; each product's sales = batches × its ratio. Production, component usage, purchases and the manpower budget (hours ÷ hours per worker a month = workers) then follow the usual chain.

### Rejects, wastage and FIFO

Two variants ICAI has used: finished output partly rejected after production (production = good units needed ÷ (1 − rejection rate)) and material lost in process (purchases = net usage ÷ (1 − wastage rate)), both in MTP May 2024 Series I Q1. Illustration 4 (SM 15.46–15.49) goes one step further and values the quarterly issues of material in a priced stores ledger on FIFO ([[cma.mat.issues|pricing material issues]]).

::: exam The traps ICAI sets
- "Increase over budgeted sales" (May 2026): apply the percentage to the previous year's **budget**, not its actual sales.
- Closing stock "N days' sales" or "N days' consumption": divide by the days in the budget period (four 5-day weeks = 20 days).
- Down-time is a percentage of **productive** hours, which are standard hours ÷ efficiency ratio; overtime is what is paid beyond workers × normal hours, at rate × (1 + premium).
- Quarter IV's "20% of next quarter" is the given year-end closing stock.
- The question's instruction: "Working notes should form part of the answer."
:::

::: trick One line for every stock budget
Need = use + closing − opening. It gives production (use = sales of finished goods) and purchases (use = consumption of material). Condition: all three figures in the same unit and for the same period.
:::

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: the opening stock that was added
"Production = sales + closing stock + opening stock." The opening stock is already made: it reduces production. **Fix:** need = use + closing − opening, for finished goods and for materials alike.
:::

::: mistake Material bought for the sales instead of for production
Multiplying **sales** units by the material per unit. **Fix:** material usage comes from the **production** budget, which already includes the finished-goods stock change; then adjust for the material stocks.
:::

::: mistake The closing stock taken on the wrong base
"Closing stock of filling = 6 days of purchases" or "6 days of the opening stock". **Fix:** read the base the question gives (days' sales for products, days' consumption for materials) and divide by the working days in the period.
:::

::: mistake Overtime without the premium
**Fix:** hours beyond the normal week are paid at the basic rate plus the premium (₹100 × 140% in the samosa example). Down-time is added to productive hours before the overtime is found.
:::

## Practice

::: quiz Check yourself
Q: Tapri expects to sell 1,20,000 cartons of premix evenly through next year and wants to end the year with one month's sales in stock. It has 12,400 good cartons in stock now. 2% of the cartons produced fail the seal test after production and are destroyed. How many cartons must it produce?
= 120000
> Good cartons needed = 1,20,000 + 10,000 (one month's sales) − 12,400 = 1,17,600. Since only 98% of output is good, production = 1,17,600 ÷ 0.98 = 1,20,000. Adding 2% to 1,17,600 instead (1,19,952) is not enough: the rejects are 2% of production, not of good output.

Q: Case scenario. Ritu is budgeting Tapri's Diwali gift boxes. Sales are expected to be 30,000 boxes in October, 45,000 in November and 20,000 in December. Each month's closing stock is to be 40% of the next month's sales, and 10,000 boxes are in stock on 1 October. What is the production budget for November?
- [x] 35,000 boxes
- [ ] 43,000 boxes
- [ ] 27,000 boxes
- [ ] 71,000 boxes
> November: sales 45,000 + closing stock (40% of 20,000) 8,000 − opening stock (October's closing, 40% of 45,000) 18,000 = 35,000. 43,000 uses 1 October's stock as November's opening stock; 27,000 forgets the closing stock; 71,000 adds the opening stock.

Q: Production next quarter is 30,000 units, each needing 2 kg of material. The material stock is 8,000 kg and is to rise to 10,000 kg by the end of the quarter. How many kg must be purchased?
= 62000
> Usage = 30,000 × 2 = 60,000 kg; purchases = 60,000 + 10,000 − 8,000 = 62,000 kg.

Q: A seasonal business keeps its production the same every month, at the maximum possible, to use the plant fully and avoid laying off workers. Which statement about this policy is correct?
- [ ] Inventory carrying cost is the lowest possible
- [x] Capacity is fully utilised, but inventory carrying cost varies with the stocks held
- [ ] Stocks stay at the minimum level throughout the year
- [ ] It needs no production budget, only a sales budget
> Stabilised production uses the plant fully, so stocks build up in the off season and carrying cost varies with them. The lowest carrying cost belongs to stabilised stocks, which may under-utilise capacity.

Q: Which of these are factors for which past sales volumes are adjusted when preparing the sales budget? Select all that apply.
- [x] Backlog of unfulfilled sales orders
- [x] Planned advertising and promotion
- [ ] The depreciation method of the plant
- [x] Productive capacity
- [x] Competition
> ICAI's eight: backlog, planned advertising and promotion, industry and economic conditions, productive capacity, projected pricing, market research, relative product profitability, competition. The depreciation method does not change how much can be sold.

## Recap

- Functional budgets: one for each function (15 in ICAI's list), subsidiary to the master budget; the sales budget comes first.
- Production = budgeted sales + desired closing stock − opening stock (units); based on sales, capacity, stock change, outside purchase policy.
- Stabilised production: plant fully used, carrying cost varies; stabilised stocks: lowest carrying cost, capacity may be idle.
- Material usage = production × standard quantity; purchases = usage + closing stock − opening stock; governed by EOQ and the re-order point with safety stock.
- Labour: standard hours ÷ efficiency ratio = productive hours; + down-time = hours paid; beyond normal hours is overtime at the premium rate.
- Cost of goods sold = materials + labour + factory overhead + opening finished goods − closing finished goods (without the stock adjustments: production cost budget).
- Master budget (CIMA): the summary budget, incorporating its component functional budgets, which is finally approved, adopted and employed: budgeted P&L and balance sheet.
- Need = use + closing − opening, every time. (Barfi first, khoya second.)

## Flashcards

Q:: CIMA's definition of the master budget?
A:: The summary budget, incorporating its component functional budgets, which is finally approved, adopted and employed.

Q:: Format: production budget in units?
A:: Budgeted sales + desired closing stock = total quantity required; less opening stock = units to be produced.

Q:: Format: direct material purchase budget?
A:: Units required for production + desired closing stock = total requirement; less opening stock = units to be purchased; × unit price = purchase cost.

Q:: The production budget is based on which four things?
A:: The sales budget; the production capacity of the factory; the planned increase or decrease in finished stocks; the policy governing outside purchase.

Q:: Stabilised production versus stabilised stocks?
A:: Stabilised production: capacity fully utilised, inventory carrying cost varies with stocks held. Stabilised stocks: carrying cost lowest, but capacity may be under-utilised.

Q:: The four purposes of the plant utilisation budget?
A:: Determine the load on each process or machine group; show overloaded ones for corrective action; dovetail sales and production budgets where capacity cannot be raised; boost sales where capacity is surplus.

Q:: Two considerations that govern the material purchase budget?
A:: The economic order quantity, and the re-order point with safety stocks to cover fluctuations in demand.

Q:: The three merits of the direct labour budget?
A:: It defines the direct and indirect labour force required; lets the personnel department plan recruitment and training so labour turnover is minimised; reveals the labour cost for the manufacturing cost and cash budgets.

Q:: Hours to be paid when the efficiency ratio is 80% and down-time is 20% of productive hours?
A:: Productive hours = standard hours ÷ 0.80; hours paid = productive hours × 1.20. Hours beyond the normal hours are overtime.

Q:: Cost of goods sold budget versus production cost budget?
A:: Both add direct materials, direct labour and factory overhead; the cost of goods sold budget also adds opening and deducts closing finished goods. Without those adjustments it is the production cost budget.

Q:: Four methods of allocating research and development expenses?
A:: A percentage of total sales value; a percentage of net profit; a total sum from past experience and future R&D plans; a sum fixed by the cash resources available.

Q:: Is a purchases budget the same as a materials budget?
A:: No: a purchases budget is a wider concept and may include purchases of items other than materials.
