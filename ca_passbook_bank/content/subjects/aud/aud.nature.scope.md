## In plain words

### Kesar's wish list

It is the planning meeting for the 2025-26 audit of Tapri Tea Co. Ltd. Kesar has brought a list for Iyer & Associates.

> **Kesar:** Lakshmi-ji, four small jobs this year. Certify that the new kulhad kiln will last twenty years. Write up the accounts yourselves, my accountant is on leave. Find out which franchisee is hiding sales. And skip the notes to accounts. Nobody reads them.
> **Lakshmi:** Three of those are not audit work, Kesar-ji. The fourth is audit work, and it is the one you want me to skip.
> **Bunty:** Ma'am, I thought an auditor checks everything?
> **Lakshmi:** An auditor checks everything the opinion needs, Bunty. That is called the scope.

**Scope** means the range or reach of something. An audit exists to make users (shareholders, banks, the government, employees) more confident in the financial statements, and it does that through one thing: the auditor's **opinion** on whether those statements are prepared, in all material respects, in accordance with the rules that apply to them. So whatever that opinion needs is inside the scope: the audit must reach every part of the business that feeds the statements, the auditor must be satisfied that the records behind them can be relied on, and the statements must disclose properly what they should. The notes to accounts are part of the statements, so Kesar's "skip the notes" asks Lakshmi to skip her job.

Some things are outside, however useful they sound. Writing the accounts is management's job. Judging how long a kiln will last needs an engineer. Proving that a signature is genuine needs a document expert. Hunting for a suspected fraud is an **investigation**, a different assignment with a narrow, special purpose.

Two more ideas complete the picture. An **engagement** is the formal agreement under which an auditor agrees to provide the service, written down in an **engagement letter**. And the person doing all this needs more than accounting: ICAI lists the personal qualities of a good auditor, starting with tact and ending with reliability.

::: analogy The health check-up and the biopsy
An annual health check-up looks at every system that matters to your health, checks that the lab reports are reliable, and ends with the doctor's opinion. The doctor does not service your car, verify your degree certificate or tell the police who stole your wallet. If a lump is found, a biopsy follows: one specific test for one suspected problem. The check-up is the audit (general and broad); the biopsy is the investigation (specific and narrow).
:::

::: key
The scope of an audit is set by its purpose, an opinion on the financial statements as a whole. Everything that opinion needs (coverage of all relevant aspects, reliable and sufficient records, proper disclosure) is inside; preparing the statements, other professions' work, authenticating documents and investigations are outside.
:::

## How it works

### 1. Scope starts from the purpose

Scope refers to range or reach of something. The purpose of an audit is to **enhance the degree of confidence of intended users** in the financial statements. Users may be shareholders, employees, customers, government and regulatory authorities, bankers and others. That confidence is achieved by the expression of an opinion by the auditor on whether the financial statements are prepared, in all material respects, in accordance with an applicable financial reporting framework (SM 1.10).

::: definition Applicable financial reporting framework
A framework adopted in the preparation and presentation of the financial statements that is acceptable in view of the nature of the entity and the objective of the financial statements, or that is required by law or regulation. For companies in India, the financial reporting framework is provided under Schedule III of the Companies Act, 2013 (SM 1.10).
:::

### 2. What the scope includes: three points and the opinion

The Study Material lists these points, in this order (SM 1.10–1.11):

::: steps
1. **Coverage of all aspects of entity.** Audit of financial statements should be organised adequately to cover all aspects of the entity relevant to the financial statements being audited.
2. **Reliability and sufficiency of financial information.** The auditor should be reasonably satisfied that information contained in underlying accounting records and other source data (like bills, vouchers, documents) is reliable and a sufficient basis for preparation of financial statements. He judges this by making a study and assessment of accounting systems and internal controls and by carrying out appropriate tests, enquiries and procedures.
3. **Proper disclosure of financial information.** The auditor should decide whether relevant information is properly disclosed in the financial statements, keeping in mind the applicable statutory requirements. He does so by ensuring that the financial statements properly summarise the transactions and events recorded therein and by considering the judgments made by management in preparing them.
4. **Expression of an opinion on financial statements**, the fourth tick in ICAI's overview of the scope (SM 1.12).
:::

**Management's judgments, the auditor's evaluation.** Management, which is responsible for preparing and presenting the financial statements, makes many judgments: choosing appropriate accounting policies, such as the method of charging depreciation on fixed assets or the method of valuing inventories. ==The auditor evaluates selection and consistent application of accounting policies by management==: whether the selection is proper, and whether the chosen policy has been applied consistently on a period-to-period basis (SM 1.11).

**"All aspects" is not "every transaction".** Coverage means organising the audit so that nothing relevant to the financial statements is left out; it does not mean checking every voucher. The auditor does not test all transactions and balances, he forms his opinion by testing samples (SM 1.14–1.15). In the Study Material's Case Study 1, the student who concluded from "coverage of all aspects" that each and every transaction is verified is marked wrong, and so is the friend who agreed with him (SM 1.20–1.22).

**An audit looks at the past.** Financial statements are prepared on the basis of historical financial information, so the audit of financial statements is also based on historical financial information (SM 1.11). Purchases and sales shown in the statements are examples: they are transactions that have already occurred.

::: definition Historical financial information
Information expressed in financial terms in relation to a particular entity, derived primarily from that entity's accounting system, about economic events occurring in past time periods or about economic conditions or circumstances at points in time in the past (SM 1.11).
:::

Forecasts and projections are prospective financial information: assurance on them is a different engagement, covered in [[aud.nature.assurance|assurance engagements]].

### 3. What the scope does not include

ICAI's overview marks four things with a cross (SM 1.12–1.13):

- **Responsibility of preparation and presentation of financial statements.** That is the responsibility of the management of the entity; the auditor expresses an opinion on them (SM 1.6).
- **Duties outside the scope of competence of the auditor.** The auditor is not expected to perform duties which fall outside the domain of his competence. The physical condition of certain assets, like sophisticated machinery, cannot be determined by him; nor is he expected to determine the suitability and life of civil structures like buildings. These need different skill sets, those of qualified engineers in their fields.
- **Expertise in authentication of documents.** An auditor is not an expert in authentication of documents, so the genuineness of documents cannot be authenticated by him.
- **Investigation.** An audit is not an official investigation into alleged wrongdoing. The auditor does not have any specific legal powers of search or of recording statements of witnesses on oath, which may be necessary for an official investigation.

::: compare What the scope includes and excludes
| Included in the scope (✓) | Not included (✗) |
|---|---|
| Coverage of all aspects of the entity relevant to the financial statements | Preparation and presentation of the financial statements |
| Reliability and sufficiency of financial information | Duties outside the auditor's competence |
| Proper disclosure of financial information | Expertise in authentication of documents |
| Expression of an opinion on the financial statements | Investigation |
:::

### 4. Audit and investigation are different things

::: definition Investigation
"Investigation is a critical examination of the accounts with a special purpose." For example, if fraud is suspected and the auditor is specifically called upon to check the accounts to find whether fraud really exists, it takes the character of investigation (SM 1.12).
:::

The objective of audit, on the other hand, is to obtain reasonable assurance about whether the financial statements as a whole are free from material misstatement, whether due to fraud or error, thereby enabling the auditor to express an opinion. ==The scope of audit is general and broad whereas scope of investigation is specific and narrow== (SM 1.12).

::: compare Audit and investigation
| Point | Audit | Investigation |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Independent examination of financial information, to express an opinion | Critical examination of the accounts with a special purpose |
| Objective | Reasonable assurance that the statements as a whole are free from material misstatement | Whatever the special purpose is, e.g. whether a suspected fraud exists |
| Scope | General and broad | Specific and narrow |
| Legal powers of search or recording statements on oath | The auditor has none | May be necessary for an official investigation |
| Assurance | A reasonable assurance engagement | Not a reasonable assurance engagement (SM 1.41) |
:::

ICAI's own example (SM 1.13, 1.41): after a fire at a plant, an insurance company appoints a CA to verify the books and the circumstances surrounding the loss. The appointment has a specific objective, to determine the genuineness of the loss and any issue affecting the insurer's liability: it is an investigation, and the CA's report is not an audit report.

### 5. What is an engagement?

**Engagement** means an arrangement to do something. In the context of auditing, it means ==a formal agreement between auditor and client under which auditor agrees to provide auditing services==, and it takes the shape of an **engagement letter** (SM 1.17). The letter records, among other things, the objective and scope of the audit of the financial statements and the responsibilities of the auditor and of management (SM 11.16); the rules for agreeing those terms are in [[aud.ethics.sa210|SA 210]].

**External audit engagements.** Their purpose is to enhance the degree of confidence of intended users of financial statements, and they are reasonable assurance engagements. In India, companies are required to get their annual accounts audited by an external auditor; non-corporate entities may choose to have their accounts audited by an external auditor because of the benefits of an audit (SM 1.17).

Not every engagement a CA accepts is an audit. The Study Material's other types, each with its own family of Standards, are taken apart in [[aud.nature.assurance|assurance engagements and the engagement standards]]:

| Engagement | What it gives the user | Standards |
|---|---|---|
| Audit of financial statements | Reasonable assurance (high) | SAs |
| Review of financial statements | Limited assurance (lower than audit) | SREs |
| Examination of prospective financial information; report on controls | Assurance on matters other than historical financial information | SAEs |
| Agreed-upon procedures; compilation | Related services: no assurance in a compilation | SRSs |

### 6. Qualities of an auditor

An auditor is concerned with reporting on the financial matters of business and other institutions. Financial matters are inherently beset with the problems of human fallibility: errors and frauds are frequent. So the Study Material lists the qualities an auditor should have, in this order (SM 1.27–1.28):

**Tact, caution, firmness, good temper, integrity, discretion, industry, judgement, patience, clear headedness and reliability.** In short, all those personal qualities that go to make a good businessman contribute to the making of a good auditor. (*Industry* here means diligence, hard work; *discretion*, knowing what to say, to whom, and what to keep confidential.)

Then, in the Study Material's order:

- He must have the **shine of culture** for attaining a great height.
- He must have the **highest degree of integrity backed by adequate independence**.
- He holds a **position of trust**, so he must have the basic human qualities apart from the technical requirement of **professional training and education**.
- He is called upon constantly to critically review financial statements, and it is useless to attempt that task unless **his own knowledge is that of an expert**.
- An **exhaustive knowledge of accounting in all its branches is the sine qua non** (the indispensable condition) of the practice of auditing: he must know thoroughly all accounting principles and techniques.

## Worked examples

:::: example The notes nobody reads
Desai & Co. are the statutory auditors of Kaveri Kulhad Works Pvt Ltd, a supplier of clay cups to Tapri, for 2025-26. During the audit, the engagement partner raises three matters with management:

(i) Management asks the team to confine its work to the books at the Indore head office. The Khurja unit, which makes 30% of the company's output, keeps its accounts on spreadsheets that are added to the head office figures at the year end.

(ii) Freight expenses of ₹62 lakh are booked from the transporter's monthly statements; no lorry receipts or delivery records are kept.

(iii) The company has mortgaged its factory land as security for a bank loan taken by a firm owned by the managing director's brother. Nothing about it appears in the notes to accounts.

Management's reply to all three: "These matters are outside the scope of your audit." Comment.

::: answer Model answer
**Relevant provision.** The purpose of an audit is to enhance the degree of confidence of intended users in the financial statements, by expressing an opinion on whether they are prepared, in all material respects, in accordance with the applicable financial reporting framework. The scope of audit of financial statements includes:

1. **Coverage of all aspects of entity**: the audit should be organised adequately to cover all aspects of the entity relevant to the financial statements being audited.
2. **Reliability and sufficiency of financial information**: the auditor should be reasonably satisfied that information in the underlying accounting records and other source data (bills, vouchers, documents) is reliable and a sufficient basis for preparing the financial statements, judged by a study and assessment of accounting systems and internal controls and by appropriate tests, enquiries and procedures.
3. **Proper disclosure of financial information**: the auditor should decide whether relevant information is properly disclosed, keeping in mind the applicable statutory requirements, by ensuring that the financial statements properly summarise the transactions and events and by considering the judgments made by management.

**Facts and analysis.**

(i) The Khurja unit's figures form part of the company's financial statements. Leaving it out would leave an aspect of the entity relevant to the financial statements uncovered; the audit must be organised to cover it.

(ii) Freight of ₹62 lakh is supported only by the transporter's own statements. Whether the records are a reliable and sufficient basis for the expense is exactly what the auditor must judge, through his assessment of the accounting system and controls and appropriate tests and enquiries.

(iii) A mortgage of the company's land to secure a loan of another firm is relevant information about the company's assets and obligations. Whether it is properly disclosed, keeping in mind statutory requirements such as Schedule III, is part of the auditor's work even though no entry appears in the books.

**Conclusion.** Management's contention is not correct. All three matters fall well within the scope of the audit of financial statements, as they affect the coverage, the reliability of the information and its proper disclosure.
:::
::::

:::: example Four favours for the audit committee
Tapri's audit committee asks Iyer & Associates to include the following in the 2025-26 statutory audit. State, with reasons, whether each falls within the scope of the audit of financial statements.

(a) Estimating whether the roof of the Sanwer cold-storage building can safely carry a second tier of solar panels.

(b) Checking that the ₹4.20 crore of capital advances paid to a related party are presented and described in the financial statements as the framework requires.

(c) After a police complaint about a ₹65 lakh cash shortage at the Ujjain depot, establishing whether the depot manager took the money.

(d) Assessing whether the sales register generated by the café billing software can be relied upon as the basis for revenue.

::: answer Model answer
**(a) Outside the scope.** The auditor is not expected to perform duties which fall outside the domain of his competence. Determining the suitability and life of civil structures like buildings requires different skill sets, those of qualified engineers. The committee should engage a structural engineer.

**(b) Within the scope.** Proper disclosure of financial information is part of the scope: the auditor decides whether relevant information is properly disclosed in the financial statements, keeping in mind the applicable statutory requirements.

**(c) Outside the scope of the audit.** Investigation is a critical examination of the accounts with a special purpose; where fraud is suspected and the examination is specifically meant to find out whether it really exists, it takes the character of investigation. An audit is not an official investigation into alleged wrongdoing, and the auditor has no legal powers of search or of recording statements of witnesses on oath. The scope of audit is general and broad, that of investigation specific and narrow. The committee may commission an investigation as a separate assignment; it cannot be made part of the audit.

**(d) Within the scope.** The auditor must be reasonably satisfied that the information in the underlying accounting records is reliable and a sufficient basis for the financial statements, which he judges through a study and assessment of accounting systems and internal controls and appropriate tests, enquiries and procedures.

Hence (b) and (d) fall within the scope of the audit; (a) and (c) do not.
:::

## Under the hood

### "Not an expert in documents" is not "accept anything"

The exclusion means the auditor cannot certify a document as genuine. It does not let him ignore warning signs. The auditor may accept records and documents as genuine unless he has reason to believe the contrary; but he must consider the reliability of information used as evidence, and where there is doubt about its reliability or an indication of possible fraud, the SAs require him to investigate further and decide what modifications or additions to audit procedures are needed (SM 11.13). The flip side is an inherent limitation: management that fabricates documents may lead an auditor, who is not an expert in authentication, to accept invalid evidence (SM 1.15; [[aud.nature.limits|inherent limitations]]).

### A clause in the appointment letter does not change what an audit is

In the September 2024 paper, a company's appointment letter said the auditor "shall be responsible for detecting the frauds" that may happen during the year. ICAI's answer: the objective of the audit under SA 200 is reasonable assurance that the financial statements as a whole are free from material misstatement, whether due to fraud or error; an audit is not an official investigation; so the clause is uncalled for and outside the scope of audit (September 2024 Q1(c)). A special-purpose fraud examination, if the company wants one, is an investigation and needs its own engagement.

### Management prepares, the auditor evaluates

Choosing accounting policies is the responsibility of management; the role of the auditor lies in evaluating their selection and consistent application (SM 1.40). An auditor who writes up the accounts himself, as Kesar asked, steps into management's responsibility and would then audit his own work: auditors performing services that are themselves subject matters of audit is one of the Study Material's instances of a self-review threat (SM 11.10; [[aud.ethics.independence|independence, threats and safeguards]]).

## Answer frame

### A case: "Does the matter fall within the scope of audit?"

1. **Provision**: the purpose of an audit (enhancing the degree of confidence of intended users through an opinion), then the inclusion that decides the case, in the Study Material's words (coverage, reliability and sufficiency, proper disclosure), or the exclusion (competence, authentication, investigation).
2. **Facts**: "In the given case, …", with the figures.
3. **Analysis**: link each fact to its inclusion or exclusion.
4. **Conclusion**: "Hence, the matter falls well within the scope of audit; management's contention is not correct", or the reverse.

### "Explain the matters which fall outside the scope of an audit"

The four, each with ICAI's example: preparation and presentation (management's responsibility); duties outside competence (sophisticated machinery, civil structures: engineers); authentication of documents (not an expert); investigation (no powers of search or of recording statements on oath; general and broad versus specific and narrow).

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: Bunty's every-voucher audit
Bunty reads "coverage of all aspects of entity" and plans to tick all 48,000 sales invoices. **Fix:** coverage means organising the audit to cover every aspect relevant to the financial statements; the auditor tests samples and does not verify each and every transaction (SM 1.14–1.15, Case Study 1).
:::

::: mistake "Notes and disclosures are the company secretary's business"
Treating an omitted disclosure as outside the audit because there is no entry in the books. **Fix:** proper disclosure of financial information is one of the three inclusions, judged against statutory requirements too.
:::

::: mistake Calling a fraud hunt an audit
Answering "yes, the auditor must find the fraud" when the board asks for a special-purpose check of suspected wrongdoing. **Fix:** that is an investigation; an audit is not an official investigation, and its scope is general and broad, not specific and narrow.
:::

::: mistake The auditor who certifies the machine
Agreeing to certify the condition or remaining life of plant, or the genuineness of a signature. **Fix:** both are outside the auditor's competence or expertise; the work belongs to engineers or document experts.
:::

## Recap

- Purpose of audit: enhance the degree of confidence of intended users, by an opinion on whether the financial statements are prepared, in all material respects, in accordance with the applicable financial reporting framework (Schedule III for companies).
- Scope includes: coverage of all aspects of the entity relevant to the financial statements; reliability and sufficiency of financial information; proper disclosure of financial information; the opinion.
- The auditor evaluates selection and consistent application of accounting policies; management chooses them.
- Audit is based on historical financial information.
- Scope excludes: preparation and presentation (management); duties outside competence (machinery, buildings); authentication of documents; investigation.
- Investigation: a critical examination of the accounts with a special purpose. Audit general and broad, investigation specific and narrow.
- Engagement: a formal agreement under which the auditor agrees to provide auditing services, in an engagement letter; external audit engagements are reasonable assurance engagements.
- Qualities: tact, caution, firmness, good temper, integrity, discretion, industry, judgement, patience, clear headedness, reliability; integrity backed by independence; accounting knowledge the sine qua non. (Kesar's kiln still needs an engineer.)

Q:: Define historical financial information.
A:: Information expressed in financial terms in relation to a particular entity, derived primarily from its accounting system, about economic events in past periods or economic conditions or circumstances at points in time in the past.

Q:: What is an engagement, in the context of auditing?
A:: A formal agreement between auditor and client under which the auditor agrees to provide auditing services; it takes the shape of an engagement letter.

Q:: External audit engagements: purpose and type?
A:: To enhance the degree of confidence of intended users of financial statements; they are reasonable assurance engagements.

Q:: List the qualities of an auditor in ICAI's order.
A:: Tact, caution, firmness, good temper, integrity, discretion, industry, judgement, patience, clear headedness and reliability.

Q:: What is the sine qua non of the practice of auditing?
A:: An exhaustive knowledge of accounting in all its branches.
