## In plain words

### The drawer of April invoices

Tapri's purchases of cardamom went up this year, yet its trade payables at 31 March fell by ₹6 crore. Bunty thinks this is good news.

> **Bunty:** Ma'am, payables are down. Tapri must be paying faster!
> **Lakshmi:** If an asset fell, I would ask whether it was lost. When a liability falls, I ask who we forgot.
> **Kesar:** *(from the doorway)* The Kerala bills came in April, Lakshmi-ji. April's headache.
> **Lakshmi:** The cardamom came in March, Kesar-ji. So did the liability.

With assets the auditor fears **overstatement**: stock or debtors that do not exist. With liabilities the usual fear is the reverse, **understatement**: a supplier's bill kept in a drawer, a tax payable left out, a lawsuit nobody mentioned. Fewer liabilities make the balance sheet look stronger and profit higher, so the auditor goes looking for what is **missing**: asking suppliers directly, matching goods received before the year end with bills recorded, and reading what was paid in April.

**Provisions** are liabilities that can be measured only by a substantial degree of estimation, such as warranties or a court case. There the question is not only "is it recorded?" but "is the estimate reasonable?": the auditor tests management's working, the expert's report and the lawyer's view.

::: analogy The kirana store's udhaar khata
To find out what a family really owes, do not ask the family: ask the shopkeeper, whose khata records every packet given on credit. Better still, do not tell him the family's figure and ask him to agree; ask him to tell you what his khata says. That is a creditor's confirmation with the balance left blank, and the Study Material calls it the preferable form.
:::

::: key
For liabilities, the auditor hunts for what is **not** in the books: confirmations from creditors (even those with small balances but large purchases), cut-off between goods received and invoices recorded, and payments and bills after the year end. For provisions, the auditor tests the **estimate**.
:::

## How it works

### 1. Which liabilities, and why they matter

Liabilities other than borrowings (see [[aud.items.equity|borrowings]]) include trade payables and other current liabilities, deferred payment credits and provisions. A liability is current if it is due to be paid within twelve months and held primarily for the purpose of being traded in the entity's normal operating cycle; for example short-term debt and dividends. Verification of liabilities is as important as that of assets, since any liability omitted, understated or overstated means the financial statements would not show a true and fair view (SM 5.71–5.72).

### 2. Existence of trade payables and other current liabilities

The procedures (SM 5.72–5.74):

1. Check that **controls** ensure that no purchase or expense invoice is recorded more than once, and that payable balances are automatically recorded in the general ledger when the expense is recorded.
2. Obtain the **accounts payable ageing report** and trace its balances to the general ledger; investigate reconciling items; examine journal entries, especially large ones.
3. **Direct confirmation**: contact vendors directly and independently to confirm the amounts payable at the period end. This should necessarily be done for ==all significant payable balances and for parties from whom material purchases were made during the period, even if their period-end balance is not significant==.
4. If there are **related party payables**, review whether they were properly authorised and the transactions reasonable and at arm's length.
5. Review a **trend line** of purchases or expenses and accounts payable, or a comparison of the two over time, for unusual trends, and enquire about the reasons.

**How the confirmation procedure is run** (SM 5.72–5.74; see [[aud.evid.sa505|SA 505]]):

- The auditor uses it **with the consent of the entity**. If management asks the auditor not to seek confirmation from certain trade payables, the auditor considers whether there are **valid grounds**, and in appropriate cases reconsiders the nature, timing and extent of procedures, including the degree of planned reliance on management's representations.
- Balances may be confirmed **as at the balance sheet date, or as at another date reasonably close to it**, decided by the auditor **in consultation with the company**.
- The request may show the balance and ask whether the creditor agrees, or show **no balance** and ask the creditor to state the balance as per its records. **The form with no balance is preferable.**
- The method of selecting creditors is **not revealed** to the company until the trial balance of the payables ledger is handed over. The entity prepares properly addressed letters for the creditors selected; the auditor keeps strict control over their correctness and despatch (or fills in names, addresses and amounts on authorised letters and mails them directly); replies and undelivered letters come back **to the auditor, not to the client**.
- **Discrepancies** may bear on accounts outside the sample: the entity investigates and reconciles them, and the auditor considers further tests on the payables as a whole.
- **Where no reply is received**, perform additional testing: test **subsequent payments** to those creditors; agree the balance to the underlying **vendor invoices**; prepare a detailed analysis of the balance, ensuring it consists of identifiable transactions that actually occurred (examination in depth).

### 3. Completeness: the search for unrecorded liabilities

The procedures (SM 5.74–5.76):

1. **Cut-off**: for the **last 5 invoices** received or recorded at the reporting date and included in trade payables, the goods should have been received or the risks and rewards transferred to the entity; and **all goods received before the year end** should be booked as purchases and included in trade creditors.
2. Test purchases and expenses on a **sample from the payables ledgers** with their supporting documents, for correct amounts and dates.
3. **Match purchase invoice dates to gate entry (inward) dates**, including invoices received after the period, to see whether they belong to the period under audit.
4. **Review subsequent expense vouchers**: all material vouchers recorded after the balance sheet date, to see whether they relate to the audit period.
5. **Advances from customers / revenue received in advance**: obtain a customer-wise listing with ageing and nature; enquire about disputes and any additional liability; verify the documents on which the advance was received.
6. **Statutory dues** (TDS, GST, luxury tax, professional tax, PF and ESI payable): prepare a **reasonability** against sales, purchases or employee benefit expenses. For example, the last month's GST liability is computed by applying the applicable rate to sales, and the last month's PF by applying the rate to the employee benefit expense; variances are explained by the client and documented. Verify the **challans for deposits made after the period end** for all statutory liabilities at the balance sheet date, and analyse differences from the liability recorded.
7. Prepare a complete list of statutory dues and consider the reporting requirements under [[aud.report.caro|CARO 2020]].

### 4. Valuation

The procedures (SM 5.76–5.77):

- Review the company's process for identifying old creditor balances or liabilities to be **written back**: consistency with last year's method and its appropriateness to the business.
- Obtain the **ageing** of payables and the list of vendors with **disputes** and customer claims under litigation, and compare with the previous year.
- Check that **write-backs** of balances no longer payable are **approved by an appropriate, authorised member of senior management**, for example the CEO or MD.
- Check that **foreign currency payables** are restated properly as per AS 11.
- Understand management's process for identifying the **principal and interest unpaid to Micro, Small and Medium Enterprise suppliers** at the year end; test-check it to decide whether the auditor can rely on it.

### 5. Provisions

A **provision** is a liability which can be measured only by using a substantial degree of estimation. It is recognised when (i) an entity has a **present obligation** (legal or constructive) as a result of a past event; (ii) it is **probable** that an outflow of resources embodying economic benefits will be required to settle it; and (iii) a **reliable estimate** can be made of the amount. If these conditions are not met, no provision is recognised. Examples: provisions for litigation and for warranties (SM 5.85).

A **contingent liability** is a possible obligation from past events whose existence will be confirmed only by uncertain future events not wholly within the entity's control; or a present obligation not recognised because an outflow is not probable or the amount cannot be measured with sufficient reliability (SM 5.85). Its audit is the subject of [[aud.items.contingent|the audit of contingent liabilities]].

The procedures for provisions (SM 5.85–5.87):

1. **Existence**: obtain a list of all provisions and compare it with the ledger balances.
2. **Completeness**: inspect the underlying agreements, such as agreements with customers for **warranty** commitments, and any legal and other claims on the entity (**litigations**).
3. **Valuation**: obtain management's working and basis for each provision and verify that it is complete and accurate.
4. Where required, obtain the **expert's report**, calculation and working: for a complex warranty, some entities use an **actuary**; for a matter under legal dispute, obtain the **legal expert's assessment** of the likelihood of a liability devolving on the entity (**probable, possible or remote**). Verify the expert's assumptions against the data shared by management.
5. When using the work of a **management's expert**, as per [[aud.evid.sa500|SA 500]] the auditor:
   - evaluates the expert's **competence, capabilities and objectivity**: employed by the entity or an outside party; independent of the entity; the auditor's previous experience of the expert's work; the expert's knowledge, qualification, membership of a professional body or industry association;
   - obtains an **understanding of the expert's work**: whether the auditor has the expertise to evaluate it; the assumptions and methods used; the internal or external data used;
   - evaluates the **appropriateness of the work as audit evidence** for the assertion: relevance and reasonableness of the findings; relevance, completeness and accuracy of the source data.
6. Obtain a **written representation** from management that it has made all the provisions required by the recognised accounting principles (see [[aud.complete.sa580|SA 580]]).

## Formats and formulas

*Reproduce these in ICAI's order: they are asked as "state the disclosures the auditor should verify".*

### Trade payables (Schedule III, Part I; SM 5.77–5.79)

**Micro and small enterprises**: whether the notes disclose

1. the principal amount and the interest due on it (shown separately) remaining unpaid to any supplier at the end of each accounting year;
2. the amount of interest paid by the buyer in terms of section 16 of the MSMED Act, 2006, with the amount of payments made to the supplier beyond the appointed day during the year;
3. the interest due and payable for the period of delay in making payment (paid, but beyond the appointed day during the year) but without adding the interest specified under the Act;
4. the interest accrued and remaining unpaid at the end of each accounting year;
5. the further interest remaining due and payable even in succeeding years, until the date the interest dues above are actually paid to the small enterprise, for disallowance as a deductible expenditure under section 23 of the Act.

**Trade payables ageing schedule** (amounts in ₹), outstanding for the following periods **from the due date of payment**:

| Particulars | Below 1 yr | 1–2 yrs | 2–3 yrs | Above 3 yrs | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| (i) MSME | | | | | |
| (ii) Others | | | | | |
| (iii) Disputed dues – MSME | | | | | |
| (iv) Disputed dues – Others | | | | | |

Where no due date of payment is specified, the periods run from the date of the transaction; **unbilled dues** are disclosed separately.

**Other current liabilities**: whether the amounts disclosed are classified as (SM 5.79–5.80):

1. current maturities of finance lease obligations;
2. interest accrued but not due on borrowings;
3. interest accrued and due on borrowings;
4. income received in advance;
5. unpaid dividends;
6. application money received for allotment of securities and due for refund, and interest accrued thereon;
7. unpaid matured deposits and interest accrued thereon;
8. unpaid matured debentures and interest accrued thereon;
9. others (specify nature).

**Share application money** (item 6) includes advances towards allotment of share capital. The auditor checks that the notes disclose (SM 5.79–5.80):

- the terms and conditions, including the number of shares proposed to be issued, the amount of premium, if any, and the period before which the shares shall be allotted;
- whether the company has sufficient authorised capital to cover the share capital resulting from allotment of shares out of such money;
- the period for which the money has been pending beyond the period for allotment stated in the document inviting applications, with the reason it is pending.

Share application money not exceeding the issued capital and to the extent not refundable is shown under the head **Equity**; the amount refundable (in excess of subscription, or where the minimum subscription requirements are not met) is shown separately under **"Other current liabilities"**.

### Provisions (Schedule III and AS 29; SM 5.87–5.89)

**Long-term provisions** and **short-term provisions**: each classified as (a) provision for employee benefits; (b) others (specify nature).

**Contingent liabilities and commitments** (to the extent not provided for):

- Contingent liabilities: (a) claims against the company not acknowledged as debt; (b) guarantees; (c) other money for which the company is contingently liable.
- Commitments: (a) estimated amount of contracts remaining to be executed on capital account and not provided for; (b) uncalled liability on shares and other investments partly paid; (c) other commitments (specify nature).

**AS 29, for each class of provision**:

- the carrying amount at the beginning and end of the period;
- additional provisions made in the period, including increases to existing provisions;
- amounts used (incurred and charged against the provision) during the period;
- unused amounts reversed during the period;
- a brief description of the nature of the obligation and the expected timing of outflows;
- an indication of the uncertainties about the amount or timing (with the major assumptions about future events, where necessary);
- the amount of any expected reimbursement, stating any asset recognised for it.

**For each class of contingent liability**, unless the possibility of an outflow is remote: a brief description and, where practicable, an estimate of its financial effect, an indication of the uncertainties, and the possibility of any reimbursement; if any of this is not practicable to give, that fact is stated.

## Worked examples

::::: example Five slips at the year end
Iyer & Associates audit Tapri for 2025-26; overall materiality is ₹5 crore, and closing inventory is the physical count in Tapri's warehouses on 31 March 2026. Bunty's cut-off test covers the last goods receipt notes (GRNs) of the year and the last invoices recorded in trade payables. INV-9902 (Assam tea) is on terms "delivered at Tapri's Indore warehouse", so risks and rewards pass on delivery; INV-9907 (premix pouches) is "ex-works", so they pass when the goods leave the supplier's factory, which these did on 30 March. Bunty's findings are in the table below; of these, only GRNs 4473 and 4475 are not included in 2025-26 trade payables:

| Ref | Goods received | Invoice date | ₹ |
|---|---|---|---|
| GRN 4471 | 27 Mar | 27 Mar | 38,40,000 |
| GRN 4473 | 30 Mar | 2 Apr | 61,00,000 |
| GRN 4475 | 31 Mar | 4 Apr | 46,50,000 |
| INV-9902 | 3 Apr | 29 Mar | 52,60,000 |
| INV-9907 | 2 Apr | 30 Mar | 74,90,000 |

Separately, Dev asks the auditors not to send a confirmation request to Khurja Clay Crafts, the kulhad supplier: "We are negotiating a price-escalation claim with them, and a letter from the auditors now would weaken our position." And Malabar Spice Traders, who supplied ₹11 crore of spices during the year but show a year-end balance of only ₹4 lakh, have not replied to the confirmation request.

Required:

(a) Comment on the cut-off findings and their effect on trade payables.

(b) How should the auditor deal with Dev's request?

(c) What should the auditor do about Malabar Spice Traders?

:::: answer Model answer
**(a) Cut-off.** As per the Study Material, for the last invoices recorded in trade payables the goods should have been received or the risks and rewards of ownership transferred in favour of the entity; all goods received before the year end should be booked as purchases and included in trade creditors; and purchase invoice dates are matched to gate entry dates to check that purchases are recorded in the correct period (SM 5.75). Applying these tests:

- **GRNs 4473 and 4475**: the goods were received before the year end, but their April invoices were not recorded. Purchases and trade payables are understated by ₹1,07,50,000; as the goods were in the 31 March count, profit is overstated by the same amount.
- **INV-9902**: recorded as a 2025-26 purchase although the goods arrived on 3 April and the risks and rewards passed only on delivery. Purchases and trade payables are overstated by ₹52,60,000; as the goods were not in the count, profit is understated by the same amount.
- **INV-9907**: correctly recorded, since the risks and rewards passed on 30 March when the goods left the supplier's factory. The auditor should check that these goods in transit are included in closing inventory ([[aud.items.current|audit of inventories]]).
- **GRN 4471**: correctly recorded.

The auditor should ask management to correct them, extend the cut-off testing (invoices received and expense vouchers recorded after the year end) to see whether the errors are more widespread, and accumulate any uncorrected misstatements for evaluation.

**(b) Dev's request.** The direct confirmation procedure is employed with the consent of the entity; where management requests the auditor not to seek confirmation from certain trade payables, the auditor should consider whether there are valid grounds for the request, and may need to reconsider the nature, timing and extent of audit procedures, including the degree of planned reliance on management's representations (SM 5.72–5.73). As per [[aud.evid.sa505|SA 505]], the auditor shall (i) inquire as to management's reasons for the refusal and seek audit evidence as to their validity and reasonableness; (ii) evaluate the implications of the refusal on the assessment of the risks of material misstatement, including the risk of fraud, and on the nature, timing and extent of other audit procedures; and (iii) perform alternative audit procedures designed to obtain relevant and reliable audit evidence (SM 4.82–4.84).

An ongoing negotiation with the confirming party is a common reason advanced for such a request and may be valid, so the auditor should see evidence of it, such as the correspondence on the price-escalation claim. If the reason is accepted, the alternative procedures for payables include examining subsequent cash disbursements, correspondence from third parties and other records such as goods received notes. The claim itself may mean an additional liability, so the auditor should also consider whether a provision or a contingent liability disclosure is needed. If the auditor concludes that the refusal is unreasonable (which may indicate a fraud risk factor to be evaluated under SA 240), or cannot obtain relevant and reliable audit evidence from alternative procedures, the auditor shall communicate with those charged with governance in accordance with SA 260 and determine the implications for the audit and the opinion in accordance with SA 705.

**(c) Malabar Spice Traders.** Confirmation is necessary for parties from whom material purchases were made during the period, even if the period-end balance is not significant (SM 5.72). Where no reply is received, the auditor should perform additional testing: test payments made to Malabar after the year end; agree the balance to the underlying vendor invoices; and prepare a detailed analysis of the balance, ensuring it consists of identifiable transactions that actually occurred (examination in depth) (SM 5.74). A small balance after ₹11 crore of purchases also calls for a review of the purchases-to-payables trend and of the cut-off for this supplier.

::::: example The compressor that promised too much
Tapri sells "ChaiMate" vending machines to offices with a one-year warranty. The warranty provision at 1 April 2025 was ₹60 lakh; claims of ₹48 lakh were paid against it during the year and the unused balance lapsed with the warranty period. In 2025-26 Tapri sold 24,000 machines, all fitted with a new compressor. Management has provided ₹28,80,000 for their warranty (a 2% failure rate × ₹6,000 average repair cost); the 2% comes from the compressor maker's brochure, which promises "up to 60% fewer failures" than the old model. Kesar: "New compressor, new life." The repair register for the old model shows failure rates of 4.8%, 5.1% and 5.1% over the last three years; no test reports or claims history exist yet for the new compressor. Tapri's legal counsel has also assessed three other matters:

(i) a show-cause notice from a food-safety authority over a mislabelled batch of premix: a penalty is probable and, based on the authority's past orders, counsel estimates it at ₹38 lakh;

(ii) a guarantee of ₹1.50 crore given to Narmada Bank for a franchisee's term loan: the franchisee pays its instalments, but its café is making losses, and counsel considers an outflow possible but not probable;

(iii) a claim of ₹25 lakh by a former landlord for "loss of goodwill" after Tapri vacated a café when its lease expired: counsel considers an outflow remote.

Required:

(a) State the audit procedures for these provisions.

(b) Comment on management's warranty estimate and prepare the warranty provision movement for disclosure on the basis of the evidence.

(c) State the treatment of the three other matters.

:::: answer Model answer
**(a) Procedures.** Obtain a list of all provisions and compare it with the ledger balances; inspect the underlying agreements, such as the warranty terms with customers, and the legal and other claims on Tapri; obtain management's working and basis for each provision and verify that it is complete and accurate; where required, obtain the expert's report, calculation and working, and the legal expert's assessment of whether each liability is probable, possible or remote, and verify the underlying assumptions with the data shared by management; evaluate the competence, capabilities and objectivity of any management's expert, understand the expert's work and evaluate its appropriateness as audit evidence ([[aud.evid.sa500|SA 500]]); and obtain a written representation that all provisions required by the recognised accounting principles have been made (SM 5.85–5.87).

**(b) The warranty estimate.** The only data available, the repair register, supports a failure rate of about 5% (average of 4.8%, 5.1% and 5.1%). The 2% rests on a brochure's "up to" claim, with no test reports or claims history: the assumption is not supported by the data shared by management. The auditor should ask management to support it (for example with the compressor maker's test data or an expert's evaluation) or to revise the estimate. On the evidence available, the provision for 2025-26 sales is 24,000 × 5% × ₹6,000 = ₹72,00,000, so the provision recorded is understated by ₹43,20,000; if not corrected, this is a misstatement to be evaluated. Movement on the evidence-based estimate:

(ii) The guarantee is an obligation, but an outflow is not probable: **no provision**; disclose a contingent liability under "Guarantees" with its nature and, where practicable, an estimate of its financial effect (₹1.50 crore), an indication of the uncertainties and the possibility of any reimbursement. ## Under the hood

### Taxes: the provision and the payment (SM 5.128–5.129)

**Provision for income tax**:

1. Obtain the computation of income and income tax prepared by the entity and verify whether it is as per the Income-tax Act, 1961 and the Rules made thereunder.
2. Review adjustments, expenses, disallowed special rebates, etc., with particular reference to the last available completed assessment.
3. Examine relevant records and documents pertaining to advance tax, self-assessment tax and other demands.
4. Compute tax payable as per the latest applicable rates in the Finance Act.
5. Ensure that the overall provision on the date of the balance sheet is adequate, having regard to the current year provision, advance tax paid, assessment orders, etc.
6. Ensure that the requirements of AS 22 on Accounting for Taxes on Income have been appropriately followed for the period under audit.

**Vouching of payment of taxes**:

1. Payment of income tax and other taxes consequent upon a regular assessment: verify with the copy of the assessment order, assessment form, notice of demand and the receipted challan.
2. Payments or advance payments of income tax: verify with the notice of demand and the receipted challan acknowledging the amount paid.
3. Interest allowed on advance payments of income tax is included as income; penal interest charged for non-payment is debited to the interest account.
4. Electronic payment of taxes (by internet banking, or credit or debit cards) is now common.
5. The entity may pay taxes electronically from the account of any other person, but the challan must clearly show the Permanent Account Number (PAN) of the assessee on whose behalf the payment is made: the auditor checks this.
6. Payment need not be made from the entity's own account in an authorised bank; while vouching such e-payments, the auditor cross-verifies the payment through the receipted challan with the PAN / TAN.

### Additional Regulatory Information printed after provisions (SM 5.90–5.91)

**1. Benami property held.** Where proceedings have been initiated or are pending against the company for holding any benami property under the Benami Transactions (Prohibition) Act, 1988 and the rules made thereunder, the company discloses:

- (a) details of the property, including the year of acquisition;
- (b) its amount;
- (c) details of beneficiaries;
- (d) if the property is in the books, the reference to the item in the balance sheet;
- (e) if it is not in the books, that fact, with reasons;
- (f) where the proceedings are against the company as an abettor of the transaction or as the transferor, the details;
- (g) the nature of the proceedings, their status and the company's view on them.

**3. Ratios.** (a) Current ratio, (b) debt-equity ratio, (c) debt service coverage ratio, (d) return on equity ratio, (e) inventory turnover ratio, (f) trade receivables turnover ratio, (g) trade payables turnover ratio, (h) net capital turnover ratio, (i) net profit ratio, (j) return on capital employed, (k) return on investment. The company explains the items included in the numerator and the denominator of each, and explains any change in a ratio by **more than 25%** compared with the preceding year.

::: trick Payables' four rows, receivables' four rows
**Payables** ageing rows: MSME, Others, Disputed MSME, Disputed Others; periods: below 1 year, 1–2, 2–3, above 3 years. **Receivables** rows: undisputed good, undisputed doubtful, disputed good, disputed doubtful; periods add "less than 6 months" and "6 months to 1 year". Condition: both run from the due date of payment (or the transaction date if none), with unbilled dues separate.
:::

## Answer frame

### "Explain the procedures for [existence / completeness / valuation] of trade payables"

1. **State the assertion** in the Study Material's words ("Trade payables and liability balances that were supposed to be recorded have been recognised": completeness).
2. **List that assertion's procedures** in ICAI's order (for completeness: cut-off on the last 5 invoices, sample from ledgers, invoice dates against gate entry, subsequent vouchers, customer advances, statutory dues reasonability and challans, CARO list).
3. **Apply them to the facts**: invoices near the year end, the creditor who did not reply, the dispute.
4. **Close** with the conclusion and, where asked, the disclosure (MSME, ageing).

### "Management refuses a confirmation request"

1. **Provision**: consent of the entity and valid grounds (SM 5.72–5.73); SA 505's three steps: reasons and their validity, effect on risk (including fraud), alternative procedures.
2. **Facts**: the reason given (a dispute, a negotiation) and the size of the balance or purchases.
3. **Analysis**: is the reason supported by evidence; which alternative procedures (subsequent payments, correspondence, goods received notes).
4. **Conclusion**: if the refusal is unreasonable or evidence cannot be obtained, communicate with those charged with governance (SA 260) and consider the opinion (SA 705).

### A provision case

1. **Provision**: the three recognition conditions; the expert and representation procedures.
2. **Facts**: the obligation, the probability, the estimate.
3. **Analysis**: provision, contingent liability disclosure, or nothing; compute the movement.
4. **Conclusion**: the treatment and the AS 29 disclosure.

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: celebrating a fall in payables
Treating lower trade payables as good news without asking why. **Fix:** for liabilities the risk is understatement; test cut-off, invoices and payments after the year end, and confirm with creditors from whom material purchases were made.
:::

::: mistake Confirming only the big balances
**Fix:** confirmations go to all significant balances **and** to parties with material purchases during the year, even if their year-end balance is small: a small balance after large purchases is itself a warning.
:::

::: mistake Hall of Shame: "They cancel out, Ma'am"
Netting an unrecorded March purchase against an April purchase wrongly booked in March, and calling the cut-off clean because the net figure is small. **Fix:** each is a separate misstatement of purchases and trade payables (and usually of profit); ask for both to be corrected, extend the cut-off test, and accumulate whatever remains uncorrected.
:::

::: mistake A representation in place of a reply
Accepting management's written representation when a creditor does not reply. **Fix:** perform the alternative procedures: subsequent payments, vendor invoices, detailed analysis of the balance.
:::

::: mistake Providing for everything the lawyer mentions
**Fix:** provide only when there is a present obligation, a probable outflow and a reliable estimate; disclose a contingent liability when the outflow is possible; do nothing when it is remote.
:::

- [ ] A tax demand under appeal where an outflow is only possible

> A provision needs a present obligation from a past event, a probable outflow and a reliable estimate; the warranty meets all three. The others are contingent liabilities to disclose, or (if remote) nothing.

## Recap

- The main risk for liabilities is understatement, and verifying them is as important as verifying assets; an omitted liability makes the balance sheet look stronger and profit higher.
- Existence rests on direct confirmation of all significant balances and of parties with material purchases, even if their balance is small; also controls against double recording, the ageing report traced to the ledger, related party payables at arm's length, and the purchases-to-payables trend.
- The confirmation form with no balance is preferable, and replies come to the auditor, not the client; the entity consents; a "do not confirm" request needs valid grounds (SA 505: reasons, risk, alternative procedures); the date is at or near the year end, set in consultation; selection is not revealed; discrepancies are reconciled; non-replies: subsequent payments, invoices, detailed analysis.
- Completeness: last 5 invoices (goods received or risks and rewards passed) and goods received before the year end; invoice dates against gate entries; subsequent vouchers; customer advances; statutory dues reasonability and later challans; CARO list. (Kesar's April bills are March's liability.)
- Valuation: write-back process and approval by senior management (CEO/MD); ageing and disputes; forex restatement (AS 11); MSME principal and interest process.
- Provisions: present obligation + probable outflow + reliable estimate; working, expert (actuary, legal: probable, possible, remote), SA 500 evaluation of a management's expert, written representation; AS 29 movement and narrative disclosures.
- Disclosures: MSME (five items), payables ageing (four rows, from due date, unbilled separately), other current liabilities (nine heads), share application money refundable under other current liabilities; ARI: benami property, struck off companies, eleven ratios with reasons for changes over 25%.

## Flashcards

Q:: Trade payables: to whom must confirmations be sent?
A:: All significant payable balances at the period end, and parties from whom material purchases were made during the period even if their period-end balance is not significant.

Q:: Which form of confirmation request is preferable for trade payables?
A:: The form with no balance, asking the creditor to state the balance as per its records.

Q:: What does the auditor do if management asks it not to confirm certain trade payables?
A:: Considers whether there are valid grounds; in appropriate cases reconsiders the nature, timing and extent of procedures, including the degree of planned reliance on management's representations. Under SA 505: inquire into the reasons and seek evidence of their validity, evaluate the effect on risk (including fraud) and other procedures, and perform alternative procedures.

Q:: What additional testing is done when a creditor does not reply to a confirmation?
A:: Test subsequent payments; agree the balance to the vendor invoices; prepare a detailed analysis of the balance and confirm the transactions occurred (examination in depth).

Q:: List the cut-off procedures for trade payables.
A:: For the last 5 invoices recorded at the reporting date, goods received or risks and rewards transferred; all goods received before the year end booked as purchases and creditors; invoice dates matched to gate entry dates.

Q:: How does the auditor test statutory dues for completeness?
A:: A reasonability of the last month's liability (rate × sales for GST, rate × employee benefit expense for PF), variances explained; challans for deposits after the period end verified; a complete list prepared for CARO 2020.

Q:: Who should approve the write-back of old creditor balances?
A:: An appropriate and authorised member of senior management, for example the CEO or MD.

Q:: When is a provision recognised?
A:: When there is a present obligation (legal or constructive) from a past event, an outflow of resources is probable, and a reliable estimate of the amount can be made.

Q:: What does the auditor evaluate when using the work of a management's expert for a provision?
A:: The expert's competence, capabilities and objectivity; an understanding of the expert's work (assumptions, methods, data); and the appropriateness of the work as audit evidence (relevance, reasonableness, source data).

Q:: AS 29: what movement is disclosed for each class of provision?
A:: Carrying amount at the beginning and end; additional provisions, including increases; amounts used; unused amounts reversed.

Q:: Schedule III: the rows of the trade payables ageing schedule?
A:: (i) MSME; (ii) Others; (iii) Disputed dues – MSME; (iv) Disputed dues – Others; periods from the due date: below 1 year, 1–2, 2–3, above 3 years; unbilled dues separately.

Q:: Schedule III: list four items of other current liabilities.
A:: Any of: current maturities of finance lease obligations; interest accrued but not due on borrowings; interest accrued and due; income received in advance; unpaid dividends; application money due for refund with interest; unpaid matured deposits and debentures with interest.
