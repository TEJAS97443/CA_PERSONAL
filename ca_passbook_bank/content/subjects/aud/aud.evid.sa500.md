## In plain words

### Four hundred photocopies of one voice

March has ended at Tapri Tea Co. Ltd, and Iyer & Associates are testing a large sale booked on 31 March to Chai Junction Distributors, a firm run by Kesar's brother-in-law. Bunty arrives with a box file and a proud tick on the cover.

> **Bunty:** Ma'am, 400 pages! Every delivery challan, photocopied, and the sales team's summary sheet.
> **Lakshmi:** Who made the challans?
> **Bunty:** Our client's dispatch office.
> **Lakshmi:** Then you have four hundred copies of one voice, Bunty. Evidence. Where is the evidence?

**Audit evidence** is the information the auditor uses to reach the conclusions on which the opinion rests. Lakshmi's problem is not the *amount* of paper but its *quality*: every page was made by the client, none is an original, and nobody outside Tapri has said anything. One letter from Chai Junction, sent straight to the auditor, saying "we received these goods and owe this amount", would say more than the whole box, and Lakshmi would still read it with extra care, because Chai Junction belongs to the MD's brother-in-law.

So evidence is judged on two scales. **Sufficiency** is the quantity: is there enough, given how big and how risky the item is? **Appropriateness** is the quality, in two parts. **Relevance**: does it bear on the question being asked? (A challan shows goods left the warehouse; it says nothing about whether the customer owes the money.) **Reliability**: can it be trusted, given who produced it, in what form, and under what controls?

::: analogy The third umpire
Ten fielders shouting "howzat" is a lot of evidence, all of it oral and all from one interested team. One replay from an independent camera settles the run-out, and no number of extra shouts makes up for a blurry replay. The umpire also needs the right replay: perfect footage of the bowler's front foot is useless for a run-out at the other end.
:::

::: key
Audit evidence must be sufficient (more of it when the item is material, risky or from a large, mixed population) and appropriate (relevant to the assertion tested, and reliable given its source, nature and circumstances). More evidence may not make up for poor quality.
:::

## How it works

SA 500, "Audit Evidence", applies to all the audit evidence obtained during the course of the audit (SM 4.32).

### 1. The objective

The objective of the auditor is ==to design and perform audit procedures in such a way as to enable the auditor to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence to draw reasonable conclusions on which to base the auditor's opinion== (SM 4.4–4.5).

### 2. What counts as audit evidence

::: definition Audit evidence
The information used by the auditor in arriving at the conclusions on which the auditor's opinion is based. Audit evidence includes both information contained in the accounting records underlying the financial statements and other information. (SM 4.5)
:::

- **Accounting records** include the records of initial accounting entries and supporting records, such as cheques and records of electronic fund transfers; invoices; contracts; the general and subsidiary ledgers, journal entries and other adjustments to the financial statements that are not reflected in journal entries; and records such as work sheets and spreadsheets supporting cost allocations, computations, reconciliations and disclosures (SM 4.5–4.6).
- **Other information** authenticates the accounting records and supports the auditor's rationale behind the true and fair presentation: for example minutes of meetings, written confirmations from trade receivables and trade payables, and manuals containing details of internal control (SM 4.6).

Evidence is **cumulative**: primarily from procedures performed during the audit, but also from other sources such as previous audits. Besides other sources inside and outside the entity, the entity's **accounting records are an important source**, and some information may have been prepared using the work of a management's expert. It includes information that **supports** management's assertions and information that **contradicts** them, and in some cases ==the absence of information (for example, management's refusal to provide a requested representation)== also constitutes audit evidence (SM 4.12).

### 3. Types: by nature and by source

| Basis | Type | Study Material's examples |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Visual | Observing physical verification of inventory conducted by the client's staff |
| Nature | Oral | Discussion with the management and various officers of the client |
| Nature | Documentary | Fixed deposit certificate, loan agreement, sales bill |
| Source | Internal: originates within the organisation being audited | Sales invoice, copies of sales challan and forwarding notes, goods received note, inspection report, copies of cash memo, debit and credit notes |
| Source | External: originates outside the client's organisation | Purchase invoice, supplier's challan and forwarding note, debit notes and credit notes coming from parties, quotations, confirmations |

(SM 4.6–4.7.) Most evidence is internal, and since the client controls its origination the auditor stays alive to manipulation. External evidence is generally more reliable, but if the auditor doubts the independence of a third party (an invoice of an associated concern, say), greater vigilance is needed. As an ordinary rule, match internal and external evidence as far as practicable (SM 4.7).

### 4. Appropriateness: relevance and reliability

::: definition Relevance
Relevance deals with the logical connection with, or bearing upon, the purpose of the audit procedure and, where appropriate, the assertion under consideration. The relevance of information to be used as audit evidence may be affected by the direction of testing. (SM 4.8)
:::

To test **overstatement** in the existence or valuation of accounts payable, testing the *recorded* payables may be relevant; to test **understatement**, it is not, but testing subsequent disbursements, unpaid invoices, suppliers' statements and unmatched receiving reports may be (SM 4.9).

::: definition Reliability
The reliability of information to be used as audit evidence, and therefore of the audit evidence itself, is influenced by its source and its nature, and the circumstances under which it is obtained, including the controls over its preparation and maintenance where relevant. (SM 4.10)

### 5. Sufficiency, and the three factors that move it

::: definition Sufficiency and appropriateness
Sufficiency is the measure of the quantity of audit evidence. Appropriateness is the measure of the quality of audit evidence; that is, its relevance and its reliability in providing support for the conclusions on which the auditor's opinion is based. (SM 4.11)
:::

The two are **interrelated**: the higher the assessed risks, the more evidence is likely to be required; the higher the quality, the less may be required (SM 4.11). ==Obtaining more audit evidence, however, may not compensate for its poor quality== (SM 4.136).

### 6. Designing procedures, and where evidence comes from

The auditor shall design and perform audit procedures that are appropriate in the circumstances for obtaining sufficient appropriate audit evidence (SM 4.12): **inspection, observation, confirmation, recalculation, reperformance and analytical procedures**, often in combination, in addition to **inquiry**. Each is explained in [[aud.evid.assertions|the assertions lesson]].

Some evidence is obtained by testing the accounting records, for example through analysis and review, reperforming procedures followed in the financial reporting process, and reconciling related types and applications of the same information; the auditor may thus find the records internally consistent and in agreement with the financial statements (SM 4.14). **More assurance is ordinarily obtained from consistent audit evidence obtained from different sources or of a different nature than from items of audit evidence considered individually**: an independent source (third-party confirmations, analysts' reports, data about competitors) may increase the assurance from internal evidence such as records, minutes or a management representation (SM 4.14–4.15). Evidence from previous audits may be appropriate where the auditor performs procedures to establish its continuing relevance (SM 4.19).

### 7. Information produced by the entity, and management's experts

When using **information produced by the entity**, the auditor shall evaluate whether it is sufficiently reliable, including as necessary (a) obtaining audit evidence about its accuracy and completeness, and (b) evaluating whether it is sufficiently precise and detailed for the auditor's purposes (SM 4.29).

::: definition Management's expert
An individual or organisation possessing expertise in a field other than accounting or auditing, whose work in that field is used by the entity to assist the entity in preparing the financial statements. (SM 4.32)
:::

When evidence has been prepared using a management's expert's work, the auditor shall, to the extent necessary, having regard to the significance of that work for the auditor's purposes (SM 4.32):

::: steps
1. **Evaluate** the competence, capabilities and objectivity of that expert;
2. **Obtain an understanding** of the work of that expert; and
3. **Evaluate the appropriateness** of that expert's work as audit evidence for the relevant assertion.
:::

The nature, timing and extent of those audit procedures may be affected by such matters as (SM 4.28–4.29):

- the nature and complexity of the matter to which the management's expert relates;
- the risks of material misstatement in the matter;
- the availability of alternative sources of audit evidence;
- the nature, scope and objectives of the management's expert's work;
- whether the management's expert is employed by the entity, or is a party engaged by it to provide relevant services;
- the extent to which management can exercise control or influence over the work of the management's expert;
- whether the management's expert is subject to technical performance standards or other professional or industry requirements;
- the nature and extent of any controls within the entity over the management's expert's work;
- the auditor's knowledge and experience of the management's expert's field of expertise; and
- the auditor's previous experience of the work of that expert.

### 8. Selecting items, and evidence that disagrees

When designing tests of controls and tests of details, the auditor shall determine means of selecting items for testing that are effective in meeting the purpose of the audit procedure: (a) selecting all items (100% examination); (b) selecting specific items; and (c) audit sampling, alone or in combination (SM 4.29–4.30).

- **Selecting all items.** 100% examination of a population (or a stratum within it) is unlikely in tests of controls but more common for tests of details. It may be appropriate when the population constitutes a small number of large value items; when there is a significant risk and other means do not provide sufficient appropriate audit evidence; or when the repetitive nature of a calculation or other process performed automatically by an information system makes a 100% examination cost effective (SM 4.30).
- **Selecting specific items**, guided by the auditor's understanding of the entity, the assessed risks of material misstatement and the characteristics of the population. Judgemental selection is subject to non-sampling risk. Specific items may include high value or key items (suspicious, unusual, particularly risk-prone, or with a history of error); all items over a certain amount, to verify a large proportion of the total; and items to obtain information about the nature of the entity or its transactions (SM 4.30–4.31).
- **Audit sampling**, designed to enable conclusions to be drawn about an entire population on the basis of testing a sample drawn from it (SM 4.31): [[aud.evid.sa530|SA 530]].

If evidence from one source is inconsistent with that from another, or the auditor doubts the reliability of information, **the auditor shall determine what modifications or additions to audit procedures are necessary to resolve the matter, and shall consider the effect of the matter, if any, on other aspects of the audit** (SM 4.31). Whether enough evidence has been obtained to reduce audit risk to an acceptably low level is a matter of **professional judgement**, and reaching it gives **reasonable assurance** (SM 4.12, 4.32).

## Worked examples

:::: example Bunty's box file
Iyer & Associates audit Tapri Tea Co. Ltd for 2025-26; overall materiality is ₹1.50 crore. On 31 March 2026 Tapri recorded sales of ₹18.60 crore to Chai Junction Distributors, a firm run by the MD's brother-in-law. Bunty's file holds photocopies of Tapri's own delivery challans, a summary prepared by the sales team, and a note of the sales head's oral assurance that "Chai Junction has accepted everything". The originals, with the transporter's acknowledgements, are "somewhere in the Indore warehouse". Bunty concludes "evidence sufficient: 400 pages".

Comment on Bunty's conclusion with reference to SA 500, and advise the audit team.

::: answer Model answer
**Relevant provision.** As per SA 500, the auditor shall obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence. Sufficiency is the measure of the quantity of audit evidence, affected by the assessed risks and by its quality; appropriateness is the measure of its quality, that is, its relevance and reliability. Reliability is increased when evidence is obtained from independent sources outside the entity, in documentary rather than oral form, and as original documents rather than photocopies. Where the independence of a third party is in doubt, greater vigilance is needed. More evidence may not compensate for poor quality.

**Facts of the case.** A year-end sale of ₹18.60 crore, over twelve times materiality, to a party related to the MD; only internal photocopies and an oral assurance on file.

**Analysis.** The item is material and its risk is high (size, year-end timing, related party), so more and better evidence is needed. All the documents are internal copies and the assurance is oral: low reliability, however many pages. Tapri's own challans can at most show despatch and its date; they are not relevant to whether the distributor accepted the goods and owes the amount. Nothing comes from an independent source.

**Conclusion.** Bunty's conclusion is not correct: the file is voluminous but not sufficient appropriate audit evidence. The team should inspect the original challans with transporter acknowledgements, obtain a confirmation sent by Chai Junction directly to the auditor (with greater vigilance, the party being related), trace receipts after the year end to bank statements, and examine sales returns after the year end. If the evidence remains inconsistent or doubtful, the auditor shall modify or add procedures and consider the effect on other aspects of the audit.
:::
::::

:::: example The tea taster's report
Tapri holds ₹42 crore of Assam and Darjeeling tea in its Siliguri warehouse, part of it from a damp monsoon season. To estimate net realisable value, management engaged Brahmaputra Tea Assessors LLP, professional tea tasters, to grade every lot. Kesar reviewed the draft and "suggested" higher grades for two lots, which the final report adopts.

Advise Iyer & Associates on what SA 500 requires before using the report as audit evidence, and on what decides how much work they must do.

::: answer Model answer
**Relevant provision.** As per SA 500, when information to be used as audit evidence has been prepared using the work of a management's expert, the auditor shall, to the extent necessary, having regard to the significance of that expert's work: (a) evaluate the competence, capabilities and objectivity of that expert; (b) obtain an understanding of the work of that expert; and (c) evaluate the appropriateness of that work as audit evidence for the relevant assertion. The nature, timing and extent of these procedures may be affected by, among others, the risks of material misstatement, the availability of alternative sources of audit evidence, and the extent to which management can exercise control or influence over the expert's work.

**Facts of the case.** Tea grading is expertise outside accounting and auditing, used by Tapri to prepare its financial statements: Brahmaputra is a management's expert. The stock is material, partly damp, and management influenced two grades.

**Analysis.** The report is significant evidence for a judgemental valuation. Management's influence weakens the expert's objectivity, a matter that increases the extent of procedures. Alternative evidence exists: post-year-end prices for the same lots and auction prices for comparable grades.

**Conclusion.** Iyer & Associates should evaluate Brahmaputra's competence, capabilities and objectivity; understand its grading method, samples and basis of values; and evaluate whether its conclusions are appropriate evidence for valuation, comparing them with post-year-end and auction prices. The two influenced lots need corroboration from those alternative sources before the report is relied on.
:::
::::

:::: example Four statements from the article room
State, with reasons, whether each statement is correct or incorrect.

(a) Kesar's refusal to give a written representation is not audit evidence, because no document was received.

(b) The milk supplier's invoice is internal evidence, because it is kept in Tapri's records.

(c) Where the audit evidence obtained is of high quality, less of it may be required.

(d) Evidence from last year's audit can never be used in this year's audit.

::: answer Model answer
**(a) Incorrect.** Audit evidence comprises information that supports and that contradicts management's assertions; in some cases the absence of information, for example management's refusal to provide a requested representation, is used by the auditor and therefore also constitutes audit evidence (SM 4.12).

**(b) Incorrect.** Evidence is classified by where it originates, not where it is filed. A purchase invoice originates outside the entity, so it is external evidence (SM 4.7, 4.127).

**(c) Correct.** Sufficiency and appropriateness are interrelated: the quantity needed is affected by the quality, and the higher the quality, the less may be required (SM 4.11). More evidence, however, may not compensate for poor quality.

**(d) Incorrect.** Audit evidence obtained from previous audits may, in certain circumstances, provide appropriate audit evidence where the auditor performs audit procedures to establish its continuing relevance (SM 4.19).
:::

## Under the hood

### Small print the examiner likes

- **Reasonable assurance.** Obtained when sufficient appropriate audit evidence has reduced audit risk (the risk of an inappropriate opinion on materially misstated financial statements) to an acceptably low level; SA 200's discussion of the nature of procedures, timeliness and the balance between benefit and cost bears on that judgement (SM 4.12–4.13; [[aud.nature.sa200|SA 200]]).
- **Two meanings of "relevant".** Tests of controls look for conditions showing a control was performed and deviations showing it was not; substantive procedures look for conditions that constitute a misstatement in the assertion (SM 4.9; [[aud.risk.testing|testing internal control]]).
- **Audit trails** recording system and user activity can increase the reliability of evidence (SM 4.28; [[aud.evid.trail|audit trail]]).

## Answer frame

### A case question: provision, facts, analysis, conclusion

1. **Relevant provision**: "As per SA 500, …" only what the case needs (sufficiency, appropriateness, the reliability generalisation that applies, management's expert).
2. **Facts of the case**: the source, form and origin of the evidence; the size and risk of the item.
3. **Analysis**: relevant to the assertion? Reliable, and why? Enough, given materiality and risk?
4. **Conclusion**: "Hence, the evidence is (not) sufficient appropriate audit evidence; the auditor should …", naming the further procedures.

### "Explain sufficiency of audit evidence and the factors affecting it" (5 marks)

1. Sufficiency is the measure of the quantity of audit evidence.
2. Quantity needed is affected by assessed risks (higher risk, more evidence) and by quality (higher quality, less may be required); more evidence may not compensate for poor quality.
3. Materiality: less material, less evidence; more material, more.
4. Risk of material misstatement (inherent and control risk): lower risk, less; higher risk, more.
5. Size and characteristics of the population: smaller and homogeneous, less; larger and heterogeneous, more.

### "Distinguish sufficiency and appropriateness"

| Point | Sufficiency | Appropriateness |
|---|---|---|
| Measures | Quantity | Quality |
| Made of | Enough evidence | Relevance and reliability |
| Affected by | Materiality, risk of material misstatement, population | Source, nature, circumstances, controls |
| Link | Higher quality, less may be required | Quantity may not compensate for poor quality |

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: the 400-page box file
Weighing the file instead of reading it. **Fix:** quantity asks "is there enough?", quality asks "is it relevant and reliable?". More copies of internal documents add no independence.
:::

::: mistake "External, therefore reliable"
**Fix:** an external source may not be knowledgeable, a related party may not be independent (greater vigilance), and a management's expert may lack objectivity (SM 4.7, 4.10).
:::

::: mistake The purchase invoice filed as internal evidence
**Fix:** classify by where evidence originates, not where it is filed. The supplier's invoice to Tapri is external, even after it is scanned into Tapri's system (SM 4.7, 4.127).
:::

::: mistake "Nature of evidence" added to the sufficiency factors
**Fix:** the three factors are materiality, risk of material misstatement, and size and characteristics of the population. Source and nature go to reliability. ICAI's September 2025 MCQ 6 was built on this trap.
:::

## Recap

- Audit evidence: information used by the auditor in arriving at the conclusions on which the opinion is based; accounting records plus other information.
- Objective: design and perform procedures to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence to draw reasonable conclusions on which to base the opinion.
- Sufficiency = quantity; appropriateness = quality = relevance + reliability. Higher risk needs more; higher quality needs less; more may not compensate for poor quality.
- Sufficiency factors: materiality, risk of material misstatement, size and characteristics of the population.
- Reliability rises with independent outside sources, effective controls, direct evidence, documentary form and originals (with exceptions).
- Evidence includes contradicting information and, sometimes, the absence of information.
- Management's expert: evaluate competence, capabilities and objectivity; understand the work; evaluate it as evidence for the assertion.
- Inconsistent or doubtful evidence: modify or add procedures; consider the effect on the rest of the audit. (Four hundred photocopies of one voice are still one voice.)

## Flashcards

Q:: SA 500: define audit evidence.
A:: The information used by the auditor in arriving at the conclusions on which the auditor's opinion is based; it includes both information contained in the accounting records underlying the financial statements and other information.

Q:: SA 500: what is the objective of the auditor?
A:: To design and perform audit procedures in such a way as to enable the auditor to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence to draw reasonable conclusions on which to base the auditor's opinion.

Q:: SA 500: sufficiency and appropriateness measure what?
A:: Sufficiency is the measure of the quantity of audit evidence; appropriateness is the measure of its quality, that is, its relevance and its reliability.

Q:: Which three factors affect the auditor's judgement as to sufficiency of audit evidence?
A:: Materiality; risk of material misstatement (inherent and control risk); size and characteristics of the population.

Q:: SA 500: what influences the reliability of audit evidence?
A:: Its source and its nature, and the circumstances under which it is obtained, including the controls over its preparation and maintenance where relevant.

Q:: State the five generalisations about the reliability of audit evidence.
A:: More reliable when (1) from independent sources outside the entity; (2) internally generated with effective related controls; (3) obtained directly by the auditor rather than indirectly or by inference; (4) documentary rather than oral; (5) original documents rather than photocopies, facsimiles or digitised copies.

Q:: SA 500: can the absence of information be audit evidence?
A:: Yes. In some cases the absence of information, for example management's refusal to provide a requested representation, is used by the auditor and therefore constitutes audit evidence.

Q:: SA 500: three requirements when evidence is prepared using the work of a management's expert?
A:: Evaluate the expert's competence, capabilities and objectivity; obtain an understanding of the expert's work; evaluate the appropriateness of the work as audit evidence for the relevant assertion.

Q:: Define management's expert.
A:: An individual or organisation possessing expertise in a field other than accounting or auditing, whose work in that field is used by the entity to assist it in preparing the financial statements.

Q:: SA 500: three means of selecting items for testing?
A:: Selecting all items (100% examination); selecting specific items; audit sampling.

Q:: SA 500: what must the auditor do if evidence from two sources is inconsistent?
A:: Determine what modifications or additions to audit procedures are necessary to resolve the matter, and consider the effect of the matter, if any, on other aspects of the audit.

Q:: Is a purchase invoice internal or external evidence?
A:: External: it originates outside the client's organisation.
