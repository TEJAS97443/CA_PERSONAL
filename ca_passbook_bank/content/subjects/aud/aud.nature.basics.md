## In plain words

### Kesar finds a stranger's signature in his annual report

Tapri Tea Co. Ltd has been listed since 2024. Its annual report runs to two hundred pages, and Kesar has read exactly one of them: the page signed by someone who does not work for him.

> **Kesar:** Lakshmi-ji, my team made every number in these accounts. Why does your signature sit at the end?
> **Lakshmi:** Because your shareholders, your banks and the tax department will believe those numbers only after someone who does not work for you has examined them.
> **Bunty:** Ma'am, "audit" comes from the Latin for "to hear". Can Kesar-ji just read the accounts aloud to us?
> **Lakshmi:** Auditors did exactly that, centuries ago. Today we ask for evidence, Bunty.

That is the whole idea. Tapri's **management** (Kesar and his directors) prepares the financial statements. An **auditor**, who must be **independent** of them, examines those statements and the evidence behind them and gives an **opinion** in a written report. The people who rely on the statements (investors, shareholders, banks, the government collecting taxes, trade unions bargaining over wages, an insurer settling a fire claim) trust them more because an outsider has looked. In one word, an audit provides **assurance**: confidence.

The auditor wants enough confidence that the accounts as a whole are not wrong by an amount big enough to change a user's decision, whether through honest error or fraud. Why that confidence is high but never total is the next two lessons.

::: analogy The hygiene inspector at the kulhad café
The café runs its own kitchen. An inspector who is not the owner's brother visits, looks hardest at what matters most, checks a sample of the kitchen's practices and publishes a rating. Diners eat with more confidence because of it. The inspector did not cook the food, and the rating is not a promise that nobody will ever get a stomach ache: it is an informed, independent opinion.
:::

::: key
An audit does not make the accounts; it makes them believable. An independent person examines the financial information and expresses an opinion on it, and that opinion gives every user confidence: high, but never a guarantee.
:::

## How it works

### 1. Kautilya got there first: where auditing came from

::: diagram From the Arthashastra to the ICAI
{"type": "layers", "layers": [{"label": "4th century BC", "note": "Kautilya's Arthashastra: accounting year, closing of accounts, audit"}, {"label": "Medieval times", "note": "Latin audire, to hear: accounts read out to auditors"}, {"label": "Industrial revolution", "note": "trade expands in Europe; demand for auditors grows"}, {"label": "1860", "note": "first Auditor General of India, in British India"}, {"label": "1949", "note": "ICAI set up by an Act of Parliament"}], "side": [{"label": "time", "arrow": "down"}], "caption": "The idea, the word, the demand, the State auditor, the profession: read top to bottom (SM 1.4)."}
:::

Kautilya's Arthashastra (4th century BC) already speaks of a fixed accounting year, the closing of accounts and their audit, periodical checking and verification, and even misstatements caused by abuse of power. The word "audit" comes from the Latin **audire**, "to hear": medieval auditors heard the accounts read out, to check that employees were not careless and negligent. The industrial revolution in Europe multiplied trade and the demand for auditors. The first Auditor General of India was appointed in 1860, with accounting and auditing functions; today the **Comptroller and Auditor General of India** is an independent constitutional authority auditing government receipts and expenditure. The **Institute of Chartered Accountants of India** was established in 1949 as a statutory body under an Act of Parliament (SM 1.4).

### 2. What an audit is

::: definition Audit
"An audit is an independent examination of financial information of any entity, whether profit oriented or not, and irrespective of its size or legal form, when such an examination is conducted with a view to expressing an opinion thereon" (SM 1.5).
:::

::: diagram The definition, piece by piece
{"type": "layers", "layers": [{"label": "An independent examination", "note": "judgement not subordinate to anyone's wishes"}, {"label": "of financial information", "note": "the financial statements and their records"}, {"label": "of any entity", "note": "profit oriented or not; any size; any legal form"}, {"label": "with a view to expressing an opinion", "note": "given in a written audit report"}], "caption": "Each layer is one phrase of ICAI's definition. ICAI's first point below covers the top two layers; its second and third points, the other two."}
:::

ICAI draws three points out of the definition (SM 1.5–1.6):

1. **It is an independent examination of financial information.** Independence implies that ==the judgement of a person is not subordinate to the wishes or direction of another person== who might have engaged him. The auditor must be independent of the entity whose financial statements are audited, so that he can form an opinion without being affected by any influence; independence increases his ability to act objectively, without biases creeping in. So a CA cannot audit the proprietary concern of his own brother (the relationship by birth subjects him to influence), and he cannot audit a company whose shares he holds (his own money, and so his self-interest, is involved). The threats to independence and their safeguards are in [[aud.ethics.independence|independence, threats and safeguards]].
2. **The entity can be any entity.** It need not be profit oriented: an NGO or a charitable trust can be audited. It may be small, medium or large, and of any legal structure: a proprietary concern, a partnership firm, an LLP, a private company, a public company, a society or a trust.
3. **The purpose is to express an opinion on the financial statements.** Preparing and presenting the financial statements is the **responsibility of the management** of the entity; the auditor expresses an opinion on them by means of a **written audit report**. In doing so he has to see that the financial statements would not mislead anybody, by ensuring that (SM 1.6):
    - the accounts have been drawn up with reference to entries in the books of account;
    - the entries in the books of account are adequately supported by sufficient and appropriate evidence;
    - none of the entries in the books of account has been omitted in the process of compilation;
    - the information conveyed by the statements is clear and unambiguous;
    - the financial statement amounts are properly classified, described and disclosed in conformity with accounting standards; and
    - the statement of accounts presents a true and fair picture of the operational results and of the assets and liabilities.

**Auditing provides assurance.** Its basic nature lies in providing assurance to users: confidence that the financial information in the financial statements can be relied upon. That assurance lends credibility to the financial statements (SM 1.6). What an audit covers, and what it does not, is in [[aud.nature.scope|scope of audit]]; how assurance comes in degrees (an audit's reasonable assurance, a review's limited assurance) is in [[aud.nature.assurance|assurance engagements]].

### 3. What an audit aims at: the objective

SA 200 "Overall Objectives of the Independent Auditor and the Conduct of an Audit in Accordance with Standards on Auditing" sets the objective. Learn its words exactly (SM 1.8):

::: definition Objectives of the auditor (SA 200)
In conducting an audit of financial statements, the objectives of the auditor are:

- (a) to obtain reasonable assurance about whether the financial statements as a whole are free from material misstatement, whether due to fraud or error, thereby enabling the auditor to express an opinion on whether the financial statements are prepared, in all material respects, in accordance with an applicable financial reporting framework; and
- (b) to report on the financial statements, and communicate as required by the SAs, in accordance with the auditor's findings.
:::

A **misstatement** is a difference between how an item is reported and how the applicable financial reporting framework requires it to be reported, arising from error or fraud (SM 3.4); it is **material** if it could reasonably be expected to influence the economic decisions of users (SM 3.16; [[aud.risk.sa320|SA 320]]). **Reasonable assurance** is a high level of assurance, but not a guarantee (SM 1.9). [[aud.nature.sa200|The SA 200 lesson]] takes the objective apart phrase by phrase; [[aud.nature.limits|the inherent limitations]] explain why it can never become a guarantee. And the objective is the same for every entity: the basic objective of auditing, the expression of an opinion on the financial statements, does not change with the nature, size or form of the entity (SM 1.40).

### 4. Why an audit is needed: the benefits

ICAI lists seven benefits, in this order (SM 1.17–1.18):

1. **High quality information.** Audited accounts give users confidence that the information they rely on is qualitative and is the outcome of an exercise carried out by following Auditing Standards recognised globally.
2. **The shareholders' interest is safeguarded.** In a company, shareholders may or may not be involved in its daily affairs, and the financial statements are prepared by management consisting of directors. As owners, the shareholders need an independent mechanism so that the financial information is qualitative and reliable.
3. **A moral check on employees** from committing frauds, for fear of being discovered by audit.
4. **Government authorities** find audited financial statements helpful for determining tax liabilities.
5. **Lenders and bankers** can rely on audited financial statements for their credit decisions: whether to lend or not to lend to a particular entity.
6. **Fraud or error, or both,** may be detected.
7. **Controls are reviewed.** An audit reviews the existence and operation of the various controls in an entity, so it is useful in pointing out deficiencies.

::: trick Seven benefits in ICAI's order
"**Q**uality **S**hareholders **M**ostly **T**ake **L**oans **F**or **C**hai": quality information, shareholders' interest, moral check, tax authorities, lenders, fraud or error, controls. The sentence only fixes the order: each point still needs its one-line reason.
:::

### 5. Compulsory or voluntary, who appoints, and who gets the report

**Mandatory or voluntary** (SM 1.18). An audit is not always legally mandatory. Entities like companies are compulsorily required by law to get their accounts audited. Non-corporate entities may be compulsorily required to under tax laws: for example, in India every person must get accounts audited if turnover crosses a certain threshold under income tax law. Some entities, like schools, may need an audit to obtain a grant or assistance from the Government. And many entities get their accounts audited voluntarily, because of the benefits, often under their own internal rules.

**Who appoints the auditor** (SM 1.18–1.19). Generally the owners, or in some cases constitutional or government authorities, in accordance with the applicable laws and regulations. Who *requires* the audit and who *appoints* the auditor are different questions: the law requires a company's audit, but its members appoint the auditor.

| Entity | Auditor appointed by |
|---|---|
| Company | Members (shareholders), in the Annual General Meeting |
| Government company in India | Comptroller and Auditor General of India |
| Partnership firm | Its partners |
| Some cases under a law or regulation, for example tax laws | A government authority, in accordance with that law |

**Who receives the report** (SM 1.19). The outcome of an audit is a written audit report in which the auditor expresses an opinion. It is submitted to the person making the appointment: for a company, the shareholders; for a firm, the partners who engaged him. The government company rules under the Companies Act, 2013 are in [[aud.entities.govt|government audit and the C&AG]].

## Worked examples

:::: example The society that wanted a signature, not an audit
Tapri Chai Sewa Samiti is a society registered by Kesar that runs free chai stalls outside two government hospitals in Indore. Its receipts last year were ₹38 lakh, all donations. At a committee meeting, the treasurer makes two proposals:

(i) "An audit is meant for profit-making companies. We are a small charity, so all we need is a CA's signature on the accounts."

(ii) "CA Nikhil Verma already writes our books as our part-time accountant and reports to me. He should sign the accounts as our auditor too; it saves fees."

With reference to the meaning of audit, examine the treasurer's proposals.

::: answer Model answer
**Relevant provision.** "An audit is an independent examination of financial information of any entity, whether profit oriented or not, and irrespective of its size or legal form, when such an examination is conducted with a view to expressing an opinion thereon." Three points follow from it: (1) the examination is independent: the judgement of the auditor is not subordinate to the wishes or direction of another person who might have engaged him, so that he forms an opinion without being affected by any influence; (2) the entity may be profit oriented or not, small, medium or large, and of any legal form, including a society or a trust; (3) the purpose is to express an opinion on the financial statements, whose preparation and presentation is the responsibility of management.

**Facts of the case.** The Samiti is a small, not-for-profit society. The proposed auditor is its part-time accountant, who writes its books and reports to the treasurer.

**Analysis.** (i) Being small and not for profit does not take the Samiti outside the meaning of audit: the definition covers any entity, irrespective of profit motive, size or legal form. Nor is an audit a signature: it is an examination conducted with a view to expressing an opinion, in which the auditor sees, among other things, that the entries in the books are adequately supported by sufficient and appropriate evidence. (ii) CA Nikhil writes the very books he would examine, and as the Samiti's accountant he works under the direction of the treasurer whose accounts he would report on. His judgement would be subordinate to the wishes of the person who engaged him: he would not be independent.

**Conclusion.** Both proposals are incorrect. The Samiti's financial information can be audited like that of any other entity, and the examination must be made by an independent auditor with a view to expressing an opinion, not by the person who keeps its books under the treasurer's direction.
:::

:::: example Pinky's firm and the bank's condition
Pinky Mehra, Kesar's cousin, runs Mehra Masala Traders, a partnership firm trading in spices. Her mother is a sleeping partner who never visits the shop. The firm's turnover is below the limit at which income tax law requires an audit, and no other law requires one. Three salesmen collect cash from shops every day. The firm has applied to a bank for a ₹50 lakh loan to build a warehouse, and the bank wants audited financial statements.

Pinky asks:

(a) Is an audit compulsory for the firm?

(b) Who would appoint the auditor, and to whom would the report go?

(c) Apart from the loan, what would the firm gain from an audit?

Advise Pinky.

::: answer Model answer
**(a) Mandatory or voluntary.** An audit is not always legally mandatory. Companies are compulsorily required to get their accounts audited under law; non-corporate entities may be compulsorily required to under tax laws, for example when turnover crosses the threshold under income tax law; some entities, like schools, need an audit to obtain a grant from the Government; and many entities get their accounts audited voluntarily because of its benefits. The firm's turnover is below the income tax threshold and no other law applies, so an audit is not compulsory for it. It would be a voluntary audit, which the bank's condition makes necessary in practice.

**(b) Appointment and report.** Generally, an auditor is appointed by the owners: in the case of a firm, by its partners. The written audit report, in which the auditor expresses an opinion, is submitted to the person making the appointment. Pinky and her mother, as partners, would appoint the auditor and receive the report.

**(c) Benefits.** Apart from the loan: (1) high quality information, the outcome of an exercise following globally recognised Auditing Standards; (2) an independent mechanism for the sleeping partner, who is not involved in daily affairs, safeguarding her interest; (3) a moral check on the three salesmen who collect cash, for fear of being discovered; (4) accounts the tax authorities can use in determining the firm's tax liability; (5) accounts this bank and future lenders can rely on for credit decisions; (6) possible detection of fraud or error; (7) a review of its controls, for example over cash collections, pointing out deficiencies.
:::

## Answer frame

### "Explain the meaning of audit" (with an incisive analysis)

1. **Definition**, word for word: "An audit is an independent examination of financial information of any entity, whether profit oriented or not, and irrespective of its size or legal form, when such an examination is conducted with a view to expressing an opinion thereon."
2. **Point 1, independence:** judgement not subordinate to the wishes or direction of another person who might have engaged him; opinion formed without influence; objectivity without bias.
3. **Point 2, any entity:** profit oriented or not; any size; any legal form (name a few).
4. **Point 3, purpose:** to express an opinion on the financial statements through a written report; preparation is management's responsibility.
5. **Apply** each point to the facts, then **conclude**.

### "Explain the benefits and need of an audit"

The seven points of SM 1.17–1.18 in order, one line each with its reason, then one closing line tying them to the case (the loan, the absent owners, the cash-handling staff).

::: cols
**Loses marks**

"Audit is useful: it catches fraud and banks like it."

Two benefits named, no reasons, nothing from the case.
---
**Passes**

Five or six benefits as bare labels: quality, shareholders, tax, banks, fraud.

The list is recognisable but thin, and the moral check and controls are missing.
---
**Full marks**

All seven in ICAI's order, each with its one-line reason, applied to the case (the lender's credit decision, the sleeping partner, the cash-collecting staff).
:::

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: the auditor who "prepared and audited" the accounts
Writing that the auditor prepares, or is responsible for, the financial statements. **Fix:** preparation and presentation of the financial statements is the responsibility of management; the auditor expresses an opinion on them in a written report (SM 1.6).
:::

::: mistake The report addressed to the management
Bunty addresses the draft report of a company "To the Board of Directors". **Fix:** the report is submitted to the person making the appointment: the shareholders of a company, the partners of a firm (SM 1.19).
:::

::: mistake Benefits in a heap
Listing "fraud detection" first and forgetting the moral check and the review of controls. **Fix:** learn the seven in ICAI's order with the mnemonic, and give each its reason.
:::

- [ ] Its members in the Annual General Meeting
- [x] The Comptroller and Auditor General of India
- [ ] The Registrar of Companies
> Company auditors are generally appointed by the members in the AGM, but for government companies the appointment is made by the Comptroller and Auditor General of India, an independent constitutional authority (SM 1.18).

- [x] It moves the responsibility for preparing the financial statements to the auditor

Q: Select all that apply. To see that the financial statements do not mislead anybody, the auditor ensures that:
- [x] none of the entries in the books of account has been omitted in compiling the statements

## Recap

- **Audit:** an independent examination of financial information of any entity, whether profit oriented or not, and irrespective of its size or legal form, conducted with a view to expressing an opinion thereon.
- **Independence:** the auditor's judgement is not subordinate to the wishes or direction of anyone who engaged him; no audit of a brother's concern or of a company whose shares he holds.
- Management prepares and presents the financial statements; the auditor expresses an opinion in a written report. The nature of auditing is **assurance**: confidence and credibility for users.
- **Objective (SA 200):** reasonable assurance that the financial statements as a whole are free from material misstatement, whether due to fraud or error, enabling an opinion; then report and communicate as the SAs require. The same objective for every entity.
- **Seven benefits, in order:** quality information, shareholders' interest, moral check, tax authorities, lenders, fraud or error detected, deficiencies in controls.
- Not always mandatory: companies by law, others under tax laws or for grants, many voluntarily.
- Appointed by the owners (company: members in the AGM; government company: C&AG; firm: partners) or by a government authority under a law; the report goes to whoever appointed the auditor.
- Origin: Kautilya's Arthashastra; *audire*, "to hear"; first Auditor General 1860; ICAI 1949. (Kesar still offers to read his accounts aloud.)
