## In plain words

### The gold card, again

Bunty still has Kesar's "Tapri Gold" card: unlimited free chai, for life. He has a theory.

> **Bunty:** Ma'am, I promise you, free chai will not change my view of Tapri's inventory by one rupee.
> **Lakshmi:** I believe you. Would Tapri's bank believe you, if it saw you sipping Kesar's chai while ticking Kesar's stock sheets?
> **Bunty:** *(slowly)* Maybe not, ma'am.
> **Lakshmi:** Then you have just learnt both halves of independence.

**Independence** means that the auditor's judgement does not bend to the wishes of whoever appointed him, or to his own self-interest. It has two halves. **Independence of mind** is the inside fact: Bunty really is unbiased. **Independence in appearance** is the outside view: a sensible, well-informed outsider who knew all the facts would also believe he is unbiased. An audit report is useful only if both hold, because readers cannot see inside Bunty's head.

Independence is attacked in five familiar ways, and ICAI calls them **threats**:

- **money** for the auditor riding on the client (self-interest);
- the auditor **checking his own work** (self-review);
- the auditor **championing the client's cause** (advocacy);
- the auditor getting **too close** to the client (familiarity);
- the auditor being **bullied** (intimidation).

When a threat exists, the auditor either walks away from the task, removes the threat, or puts **safeguards** in place that bring it down to an acceptable level, and writes them down. If no credible safeguard works, he does not take the work.

The same chapter adds the auditor's working attitude: **professional skepticism**, a questioning mind that stays alert for anything that does not add up. It is not suspicion of everyone; it is refusing to stop thinking.

::: analogy The examiner and the answer sheets
An examiner should not check her nephew's answer sheet (too close), should not be paid a bonus for every pass (money), should not check answers she herself dictated to the class (her own work), should not campaign for a student's admission while marking him (championing), and should not be told "pass everyone or lose your job" (bullied). A good examiner also does not assume every student cheated. But when two sheets share the same odd spelling mistake on the same line, she looks closer. That second habit is professional skepticism.
:::

::: key
Independence must exist in fact (independence of mind) **and** appear to exist to a reasonable and informed third party (independence in appearance). Five threats attack it: self-interest, self-review, advocacy, familiarity and intimidation. When one exists, desist, eliminate it, or reduce it to an acceptable level with recorded safeguards; if credible and adequate safeguards cannot be fully implemented, do not accept the work.
:::

## How it works

### 1. What independence means

::: definition Independence
Independence implies that ==the judgement of a person is not subordinate to the wishes or direction of another person who might have engaged him, or to his own self-interest== (SM 11.8).
:::

The Study Material adds (SM 11.8–11.9):

- It is not possible to define "independence" precisely. Rules of professional conduct are framed with an objective, but the rules by themselves cannot create or ensure independence.
- Independence is a condition of mind as well as personal character. It should not be confused with the superficial and visible standards of independence sometimes imposed by law: those may be relaxed or strengthened, but the quality of independence remains unaltered.
- Independence is subjective. One person might be independent in a set of circumstances in which another feels he is not. So it is the duty of every Chartered Accountant to determine for himself whether he can act independently and, quite apart from legal rules, in no case to place himself in a position which would compromise his independence.

There are **two interlinked perspectives** (SM 11.8–11.9):

::: definition Independence of mind and independence in appearance
(a) **Independence of mind**: the state of mind that permits the provision of an opinion without being affected by influences that compromise professional judgment, allowing an individual to act with integrity, and exercise objectivity and professional skepticism.

(b) **Independence in appearance**: the avoidance of facts and circumstances that are so significant that a reasonable and informed third party, having knowledge of all relevant information, including any safeguards applied, would reasonably conclude that a firm's, or a member of the assurance team's, integrity, objectivity or professional skepticism had been compromised.
:::

Independence has not only to exist in fact but also to appear to exist to all reasonable persons. The relationship between auditor and client should be such that, **first**, the auditor is himself satisfied about his independence and, **second**, no unbiased person would be forced to the conclusion, on an objective assessment of the circumstances, that there is likely to be an abridgement of the auditor's independence (SM 11.9).

::: compare
| Point | Independence of mind | Independence in appearance |
|---|---|---|
| Asks | Is my judgement actually free of influence? | Would an informed outsider think it is? |
| Who judges | The auditor himself | A reasonable and informed third party |
| Fails when | Influences compromise professional judgement | Significant facts suggest integrity, objectivity or skepticism are compromised |
| Bunty's card | He believes his view is unaffected | The bank would doubt it |
:::

Why it matters: as statutory auditor of a listed company, a Chartered Accountant would cease to perform any useful function if those who rely on the accounts had no faith in his independence and integrity. He is expected to be objective, fearless, and capable of expressing an honest opinion (SM 11.9).

### 2. The five threats

Many circumstances, or combinations of them, can threaten independence, so it is impossible to define every situation and the mitigating action for each; and different assurance engagements may face different threats needing different safeguards (SM 11.9). The Study Material discusses five types (SM 11.10–11.11):

#### (1) Self-interest threats

Self-interest threats occur when an auditing firm, its partner or associate could benefit from a financial interest in an audit client. Examples include (SM 11.10):

- (i) direct financial interest or materially significant indirect financial interest in a client;
- (ii) loan or guarantee to or from the concerned client;
- (iii) undue dependence on a client's fees and, hence, concerns about losing the engagement;
- (iv) close business relationship with an audit client;
- (v) potential employment with the client; and
- (vi) contingent fees for the audit engagement.

#### (2) Self-review threats

Self-review threats occur during a review of any judgement or conclusion reached in a previous audit or non-audit engagement, or when a member of the audit team was previously a director or senior employee of the client. **Non-audit services** include any professional services provided to an entity by an auditor, other than audit or review of the financial statements: management services, internal audit, investment advisory service, etc. Instances (SM 11.10):

- (i) an auditor having recently been a director or senior officer of the company;
- (ii) auditors performing services that are themselves subject matters of audit.

#### (3) Advocacy threats

Advocacy threats occur when the auditor promotes, or is perceived to promote, a client's opinion to a point where people may believe that objectivity is getting compromised: for example, when an auditor **deals with shares or securities** of the audited company, or becomes the **client's advocate in litigation and third party disputes**. The auditor can then be perceived as backing and championing the causes of the auditee client (SM 11.10–11.11).

#### (4) Familiarity threats

Familiarity threats are self-evident, and occur when auditors form relationships with the client where they end up being too sympathetic to the client's interests. This can occur in many ways, including (SM 11.11):

- (i) a close relative of the audit team working in a senior position in the client company;
- (ii) a former partner of the audit firm being a director or senior employee of the client;
- (iii) long association between specific auditors and their specific client counterparts; and
- (iv) acceptance of significant gifts or hospitality from the client company, its directors or employees.

The provisions of the Companies Act, 2013 on **rotation of auditors** mainly address these familiarity threats: the auditor is rotated after a certain number of years so that auditors do not become too familiar with their clients (SM 11.11).

Bunty's gold card falls under (iv): unlimited free chai for life is a gift of real value from the client, so keeping it creates a familiarity threat. The safeguard costs nothing: he returns it.

#### (5) Intimidation threats

Intimidation threats occur when auditors are deterred from acting objectively with an adequate degree of professional skepticism. They could happen because of (SM 11.11):

- the threat of replacement over disagreements with the application of accounting principles;
- pressure to disproportionately reduce work in response to reduced audit fees; or
- being threatened with litigation.

### 3. Spotting the threat

| Threat | Cue in the facts | Study Material example |
|---|---|---|
| Self-interest | The auditor gains or loses money | Contingent audit fee |
| Self-review | The auditor's own past work or past post | Services that are subject matter of audit |
| Advocacy | The auditor backs the client in public | Client's advocate in a dispute |
| Familiarity | Closeness: family, old ties, gifts | Long association |
| Intimidation | Pressure, threats, fear | Threat of replacement |

### 4. Safeguards

Chartered Accountants have a responsibility to remain independent by taking into account the context in which they practise, the threats to independence and the safeguards available to address them (SM 11.11).

::: definition Safeguards
Safeguards are actions, individually or in combination, that the professional accountant takes that effectively reduce threats to comply with the fundamental principles to an acceptable level (SM 11.11).
:::

The guiding principles, in the Study Material's order (SM 11.12):

1. For the public to have confidence in the quality of audit, auditors should always be, and appear to be, independent of the entities they audit.
2. Before taking on any work, an auditor must conscientiously consider whether it involves threats to his independence.
3. When such threats exist, the auditor should either **desist** from the task, or **eliminate** the threat, or at the very least put in place **safeguards** which reduce the threats to an acceptable level. All such safeguard measures need to be **recorded** in a form that can serve as evidence of compliance with due process.
4. ==If the auditor is unable to fully implement credible and adequate safeguards, then he must not accept the work.==

### 5. Professional skepticism

Independence of mind lets the auditor exercise **professional skepticism**; intimidation is defined as what deters it. So the Study Material places it here (SM 11.12–11.13; it is also part of [[aud.nature.sa200|SA 200's overall conduct of an audit]]).

::: definition Professional skepticism
Professional skepticism refers to an attitude that includes ==a questioning mind, being alert to conditions which may indicate possible misstatement due to error or fraud, and a critical assessment of audit evidence== (SM 11.12).
:::

It signifies that the auditor has to remain alert throughout: a questioning mind that challenges things in the light of the available evidence. **The auditor shall plan and perform an audit with professional skepticism, recognising that circumstances may exist that cause the financial statements to be materially misstated** (SM 11.12).

**Professional skepticism includes being alert to, for example** (SM 11.12):

1. Audit evidence that contradicts other audit evidence obtained.
2. Information that brings into question the reliability of documents and responses to inquiries to be used as audit evidence.
3. Conditions that may indicate possible fraud.
4. Circumstances that suggest the need for audit procedures in addition to those required by the SAs.

**Maintaining it throughout the audit reduces the risks of** (SM 11.13):

1. Overlooking unusual circumstances.
2. Over generalising when drawing conclusions from audit observations.
3. Using inappropriate assumptions in determining the nature, timing and extent of the audit procedures and evaluating the results thereof.

**Critical assessment of evidence** (SM 11.13). Professional skepticism is necessary to the critical assessment of audit evidence. It also includes considering the sufficiency and appropriateness of the evidence in the light of the circumstances: for example, where fraud risk factors exist and a single document, of a nature that is susceptible to fraud, is the sole supporting evidence for a material amount.

**Genuine unless there is reason to doubt** (SM 11.13). The auditor may accept records and documents as genuine unless he has reason to believe the contrary. Nevertheless, he must consider the reliability of information to be used as audit evidence. In cases of doubt about reliability, or indications of possible fraud, the SAs require the auditor to investigate further and determine what modifications or additions to audit procedures are necessary to resolve the matter.

**Past honesty is not a pass** (SM 11.13). The auditor cannot be expected to disregard past experience of the honesty and integrity of management and those charged with governance. But a belief that they are honest and have integrity does not relieve the auditor of the need to maintain professional skepticism.

## Worked examples

:::: example Lakshmi's independence register
Before accepting reappointment as statutory auditors of Tapri Tea Co. Ltd for 2026-27, Ms. Lakshmi Iyer reviews the independence register of Iyer & Associates. It records:

(i) Tapri's audit committee proposes the 2026-27 audit fee as ₹48 lakh plus 1% of any tax saving that the audit team identifies.

(ii) Meera Khanna, the elder sister of article assistant Bunty, has joined Tapri as Head of Treasury.

(iii) Last year the firm's advisory wing designed and installed the inventory costing module of Tapri's ERP, which computes the closing inventory reported in Tapri's financial statements.

(iv) After the audit team insisted on writing down obsolete stock, Kesar emailed: "I am cutting your fee by 40%. Cut your hours by 40% too. Plenty of firms would love this audit."

(v) Tapri is contesting BrewBucks's claim over the "Kulhad" trademark before a tribunal, and Kesar wants Lakshmi to appear for Tapri and argue its case.

(vi) CA Rohan Desai, the audit manager on Tapri, is in the final round of interviews for the post of Deputy CFO at Tapri.

Classify, with reasons, the threat to independence in each situation, and state what the firm should do about these threats.

::: answer Model answer
**Classification**

| Situation | Threat | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| (i) Fee linked to tax savings | Self-interest | Contingent fees for the audit engagement: the firm benefits financially from the outcome |
| (ii) Bunty's sister, Head of Treasury | Familiarity | A close relative of the audit team in a senior position in the client company |
| (iii) Firm built the inventory costing module | Self-review | Auditors performing services that are themselves subject matters of audit |
| (iv) Fee cut with demand to cut hours | Intimidation | Pressure to disproportionately reduce work in response to reduced audit fees, with an implied threat of replacement after a disagreement on accounting |
| (v) Arguing Tapri's case before the tribunal | Advocacy | The auditor would become the client's advocate in litigation and third party disputes |
| (vi) Manager seeking a post at Tapri | Self-interest | Potential employment with the client |

**What the firm should do.** Before taking on any work, an auditor must conscientiously consider whether it involves threats to his independence. When such threats exist, the auditor should either desist from the task, or eliminate the threat, or at the very least put in place safeguards which reduce the threats to an acceptable level, recording all safeguard measures as evidence of compliance with due process. If the auditor is unable to fully implement credible and adequate safeguards, he must not accept the work. Applied here:

- **(i)** Eliminate the threat: refuse the contingent element and agree a fee that does not depend on the outcome of the work.
- **(ii)** Safeguard: Bunty should not be on the Tapri engagement team while his sister holds a senior position there; record the step.
- **(iii)** The firm must evaluate whether credible and adequate safeguards (for example, an independent review of the module's output by people who had no role in designing it) can reduce the threat to an acceptable level. Its own service produces a figure it would audit; if such safeguards cannot be fully implemented, it must not accept the reappointment.
- **(iv)** The nature, timing and extent of audit procedures are decided by the auditor to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence; they cannot be cut to match a fee. If the pressure cannot be reduced to an acceptable level, the firm must not accept the work.
- **(v)** Desist: decline to act as Tapri's advocate before the tribunal.
- **(vi)** Safeguard: remove Rohan from the Tapri engagement at once, and have his recent work reviewed; record both steps.

Every step taken is recorded in a form that can serve as evidence of due process. The firm accepts reappointment only if each threat has been eliminated or reduced to an acceptable level.
:::

:::: example Bunty's two swings
After being caught ticking vouchers he had not read, Bunty resolves to be "the most skeptical auditor in India". On the 2025-26 audit of Tapri Tea Co. Ltd he records three things:

(a) **Café rent agreements.** He refuses to accept any of the 400 café rent agreements until each landlord re-signs it before a notary. The agreements match the rent paid through the bank and nothing suggests that any is forged. The audit is delayed by three weeks.

(b) **Samosa factory inventory.** Stock sheets show 12,000 kg of frozen potato at 31 March. The cold-chain division's temperature log, obtained by the team, shows that the cold store holding it was switched off for maintenance from 20 to 31 March. The factory manager says the stock was fine. Bunty accepts this, noting that "the manager has been honest for twelve years".

(c) **Café cash.** He counted the cash at two cafés in Indore, found no difference, and concluded: "Cash controls at the cafés in all 40 cities are effective."

Examine Bunty's approach in each area in the light of professional skepticism, and state the risks that maintaining professional skepticism throughout the audit reduces.

::: answer Model answer
**Relevant provision.** Professional skepticism refers to an attitude that includes a questioning mind, being alert to conditions which may indicate possible misstatement due to error or fraud, and a critical assessment of audit evidence. The auditor shall plan and perform an audit with professional skepticism, recognising that circumstances may exist that cause the financial statements to be materially misstated. It includes being alert to audit evidence that contradicts other audit evidence obtained, and to information that brings into question the reliability of documents and responses to inquiries. The auditor may accept records and documents as genuine unless he has reason to believe the contrary; in cases of doubt about reliability or indications of possible fraud, he shall investigate further and determine what modifications or additions to audit procedures are necessary. A belief that management is honest does not relieve the auditor of the need to maintain professional skepticism.

**(a) Rent agreements.** The agreements are corroborated by the rent paid through the bank and nothing gives reason to believe they are not genuine. Professional skepticism does not require the auditor to treat every document as forged: he may accept records and documents as genuine unless he has reason to believe the contrary. Demanding notarised re-signing of all 400 agreements is not required by skepticism and is not a proper use of the audit's time.

**(b) Inventory.** The temperature log is audit evidence that contradicts the stock sheets about the condition of the inventory, and the manager's reply is only a response to an inquiry. Bunty should have been alert to the contradiction, investigated further and determined the additional procedures needed (for example, examining the condition of the stock and its later use or disposal). The manager's past honesty cannot be disregarded, but it does not relieve the auditor of the need to maintain professional skepticism. Bunty's approach is not in accordance with professional skepticism.

**(c) Cash.** A conclusion about cafés in 40 cities drawn from observations at two cafés in one city is over generalising when drawing conclusions from audit observations. Bunty should extend his procedures before reaching any conclusion on cash controls across the cafés.

**Risks reduced.** Maintaining professional skepticism throughout the audit reduces the risks of: (i) overlooking unusual circumstances; (ii) over generalising when drawing conclusions from audit observations; and (iii) using inappropriate assumptions in determining the nature, timing and extent of the audit procedures and evaluating the results thereof.

**Conclusion.** In (a) Bunty applied more doubt than the facts justified; in (b) and (c) he did not apply professional skepticism. Skepticism is a questioning mind applied to the evidence, not a presumption that everyone is dishonest.
:::

## Answer frame

### "Classify the threats and state what the auditor should do"

1. A table: **situation → threat**, one line each.
2. For each, the **reason** in the Study Material's words ("could benefit from a financial interest", "services that are themselves subject matters of audit", "client's advocate in litigation", "close relative … in a senior position", "pressure to disproportionately reduce work").
3. **What to do**, in the guiding principles' words: consider threats before taking on work; desist, eliminate, or safeguards to an acceptable level, recorded; if credible and adequate safeguards cannot be fully implemented, do not accept the work.
4. Where marks allow, the specific step for each threat (sell the interest, remove the person, decline the service).

### A skepticism case: provision, facts, analysis, conclusion

Definition → the "alert to" item the facts trigger (contradicting evidence, doubtful reliability, fraud conditions) → what the auditor should do (investigate further, modify or add procedures; past honesty does not excuse) → the risks it reduces → conclusion on the auditor's approach.

### "Distinguish between independence of mind and independence in appearance"

| Point | Independence of mind | Independence in appearance |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | State of mind permitting an opinion without influences that compromise judgement | Avoiding facts and circumstances so significant that an informed third party would conclude independence is compromised |
| Test | The auditor is himself satisfied | No unbiased person would be forced to conclude independence is likely abridged |
| Enables | Integrity, objectivity and professional skepticism | Users' confidence in the report |

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: "I know I am independent, so there is no threat"
Bunty's gold-card defence. **Fix:** independence must also exist in appearance: the test is whether a reasonable and informed third party, knowing all the facts, would conclude that integrity, objectivity or skepticism had been compromised.
:::

::: mistake The share dealer filed under self-interest
**Fix:** a financial interest in the client is self-interest, but the Study Material's example of an auditor who **deals in the shares or securities** of the audited company is **advocacy**: he is seen to promote the client.
:::

::: mistake Self-review and familiarity reversed
**Fix:** the client's former director now on the audit team is self-review; the audit firm's former partner now a director of the client is familiarity.
:::

::: mistake Safeguards as an afterthought
Writing "the auditor can apply safeguards" and stopping. **Fix:** say which, that they must reduce the threat to an acceptable level and be recorded, and that if credible and adequate safeguards cannot be fully implemented, the work must not be accepted.
:::

::: mistake Skepticism as suspicion of everyone
**Fix:** the auditor may accept records and documents as genuine unless he has reason to believe the contrary; skepticism means a questioning mind, alertness and critical assessment, and investigating further when there is reason to doubt.

## Recap

- Independence: judgement not subordinate to the wishes or direction of another who engaged him, or to his own self-interest; a condition of mind and character that rules alone cannot create.
- Two perspectives: independence of mind and independence in appearance (the reasonable and informed third party); it must exist in fact and appear to exist.
- Self-interest: financial interest, loan or guarantee, fee dependence, close business relationship, potential employment, contingent audit fees.
- Self-review: reviewing one's own earlier judgement or service; audit team member recently a director or senior employee of the client.
- Advocacy: dealing in the client's shares, acting as its advocate in litigation. Familiarity: close relative in a senior post, former firm partner at the client, long association, significant gifts. Intimidation: threat of replacement, pressure to cut work with fees, threat of litigation.
- Threat exists: desist, eliminate, or safeguards to an acceptable level, recorded; if credible and adequate safeguards cannot be fully implemented, do not accept.
- Professional skepticism: questioning mind, alertness to misstatement from error or fraud, critical assessment of evidence; documents may be accepted as genuine unless there is reason to doubt; past honesty does not excuse it. (Bunty returned the gold card. He now buys his own chai.)

## Flashcards

Q:: Define independence, as the Study Material does.
A:: Independence implies that the judgement of a person is not subordinate to the wishes or direction of another person who might have engaged him, or to his own self-interest.

Q:: What is independence in appearance?
A:: The avoidance of facts and circumstances so significant that a reasonable and informed third party, knowing all relevant information including safeguards, would reasonably conclude that the integrity, objectivity or professional skepticism of the firm or an assurance team member had been compromised.

Q:: Name the five threats to independence.
A:: Self-interest, self-review, advocacy, familiarity and intimidation.

Q:: Self-interest threat: the Study Material's six examples?
A:: Direct or materially significant indirect financial interest; loan or guarantee to or from the client; undue dependence on the client's fees; close business relationship; potential employment with the client; contingent fees for the audit.

Q:: Self-review threat: the two instances in the Study Material?
A:: The auditor having recently been a director or senior officer of the company; auditors performing services that are themselves subject matters of audit.

Q:: Familiarity threat: the four ways it can occur?
A:: Close relative of the audit team in a senior position at the client; former partner of the audit firm as director or senior employee of the client; long association with client counterparts; significant gifts or hospitality from the client.

Q:: Intimidation threat: three causes in the Study Material?
A:: Threat of replacement over disagreements on accounting principles; pressure to disproportionately reduce work in response to reduced fees; being threatened with litigation.

Q:: What are safeguards?
A:: Actions, individually or in combination, that the professional accountant takes that effectively reduce threats to comply with the fundamental principles to an acceptable level.

Q:: When threats to independence exist, what should the auditor do?
A:: Desist from the task, or eliminate the threat, or at least put in place safeguards reducing it to an acceptable level, recorded as evidence; if credible and adequate safeguards cannot be fully implemented, not accept the work.

Q:: Define professional skepticism.
A:: An attitude that includes a questioning mind, being alert to conditions which may indicate possible misstatement due to error or fraud, and a critical assessment of audit evidence.

Q:: Maintaining professional skepticism reduces which three risks?
A:: Overlooking unusual circumstances; over generalising when drawing conclusions from audit observations; using inappropriate assumptions in determining the nature, timing and extent of procedures and evaluating their results.

Q:: Which Companies Act provisions mainly address familiarity threats?
A:: The provisions on rotation of auditors.
