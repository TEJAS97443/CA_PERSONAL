## In plain words

### Where did the milk go?

Tapri Tea Co. Ltd's 40 cafés bought ₹8.10 crore of milk in 2025-26. Bunty is ready to vouch 2,400 milk bills. Lakshmi has a calculator instead.

> **Lakshmi:** How many cups did the cafés sell, Bunty?
> **Bunty:** 96 lakh, ma'am. The recipe card says 120 ml of milk a cup.
> **Lakshmi:** Then the milk should cost about ₹7 crore. Where did the other ₹1 crore go?
> **Kesar:** Customers like extra milk. Sab adjust ho jayega.

Lakshmi has not checked a single bill, yet she knows where to look. She built an **expectation** from a plausible relationship (cups sold × milk per cup × price) between **non-financial data** (cups, millilitres) and **financial data** (the price), compared it with the **recorded amount**, and found a difference too big to ignore. That is an **analytical procedure**.

The difference is not yet a misstatement. It is a question. Perhaps milk prices rose in the monsoon, perhaps the cafés waste more than the recipe card admits, perhaps someone is paying a milk vendor for milk that never came. The auditor asks management, then checks the answer against evidence, and does more work if the answer does not hold up.

Auditors use such procedures three times: at **planning** to spot risky areas, during **testing** as evidence in their own right (**substantive analytical procedures**), and at **completion** to check that the financial statements as a whole make sense.

::: analogy The electricity bill at home
Your family knows roughly what the electricity bill should be: higher in May when the AC runs, lower in December. Nobody reads every unit on the meter. When the December bill doubles, you ask around first (did someone leave the geyser on?), and if the answers do not explain it, you check the meter. Expect, compare, investigate.
:::

::: key
An analytical procedure compares a recorded amount with what it should be, given a plausible relationship. Its power depends on how good the expectation is; and a significant difference must be investigated (inquiry, then corroborating evidence), never simply accepted or simply booked as an error.
:::

## How it works

### 1. What analytical procedures are

::: definition Analytical procedures
Evaluations of financial information through analysis of plausible relationships among both financial and non-financial data. Analytical procedures also encompass such investigation as is necessary of identified fluctuations or relationships that are inconsistent with other relevant information or that differ from expected values by a significant amount. (SM 4.98)
:::

Routine checks cannot be depended on to disclose all mistakes or manipulation: in the Study Material's rent example, every monthly payment agrees with the rent agreement, and only a comparison with rents in similar locations reveals the inconsistency (SM 4.97–4.98).

Analytical procedures include **comparisons** of the entity's financial information with (SM 4.99–4.100):

- comparable information for prior periods;
- anticipated results of the entity, such as budgets or forecasts, or expectations of the auditor, such as an estimation of depreciation;
- similar industry information, such as the entity's ratio of sales to accounts receivable against industry averages or other entities of comparable size in the same industry.

They also include consideration of **relationships** (SM 4.100):

- among elements of financial information expected to conform to a predictable pattern based on the entity's experience, such as gross margin percentages;
- between financial information and relevant non-financial information, such as payroll costs to number of employees.

Methods range from simple comparisons to complex analyses using advanced statistical techniques, and may be applied to consolidated financial statements, components and individual elements of information (SM 4.100). The major types are: comparison of client and industry data; of client data with similar prior period data; with client-determined expected results; with auditor-determined expected results; and with expected results using non-financial data (SM 4.100–4.101).

### 2. Scope and objectives of SA 520

SA 520 deals with the auditor's use of analytical procedures as substantive procedures ("substantive analytical procedures"), and as procedures near the end of the audit that assist the auditor when forming an overall conclusion on the financial statements (SM 4.101). The objectives of the auditor are (SM 4.101):

- (a) ==to obtain relevant and reliable audit evidence when using substantive analytical procedures==; and
- (b) to design and perform analytical procedures near the end of the audit that assist the auditor when forming an overall conclusion as to whether the financial statements are consistent with the auditor's understanding of the entity.

Analytical procedures as risk assessment procedures at planning are dealt with under [[aud.risk.sa315|SA 315]] (SM 3.13); SA 520 covers the other two uses.

### 3. Purpose, and the Study Material's "overall tests"

Analytical procedures use comparisons and relationships to assess whether account balances or other data appear reasonable (SM 4.101). They help detect an unusual state of affairs and mistakes: a fall in the gross profit ratio compared with the previous year calls for further enquiry, as it may be due to pilferage of inventories, misappropriation of a part of the sale proceeds, or a change in the cost of sales without a corresponding increase in the sales price (SM 4.102). The same "overall tests" extend to:

- inter-firm comparison (the Statement of Profit and Loss of another entity in the same trade under similar circumstances) and comparison with the previous period, to find reasons for changes in profit;
- expense ratios to sales compared with the previous year; if differences are material, the auditor ascertains the reasons and assesses whether the accounts have been manipulated to inflate or suppress profits;
- independent verification of items with a direct relationship: import cost from the ad-valorem duty paid, a sugar mill's quantity sold from the GST paid, a manager's commission from net profit, a selling agent's commission from sales (SM 4.102–4.104).

Unusual or unexpected relationships may help the auditor identify risks of material misstatement, especially risks due to fraud (SM 4.104).

In planning, analytical procedures help the auditor understand the client's business and identify areas of potential risk, by indicating aspects and developments of which the auditor was unaware; this helps determine the nature, timing and extent of other procedures (SM 4.104).

### 5. Substantive analytical procedures, and when they fit

Substantive procedures at the assertion level may be tests of details, substantive analytical procedures, or a combination. The choice rests on the auditor's judgement of the expected effectiveness and efficiency of the available procedures in reducing audit risk at the assertion level to an acceptably low level. The auditor may inquire of management about the availability and reliability of the information needed, and may use analytical data prepared by management if satisfied it is properly prepared (SM 4.105). The factors to consider (SM 4.105–4.107):

| Factor | What the Study Material says |
|---|---|
| Availability of data | Reliable and relevant data facilitates effective analytical procedures |
| Disaggregation | The degree of disaggregation in the data directly affects its usefulness in detecting misstatements |
| Account type | Income statement accounts tend to be more predictable (accumulated transactions over a period); balance sheet accounts are a net effect at a point in time or subject to more management judgement |
| Source | Numerous, similar, routine transactions (sales, purchases) are more predictable; non-routine and estimation transactions (research, advertisement) are harder to predict |
| Predictability | More appropriate when a balance or relationship is predictable (sales and cost of sales; receivables and cash receipts): one that may reasonably be expected to exist and continue over time |
| Nature of assertion | May be more effective for some assertions (completeness, valuation) than others (rights and obligations); predictive data analytics can address completeness, valuation or measurement and occurrence |
| Inherent risk, or "what can go wrong" | Higher inherent risk may call for tests of details; for significant risks, evidence solely from substantive analytical procedures is unlikely to be sufficient |

### 6. The four techniques

| Technique (SM 4.107–4.109) | What it is | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Trend analysis** | Comparison of current data with the prior period balance or a trend in two or more prior period balances | Salary over several years: an unusual increase may indicate payments to fake employees |
| **Ratio analysis** | Relationships between accounts; useful for asset and liability accounts as well as revenue and expense; compared over time, within the group or with the industry | Receivables or inventory turnover; freight as a percentage of sales; gross profit ratio |
| **Reasonableness tests** | Do not rely on prior periods but on non-financial data for the audit period; more applicable to income statement accounts and certain accruals or prepayments | Interest expense against interest-bearing obligations; raw material consumption to production; rental revenue from occupancy |
| **Structural modelling** | A statistical model built from financial and/or non-financial data of prior periods to predict current balances | Linear regression |

### 7. The four requirements when performing them

When designing and performing substantive analytical procedures, alone or with tests of details, as substantive procedures in accordance with [[aud.risk.sa330|SA 330]], the auditor shall (SM 4.109–4.110):

::: steps
1. **Determine the suitability** of particular substantive analytical procedures for given assertions, taking account of the assessed risks of material misstatement and tests of details, if any, for these assertions;
2. **Evaluate the reliability of data** from which the auditor's expectation of recorded amounts or ratios is developed, taking account of source, comparability, and nature and relevance of information available, and controls over preparation;
3. **Develop an expectation** of recorded amounts or ratios and evaluate whether the expectation is sufficiently precise to identify a misstatement that, individually or when aggregated with other misstatements, may cause the financial statements to be materially misstated; and
4. **Determine the amount of any difference** of recorded amounts from expected values that is acceptable without further investigation.
:::

**Suitability** (SM 4.110–4.111). Substantive analytical procedures are generally ==more applicable to large volumes of transactions that tend to be predictable over time==; they assume relationships exist and continue in the absence of known conditions to the contrary; even an unsophisticated model may work (a known number of employees at fixed rates of pay can estimate total payroll with high accuracy, reducing tests of details). Different procedures give different assurance: predicting rental income from rates, number of apartments and vacancy rates can provide persuasive evidence and may eliminate the need for tests of details, provided the elements are verified; comparing gross margin percentages to confirm revenue gives less persuasive evidence, useful as corroboration. If controls over sales order processing are weak, more reliance goes on tests of details for receivables. A procedure may also be combined with tests of details on the same assertion: an analysis of the ageing of receivables alongside tests of subsequent cash receipts for valuation.

**Reliability of data** (SM 4.112–4.113). Relevant matters:

1. **Source**: information may be more reliable from independent sources outside the entity;
2. **Comparability**: broad industry data may need to be supplemented to be comparable to an entity that produces and sells specialised products;
3. **Nature and relevance**: for example, whether budgets have been established as results to be expected rather than as goals to be achieved;
4. **Controls over preparation** designed to ensure completeness, accuracy and validity, for example over the preparation, review and maintenance of budgets.

The auditor may test the operating effectiveness of controls over the information (controls over recording unit sales, tested with controls over sales invoicing), or consider whether the information was itself subjected to audit testing (SM 4.112–4.113; [[aud.evid.sa500|SA 500]] on information produced by the entity).

**Precision of the expectation** (SM 4.113) depends on: (i) the accuracy with which expected results can be predicted (gross profit margins are more consistent than discretionary expenses such as research or advertising); (ii) the degree to which information can be disaggregated (sections of an operation or components of a diversified entity rather than the entity as a whole); (iii) the availability of financial and non-financial information (budgets, forecasts, units produced or sold), and its reliability.

**Acceptable difference** (SM 4.113–4.114) is influenced by materiality and consistency with the desired level of assurance. SA 330 requires more persuasive evidence the higher the assessed risk, so **as the assessed risk increases, the difference acceptable without investigation decreases**.

### 8. Investigating the results

If analytical procedures identify fluctuations or relationships that are inconsistent with other relevant information or that differ from expected values by a significant amount, the auditor shall investigate such differences by (SM 4.114):

- (i) **inquiring of management and obtaining appropriate audit evidence relevant to management's responses**, evaluating the responses against the auditor's understanding of the entity and other audit evidence; and
- (ii) **performing other audit procedures as necessary in the circumstances**, for example when management cannot explain, or the explanation with its evidence is not adequate.

### 9. The overall conclusion

Analytical procedures near the end of the audit are intended to corroborate conclusions formed on individual components or elements of the financial statements, helping the auditor draw reasonable conclusions on which to base the opinion. If they identify a previously unrecognised risk of material misstatement, SA 315 requires the auditor to revise the risk assessment and modify the further planned audit procedures (SM 4.114–4.115).

## Worked examples

::::: example Where did the milk go?
Tapri's 40 cafés recorded milk purchases of ₹8,10,00,000 for 2025-26. Iyer & Associates obtain:

- cups of chai sold, from the point-of-sale system (its controls over unit sales were tested along with sales invoicing): 96,00,000;
- milk per cup, from the standard recipe: 120 ml, with a normal wastage allowance of 5%;
- the milk supply contract rate: ₹58 a litre for the year.

The acceptable difference without further investigation is set at ₹35,00,000.

Required:

(i) Compute the expected milk cost and the difference from the recorded amount.

(ii) Kesar explains that milk "cost more in the monsoon". Purchase records show an average of ₹61 a litre. Evaluate the explanation and state what the auditor should do next.

**(i)** Expected litres = 96,00,000 cups × 0.120 litre × 1.05 = **12,09,600 litres**. Expected cost = 12,09,600 × ₹58 = **₹7,01,56,800**. Difference = ₹8,10,00,000 − ₹7,01,56,800 = **₹1,08,43,200**, 15.46% of the expectation and well above the acceptable ₹35,00,000. It must be investigated.

At the evidenced average of ₹61, expected cost = 12,09,600 × ₹61 = ₹7,37,85,600; the remaining difference is ₹72,14,400, still above ₹35,00,000. :::: example Two cafés, one gross profit
Tapri's two Indore cafés sell the same menu at the same prices:

| Café | Year | Sales (₹ lakh) | Gross profit (₹ lakh) |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Vijay Nagar | 2024-25 | 360.00 | 223.20 |
| Vijay Nagar | 2025-26 | 380.00 | 193.80 |
| Palasia | 2024-25 | 310.00 | 192.20 |
| Palasia | 2025-26 | 330.00 | 204.60 |

Comment on what the auditor should infer and do, with reference to SA 520.

::: answer Model answer
**Relevant provision.** As per SA 520, analytical procedures are evaluations of financial information through analysis of plausible relationships, and encompass investigation of fluctuations that are inconsistent with other relevant information or differ from expected values by a significant amount. A fall in the gross profit ratio may be due to pilferage of inventories, misappropriation of a part of the sale proceeds, or a change in the cost of sales without a corresponding increase in the sales price. The auditor shall investigate by inquiring of management, obtaining audit evidence relevant to the responses, and performing other procedures as necessary.

**Facts and analysis.** Gross profit ratios: Vijay Nagar 62.0% in 2024-25 and 51.0% in 2025-26; Palasia 62.0% in both years. At last year's ratio Vijay Nagar's gross profit would be ₹235.60 lakh, so ₹41.80 lakh is unexplained. A general rise in costs would have hit Palasia, with the same menu and prices, too: it did not. The relationship is inconsistent with other relevant information (intra-firm comparison).

**Conclusion.** The fall points to a cause specific to Vijay Nagar, such as pilferage of inventories, misappropriation of a part of the sale proceeds, or a rise in that café's cost of sales (wastage, a different supplier) not matched by its prices; the analytical procedure only signals where to look. The auditor should inquire of the café and regional management, corroborate the answers (stock records and physical count against consumption, daily cash and card collections against the point-of-sale record, discounts and complimentary items), extend tests of details there, and consider the implications for the risk of fraud and for controls at other cafés.
:::

:::: example Kesar's data pack
For substantive analytical procedures on revenue, Kesar hands Iyer & Associates (a) the 2025-26 budget, prepared as "stretch targets to motivate the café managers"; (b) a national FMCG industry report on packaged foods; and (c) monthly unit-sales reports from a new café billing app, whose controls have not been tested.

Advise the auditor on whether this data can be relied on.

::: answer Model answer
**Relevant provision.** As per SA 520, the auditor shall evaluate the reliability of data from which the expectation is developed, taking account of: the source of the information (more reliable from independent sources outside the entity); its comparability (broad industry data may need to be supplemented to be comparable to an entity with specialised products); its nature and relevance (whether budgets have been established as results to be expected rather than as goals to be achieved); and the controls over its preparation designed to ensure completeness, accuracy and validity. **Analysis.** (a) The budget is a set of goals to be achieved, not results to be expected: unsuitable for an expectation. (b) Packaged-foods data is broad and not comparable to a café chain: it needs supplementing with café-industry data before use. (c) The app's unit-sales data is internal and its controls are untested.

**Conclusion.** The auditor should not build expectations on (a), should use (b) only once made comparable, and should test the controls over the app's recording of unit sales (with the tests of controls over sales invoicing) or test the data itself before using (c). Until reliable data is available, revenue assertions should rest more on tests of details.

## Under the hood

### Small print

- **Analytical review.** The Study Material also calls the calculation of ratios, trends and comparisons "analytical review" (SM 4.104).
- **The Study Material's own illustration.** Client inventory turnover 2.8 and 2.9 against an industry 3.1 and 2.8, and gross margin 22.5% and 22.7% against 23.6% and 22.2%, show the kind of client-versus-industry comparison examined (SM 4.100).
- **Going concern signals.** Analytical procedures can surface "adverse key financial ratios" or "substantial operating losses", which the Study Material lists among the events or conditions that may cast significant doubt on going concern (SM 7.17; [[aud.complete.sa570|SA 570]]).

::: trick Trend looks back, reasonableness looks sideways
**Trend analysis** compares with **prior periods**. A **reasonableness test** uses **non-financial data of the same period** (rooms, rates, units) and ignores prior periods. **Ratio analysis** relates **one account to another**. **Structural modelling** fits a **statistical model** to past data. Holds for the Study Material's four techniques.
:::

## Answer frame

### "Identifies inconsistencies: what should the auditor do?" (3 or 4 marks)

1. **Provision**: "As per SA 520, if analytical procedures identify fluctuations or relationships that are inconsistent with other relevant information or that differ from expected values by a significant amount, the auditor shall investigate such differences by:"
2. **(i) Inquiry and evidence**: inquiring of management and obtaining appropriate audit evidence relevant to management's responses, evaluated against the auditor's understanding of the entity and other evidence.
3. **(ii) Other procedures**: performing other audit procedures as necessary, for example when management cannot explain or the explanation is not adequate.
4. **Application**: "In the given case, CA … should …".

### "Distinguish trend analysis and reasonableness tests"

| Point | Trend analysis | Reasonableness test |
|---|---|---|
| Basis | Prior period balances or a trend of several periods | Non-financial data for the audit period |
| Relies on past periods | Yes | No |
| Best for | Accounts that move in a pattern over time | Income statement accounts, certain accruals and prepayments |
| Example | Salary over five years | Interest expense against interest-bearing obligations |

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: booking the difference as the error
The milk is ₹1.08 crore over the expectation, so Bunty proposes a ₹1.08 crore adjustment. **Fix:** a difference is a signal, not a misstatement. Investigate first: inquiry with corroborating evidence, then other procedures.
:::

::: mistake Accepting "milk cost more in the monsoon"
**Fix:** management's response needs audit evidence; then re-test the expectation with it. The ₹61 rate still left ₹72 lakh unexplained.
:::

::: mistake "Analytical procedures are for planning only"
**Fix:** required at planning and at completion, and often used in testing (SM 4.104, 4.129).
:::

::: mistake "Substantive analytical procedures suit small volumes"
**Fix:** they are generally more applicable to large volumes of transactions that tend to be predictable over time; and for significant risks, evidence solely from them is unlikely to be sufficient (SM 4.107, 4.110).
:::

## Recap

- Analytical procedures: evaluations of financial information through analysis of plausible relationships among both financial and non-financial data, including investigating significant or inconsistent fluctuations.
- Objectives: relevant and reliable evidence from substantive analytical procedures; an overall conclusion near the end on consistency with the auditor's understanding.
- Used at planning (required), testing (often), completion (required).
- Factors: availability of data, disaggregation, account type, source, predictability, nature of assertion, inherent risk.
- Techniques: trend analysis, ratio analysis, reasonableness tests, structural modelling.
- Four requirements: suitability; reliability of data; a sufficiently precise expectation; the acceptable difference (smaller as risk rises).
- Investigate: inquiry with evidence for the responses; other procedures as necessary. A new risk at completion means revising the assessment (SA 315). (Where did the milk go? Ask, then check.)

A:: Evaluations of financial information through analysis of plausible relationships among both financial and non-financial data, including such investigation as is necessary of fluctuations or relationships that are inconsistent with other relevant information or differ from expected values by a significant amount.

Q:: SA 520: what are the objectives of the auditor?
A:: (a) To obtain relevant and reliable audit evidence when using substantive analytical procedures; (b) to design and perform analytical procedures near the end of the audit that assist when forming an overall conclusion as to whether the financial statements are consistent with the auditor's understanding of the entity.

Q:: Name the four techniques of substantive analytical procedures.
A:: Trend analysis, ratio analysis, reasonableness tests, structural modelling.

Q:: SA 520: the four requirements when designing and performing substantive analytical procedures?
A:: Determine suitability for the assertions; evaluate the reliability of the data; develop a sufficiently precise expectation; determine the difference acceptable without further investigation.

Q:: Which four matters are relevant to the reliability of data for substantive analytical procedures?
A:: Source of the information; its comparability; its nature and relevance (e.g. budgets as expected results, not goals); controls over its preparation.

Q:: SA 520: how does the auditor investigate a significant difference?
A:: By inquiring of management and obtaining appropriate audit evidence relevant to management's responses, and by performing other audit procedures as necessary in the circumstances.

Q:: How does a higher assessed risk affect the acceptable difference?
A:: It decreases it, because SA 330 requires more persuasive evidence the higher the assessed risk.

Q:: How does a reasonableness test differ from trend analysis?
A:: It does not rely on prior periods but on non-financial data for the audit period (e.g. occupancy rates to estimate rental income).

Q:: Name the seven factors for substantive analytical procedures.
A:: Availability of data, disaggregation, account type, source, predictability, nature of assertion, inherent risk ("what can go wrong").

Q:: At what stages are analytical procedures used?
A:: Required at planning and completion; often used during testing.

Q:: What if analytical procedures at the end of the audit reveal a previously unrecognised risk?
A:: SA 315 requires the auditor to revise the assessment of the risks of material misstatement and modify the further planned audit procedures.
