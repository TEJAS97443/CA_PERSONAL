## In plain words

### The ₹38 bus ticket and the ₹45,000 shortage

Lakshmi is planning the audit of Tapri Tea Co. Ltd. Bunty has brought a spreadsheet with 14,000 rows.

> **Bunty:** Ma'am, should I check every row? Even the ₹38 bus ticket?
> **Lakshmi:** No. First decide what size of mistake would change the mind of someone reading these accounts.
> **Kesar:** Then a ₹45,000 shortage at the Pune café is nothing, na? Sab adjust ho jayega.
> **Lakshmi:** If the café manager stole it, Kesar-ji, it is not nothing.

A misstatement is **material** when it could reasonably change the economic decisions people take on the basis of the accounts. Before the audit starts, the auditor sets a rupee figure for this, called **materiality**, usually a percentage of a figure users watch closely (the **benchmark**, such as profit before tax). The figure tells the auditor where to dig and how deep.

It is a guide, not a wall. Small errors can add up, so the auditor also sets a lower working figure, **performance materiality**, as a safety margin. And some items are material by **nature**, whatever their size: a small fraud, or a disclosure the law requires.

::: analogy The weighing scale at the kirana store
A shopkeeper selling rice does not care if a scoop is two grains light. He cares if the bag is 50 grams short, because the customer would. So he keeps his scale a little tighter than 50 grams, since several slightly short scoops make one short bag. Materiality is the 50 grams; performance materiality is the tighter setting. And one weevil in the rice matters, however little it weighs.
:::

::: key
Materiality is the size (or nature) of misstatement that could influence users' economic decisions. The auditor sets it by judgment from a benchmark and a percentage, sets performance materiality lower to cover misstatements that add up, applies both from planning to the opinion, revises them when circumstances change, and documents them.
:::

## How it works

### 1. When is a misstatement material?

::: definition Material misstatement
Misstatements, including omissions, are considered to be material if they, individually or in the aggregate, could reasonably be expected to influence the economic decisions of users taken on the basis of the financial statements (SA 320; SM 3.16).
:::

An audit seeks reasonable assurance that the financial statements as a whole are free from material misstatement, so it is geared towards the significant risks of material misstatement (SM 3.16). **Materiality is not always a matter of relative size**: a small amount lost through the fraud of certain employees can indicate a serious flaw in internal control that needs immediate attention to avoid greater losses (SM 3.17).

### 2. What SA 320 covers

SA 320 deals with the auditor's responsibility to apply the concept of materiality ==in planning and performing an audit== of financial statements. The concept is also applied in evaluating the effect of identified misstatements on the audit and of uncorrected misstatements on the financial statements, and in forming the opinion (SM 3.17).

Financial reporting frameworks generally explain that (SM 3.17):

- misstatements, including omissions, are material if they, individually or in the aggregate, could reasonably be expected to influence users' economic decisions;
- judgments about materiality are made in the light of surrounding circumstances, and are affected by the **size or nature** of a misstatement, or both; and
- judgments are based on the **common financial information needs of users as a group**; the possible effect on specific individual users, whose needs may vary widely, is not considered.

If the applicable financial reporting framework does not discuss materiality, these characteristics provide the auditor with the frame of reference.

### 3. What planning materiality is used for

Judgments about the size of misstatements that will be material provide a basis for (SM 3.18):

- (a) determining the nature, timing and extent of risk assessment procedures;
- (b) identifying and assessing the risks of material misstatement; and
- (c) determining the nature, timing and extent of further audit procedures.

The materiality determined when planning **does not necessarily establish an amount below which uncorrected misstatements, individually or in aggregate, will always be evaluated as immaterial**. The circumstances of some misstatements may cause the auditor to evaluate them as material even if they are below materiality. Although it is not practicable to design audit procedures to detect misstatements that could be material solely because of their nature, the auditor considers not only the size but also the nature of uncorrected misstatements, and the particular circumstances of their occurrence, when evaluating their effect (SM 3.18).

The auditor applies professional judgment in determining materiality, choosing the benchmark and its level; materiality forms the basis for determining the audit scope and the levels of testing (SM 3.18).

The significance of an item is viewed from different perspectives: its impact on the profit and loss, or on the balance sheet, or on the total of the category of expenditure or income to which it pertains, and its comparison with the corresponding figure for the previous year (SM 3.18).

**A statutory disclosure requirement makes an item material irrespective of its amount** (SM 3.18–3.19). The Study Material's examples from Division I of Schedule III to the Companies Act, 2013:

- any item of income or expenditure exceeding one per cent of the revenue from operations or ₹1,00,000, whichever is higher, is disclosed separately;
- the notes disclose the shares held by each shareholder holding more than 5 per cent shares, with the number of shares held.

::: warning
The Schedule III figures are as stated in the Study Material. Verify against the Study Material and Statutory Update for your attempt.
:::

### 4. A matter of professional judgment

The auditor's determination of materiality is a matter of professional judgment, affected by the auditor's perception of users' financial information needs. It is reasonable to assume that users (SM 3.19):

- (a) have a reasonable knowledge of business and economic activities and accounting, and a willingness to study the financial statements with reasonable diligence;
- (b) understand that financial statements are prepared, presented and audited to levels of materiality;
- (c) recognise the uncertainties inherent in amounts based on estimates, judgment and future events; and
- (d) make reasonable economic decisions on the basis of the information in the financial statements.

### 5. Setting the figures when planning

When establishing the [[aud.plan.sa300|overall audit strategy]], the auditor shall determine **materiality for the financial statements as a whole**. If particular classes of transactions, account balances or disclosures exist for which misstatements of lesser amounts could reasonably be expected to influence users, the auditor shall also determine the **materiality level or levels** for them (SM 3.20).

### 6. Choosing the benchmark and the percentage

A percentage is often applied to a chosen benchmark as a starting point. **Factors that may affect the identification of an appropriate benchmark** (SM 3.20):

1. The elements of the financial statements (assets, liabilities, equity, revenue, expenses).
2. Whether there are items on which users' attention tends to be focused (for evaluating financial performance, users may focus on profit, revenue or net assets).
3. The nature of the entity, where it is in its life cycle, its industry and economic environment, its ownership structure and the way it is financed (an entity financed solely by debt: users may emphasise assets and claims on them more than earnings).
4. The relative volatility of the benchmark.

**Examples of benchmarks** include categories of reported income such as profit before tax, total revenue, gross profit and total expenses, total equity or net asset value. **Profit before tax from continuing operations** is often used for profit-oriented entities; when it is volatile, other benchmarks such as gross profit or total revenues may be more appropriate (SM 3.20–3.21).

The **relevant financial data** for the benchmark ordinarily include prior periods' results and financial positions, the period-to-date results and position, and budgets or forecasts for the current period, adjusted for significant changes in the entity's circumstances (such as a significant business acquisition) and in its industry or economic environment. If profit before tax shows an exceptional decrease or increase, the auditor may determine materiality from a **normalised profit before tax from continuing operations based on past results** (SM 3.21).

The **percentage** is also judgment, and it is related to the benchmark: a percentage applied to profit before tax from continuing operations will normally be higher than one applied to total revenue. The auditor may consider **5% of profit before tax from continuing operations** appropriate for a profit-oriented manufacturer, and **1% of total revenue or total expenses** for a not-for-profit entity; higher or lower percentages may be appropriate in other circumstances (SM 3.21).

### 7. Lower levels for particular items

Factors indicating that particular classes of transactions, account balances or disclosures need materiality levels below materiality for the financial statements as a whole (SM 3.22):

1. Whether law, regulations or the framework affect users' expectations about the measurement or disclosure of certain items, such as related party transactions and the remuneration of management and those charged with governance.
2. The key disclosures in the entity's industry, such as research and development costs for a pharmaceutical company.
3. Whether attention is focused on a particular aspect of the business that is separately disclosed, such as a newly acquired business.

### 8. Performance materiality: the safety margin

It is difficult to design tests for individual material misstatements, and misstatements are likely to be material in aggregate (SM 3.19).

::: definition Performance materiality
The amount or amounts set by the auditor at less than materiality for the financial statements as a whole to reduce to an appropriately low level the probability that the aggregate of uncorrected and undetected misstatements exceeds materiality for the financial statements as a whole. If applicable, performance materiality also refers to the amount or amounts set by the auditor at less than the materiality level or levels for particular classes of transactions, account balances or disclosures (SM 3.19).
:::

Set below overall materiality, it lowers the risk that the auditor fails to identify misstatements that are material when added together (SM 3.20).

### 9. Revising as the audit progresses

Materiality (and any lower levels) may need revision because of a **change in circumstances** during the audit (for example, a decision to dispose of a major part of the business), **new information**, or a change in the auditor's understanding from further audit procedures. If actual results are likely to be substantially different from the anticipated results used initially, the auditor revises materiality. If a **lower** materiality is appropriate, the auditor shall determine whether to revise performance materiality, and whether the nature, timing and extent of further audit procedures remain appropriate (SM 3.22).

### 10. Materiality and audit risk, throughout the audit

Materiality and [[aud.risk.model|audit risk]] are considered throughout the audit, in particular when (SM 3.23):

- (a) identifying and assessing the risks of material misstatement;
- (b) determining the nature, timing and extent of further audit procedures; and
- (c) evaluating the effect of uncorrected misstatements, if any, on the financial statements and in forming the opinion.

There is an **inverse relationship between materiality and the degree of audit risk**: the higher the materiality level, the lower the audit risk, and vice versa (SM 3.81). At the end of the audit, uncorrected misstatements are evaluated under [[aud.complete.sa450|SA 450]], after reassessing materiality to confirm that it remains appropriate in the context of the actual financial results (SM 7.26).

## Formats and formulas

::: formula Materiality for the financial statements as a whole
$$\text{Materiality} = \text{Benchmark} \times \text{Percentage}$$
A starting point, not a mechanical rule: a percentage is often applied to a chosen benchmark as a starting point (SM 3.20). Benchmark: the figure users focus on, from relevant financial data, normalised if exceptional. Percentage: professional judgment (for example 5% of profit before tax from continuing operations, 1% of total revenue or expenses for a not-for-profit entity) (SM 3.20–3.21).
:::

### The materiality working paper

The audit documentation shall include these amounts **and the factors considered in their determination** (SM 3.23). *Reproduce the four items from memory, in this order.*

| Item to document | What it records |
|---|---|
| (a) Materiality for the financial statements as a whole | Benchmark, percentage, amount, reasons |
| (b) Materiality level(s) for particular classes, balances or disclosures, if applicable | The items, amounts, reasons |
| (c) Performance materiality | Amount(s), reasons |
| (d) Any revision of (a) to (c) as the audit progressed | What changed, revised amounts |

:::: example Kesar's three "small" things
For 2025-26, Iyer & Associates have set materiality for Tapri's financial statements as a whole at ₹1.70 crore. Near completion, these remain uncorrected:

(a) Repairs of ₹0.65 crore capitalised as machinery, work-in-progress overvalued by ₹0.55 crore, and March sales of ₹0.60 crore recorded although the goods were delivered in April. Kesar: "Each one is below your ₹1.70 crore. Ignore all three."

(b) The Pune café manager took ₹45,000 from cash over the year through fake petty cash vouchers.

(c) The notes omit the list of shareholders holding more than 5% of the shares.

Comment, with reasons, on whether each is material.

::: answer Model answer
**Relevant provision.** As per SA 320, misstatements, including omissions, are material if they, individually or in the aggregate, could reasonably be expected to influence the economic decisions of users taken on the basis of the financial statements. Judgments about materiality are affected by the size or nature of a misstatement, or both. Materiality determined at planning does not establish an amount below which uncorrected misstatements will always be evaluated as immaterial.

**(a)** Each misstatement is below ₹1.70 crore, but together they total ₹0.65 + ₹0.55 + ₹0.60 = **₹1.80 crore**, which exceeds materiality, and all three overstate profit. The misstatements are **material in the aggregate**. Kesar's view is not correct. The auditor should request management to correct them; if they remain uncorrected, their effect on the opinion is evaluated under SA 450 (SM 7.25–7.26).

**(b)** Materiality is not always a matter of relative size. A small amount lost by fraudulent practices of an employee can indicate a serious flaw in internal control requiring immediate attention to avoid greater losses. The ₹45,000 is small, but **its nature makes it significant**: it calls for attention to cash controls across the cafés and cannot be ignored as trivial.

**(c)** If there is a statutory requirement of disclosure, the item is considered **material irrespective of the amount**. Division I of Schedule III requires disclosure of shares held by each shareholder holding more than 5 per cent shares. The omission is material.

## Under the hood

### Tolerable misstatement and the sample

In audit sampling, **tolerable misstatement** is a monetary amount set by the auditor in respect of which the auditor seeks an appropriate level of assurance that it is not exceeded by the actual misstatement in the population (SM 4.67). An increase in it decreases the sample size (SM 4.59); ICAI asked this in January 2026 MCQ 2 and MTP September 2024 Series I MCQ 8. Sampling is in [[aud.evid.sa530|SA 530]].

### Materiality also decides how much evidence

Less audit evidence is required for assertions less material to users of the financial statements, and more for those more material (SM 4.13); see [[aud.evid.sa500|SA 500]].

## Answer frame

### A case on materiality

1. **Provision**: the SA 320 definition, plus the rule the case needs (benchmark factors, nature over size, statutory disclosure, revision, performance materiality).
2. **Facts**: the amounts and their comparison with materiality, or the nature of the item.
3. **Analysis**: size, nature, aggregate, statutory requirement.
4. **Conclusion**: "material / not material", and the consequence (evaluate under SA 450, revise performance materiality, document).

### "Distinguish materiality from performance materiality"

| Point | Materiality | Performance materiality |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Misstatements that could reasonably influence users' economic decisions | Amount set below materiality |
| Purpose | Frame for the whole audit and the opinion | Reduces to an appropriately low level the probability that uncorrected and undetected misstatements together exceed materiality |
| Level | Benchmark × percentage | Less than materiality (or than a lower level) |
| Documented | Yes, with factors | Yes, with factors |

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: "below materiality, so ignore it"
Bunty clears three misstatements one by one because each is under ₹1.70 crore. **Fix:** materiality is "individually or in the aggregate"; add them up. And a small fraud or a missing statutory disclosure is material by nature.
:::

::: mistake Materiality set once and forgotten
**Fix:** it is applied in planning, performing and evaluating, and revised when circumstances or the auditor's understanding change (SM 3.22–3.23).
:::

::: mistake Using this year's profit in a freak year
**Fix:** an exceptional decrease or increase calls for a normalised profit before tax from continuing operations based on past results (SM 3.21).
:::

::: mistake Performance materiality above materiality
**Fix:** by definition it is set **at less than** materiality for the financial statements as a whole. An amount at or above it is not performance materiality.
:::

## Recap

- Material: misstatements, including omissions, that individually or in the aggregate could reasonably be expected to influence users' economic decisions. Size or nature, or both; users as a group.
- Planning materiality guides risk assessment procedures, the assessment of risks and further audit procedures; it is not a bright line.
- Statutory disclosure items are material irrespective of amount; a small fraud can be material by nature.
- Benchmark factors: elements, users' focus, nature of the entity (life cycle, industry, ownership, financing), volatility. Profit before tax from continuing operations is common; normalise an exceptional year.
- Example percentages: 5% of profit before tax (profit-oriented manufacturer); 1% of revenue or expenses (not-for-profit).
- Performance materiality: set below materiality, to reduce to an appropriately low level the probability that uncorrected plus undetected misstatements exceed materiality.
- Revise for changed circumstances, new information or better understanding; if lower, revisit performance materiality and further procedures.
- Document materiality, item levels, performance materiality and revisions, with factors. (Mind the weevil, not the grain.)

## Flashcards

Q:: SA 320: when are misstatements material?
A:: When, individually or in the aggregate, they could reasonably be expected to influence the economic decisions of users taken on the basis of the financial statements.

Q:: SA 320: define performance materiality.
A:: The amount(s) set by the auditor at less than materiality for the financial statements as a whole, to reduce to an appropriately low level the probability that the aggregate of uncorrected and undetected misstatements exceeds that materiality (and, if applicable, set below the levels for particular classes, balances or disclosures).

Q:: SA 320: four factors affecting the identification of an appropriate benchmark?
A:: The elements of the financial statements; items on which users' attention tends to be focused; the nature of the entity, its life cycle, industry, economic environment, ownership and financing; and the relative volatility of the benchmark.

Q:: SA 320: examples of benchmarks?
A:: Profit before tax, total revenue, gross profit, total expenses, total equity or net asset value. Profit before tax from continuing operations is often used for profit-oriented entities.

Q:: SA 320: the Study Material's example percentages?
A:: 5% of profit before tax from continuing operations for a profit-oriented manufacturer; 1% of total revenue or total expenses for a not-for-profit entity.

Q:: SA 320: what four things must the audit documentation include?
A:: Materiality for the financial statements as a whole; materiality levels for particular classes, balances or disclosures, if applicable; performance materiality; and any revision of these, each with the factors considered.

Q:: SA 320: what four assumptions may the auditor make about users?
A:: They have reasonable knowledge of business, economics and accounting and will study the statements diligently; understand that statements are prepared and audited to levels of materiality; recognise uncertainties in estimates; and make reasonable economic decisions on the information.

Q:: SA 320: three factors indicating a lower materiality level for particular items?
A:: Law, regulation or the framework affecting users' expectations (related party transactions, remuneration of management and TCWG); key industry disclosures (R&D costs for a pharmaceutical company); attention on a separately disclosed aspect (a newly acquired business).

Q:: SA 320: when is materiality revised?
A:: On a change in circumstances during the audit (such as a decision to dispose of a major part of the business), new information, or a change in the auditor's understanding; and when actual results are likely to differ substantially from those used initially.

Q:: What is the relationship between materiality and audit risk?
A:: Inverse: the higher the materiality level, the lower the audit risk, and vice versa.

Q:: Is an item that law requires to be disclosed material if its amount is small?
A:: Yes. A statutory disclosure requirement makes the item material irrespective of its amount.
