## In plain words

### BrewBucks opens next door

BrewBucks has opened a café across the road from Tapri's busiest outlet in Indore.

> **Kesar:** We will be the cheapest, the most premium, and the favourite of every college student. All three, from Monday!
> **Ananya:** The cheapest needs a company that hates waste; the most premium needs one that spends on being unique; the students need one that knows one small segment inside out. Three different companies, Kesar-ji.
> **Dev:** Pick one. Then I will discount it.

A company has a **competitive advantage** when its profits are higher than the average for its industry, because it does something rivals cannot copy, or find too costly to copy. Michael Porter says there are three bases for it, the **generic strategies**:

- **Cost leadership**: be the lowest-cost producer for the whole market, selling standard products to price-sensitive buyers. Lower costs let you charge less and still earn satisfactory profits.
- **Differentiation**: make something the whole market sees as unique, and charge a premium to buyers who care less about price.
- **Focus**: serve one narrow segment better than the broad-market players do: either on price within that segment (**focused cost leadership**) or with unique features for it (**focused differentiation**).

The Study Material adds a fourth position, the **best-cost provider**: more value for money, by offering comparable quality at a lower price than rivals, or much better quality at a similar price.

::: analogy The Indian food street
The ₹20 vada pav sold at every station, made the same way in huge numbers, is cost leadership. The famous biryani chain whose name alone fills the room at a premium is differentiation. The Jain tiffin service for six housing societies, cheaper than any other Jain caterer there, is focused cost leadership; the caterer who does only royal-themed weddings in Udaipur is focused differentiation. And the new thali chain serving restaurant-quality food at dhaba prices is the best-cost provider.
:::

::: key
Competitive advantage is profitability above the industry average that rivals cannot duplicate. Porter's three generic strategies, **cost leadership, differentiation and focus** (focused cost leadership or focused differentiation), are three bases for it; the **best-cost provider** gives more value for money by combining low cost with upscale features. Each needs different organisational arrangements, controls and incentives.
:::

## How it works

### Competitive advantage

If a company's strategies result in superior performance relative to rivals, it is said to have a **competitive advantage**. It allows a firm to gain an edge over rivals when competing (SM 3.24).

::: definition Competitive advantage
"A set of unique features of a company and its products that are perceived by the target market as significant and superior to the competition." An organisation has competitive advantage if its **profitability is higher than the average profitability** for all companies in its industry (SM 3.24).
:::

It is achieved when the firm successfully formulates and implements a value creation strategy and other firms are **unable to duplicate it or find it too costly to imitate**; a firm can be said to have achieved it only after other firms' efforts to duplicate or imitate it fail (SM 3.24). "If you don't have a competitive advantage, don't compete" (Jack Welch). Whether it lasts depends on the durability, transferability, imitability and appropriability of the resources behind it ([[sm.int.resources|resources and core competence]]).

### Porter's three bases

According to Porter, strategies allow organisations to gain competitive advantage from three different bases: **cost leadership, differentiation and focus**. Porter called them **generic** because they can be pursued by **any type or size of business firm, and even by not-for-profit organisations** (SM 3.25).

- **Cost leadership** emphasises producing standardised products at a very low per-unit cost for consumers who are price-sensitive.
- **Differentiation** aims at producing products and services considered unique industry-wide, directed at consumers who are relatively price-insensitive.
- **Focus** means producing products and services that fulfil the needs of small groups of consumers with very specific tastes (SM 3.25–3.26).

Porter's strategies imply **different organisational arrangements, control procedures and incentive systems**. Larger firms with greater access to resources typically compete on cost leadership and/or differentiation; smaller firms often compete on focus (SM 3.26).

Porter also stresses cost-benefit analysis of **"sharing opportunities"** among the firm's existing and potential business units (sharing activities and resources lowers costs or raises differentiation) and the effective **"transfer" of skills and expertise** among autonomous business units (SM 3.26).

### Cost leadership

A low-cost competitive strategy that aims at the **broad mass market**. It requires vigorous pursuit of cost reduction in **procurement, production, storage and distribution**, and economies in overhead costs. Because of its lower costs, the cost leader can charge a lower price than most competitors and still earn satisfactory profits: McDonald's and Decathlon are the Study Material's examples (SM 3.26–3.27).

- A primary reason for **forward, backward and horizontal integration** is to gain cost leadership benefits. Generally, cost leadership must be pursued **in conjunction with differentiation** (SM 3.27).
- Cost elements that affect the choice: economies or diseconomies of scale, learning and experience curve effects, capacity utilisation, linkages with suppliers and distributors; also the potential for sharing costs and knowledge within the organisation, R&D costs, labour costs, tax rates, energy costs and shipping costs. Sharing resources internally to build advantage is the **synergy benefit** (SM 3.27).
- It is especially effective **when the market has many price-sensitive buyers, when there are few ways to achieve product differentiation, when buyers do not care much about brand differences, or when there are many buyers with significant bargaining power**. The idea is to underprice competitors, gain market share and drive some competitors out (SM 3.27).
- A successful cost leadership strategy **permeates the entire firm**: high efficiency, low overheads, limited perks, intolerance of waste, intensive screening of budget requests, wide spans of control, rewards linked to cost containment, broad employee participation in cost control (SM 3.27).
- **Risks**: competitors may imitate it, driving industry profits down; technological breakthroughs may make it ineffective; buyer interest may swing to differentiating features other than price (SM 3.27–3.28).

**Achieving cost leadership** (SM 3.28): (1) prompt forecasting of demand; (2) optimum utilisation of resources; (3) economies of scale, so lower per-unit cost; (4) standardisation of products for mass production (McDonald's); (5) investment in cost-saving and advanced technologies; (6) resistance to differentiation till it becomes essential.

**Disadvantages** (SM 3.29): (1) the cost advantage may not last long, as competitors may imitate cost reduction techniques; (2) it succeeds only if the firm achieves higher sales volume; (3) cost leaders keep costs low by minimising advertising, market research and R&D, which can prove expensive in the long run; (4) technological advancement is a great threat.

### Differentiation

Aimed at the **broad mass market**, it involves creating a product or service that customers perceive as **unique**. The uniqueness can come from **product design, brand image, features, technology, dealer network or customer service**, and lets the business **charge a premium**: Domino's "30 minutes or free" is the Study Material's example (SM 3.29).

- It does not guarantee competitive advantage if standard products sufficiently meet customer needs or rapid imitation is possible; durable products protected by barriers to quick imitation are better. Successful differentiation can mean greater product flexibility, compatibility, lower costs, improved service, less maintenance, greater convenience or more features (SM 3.29).
- It should be pursued **only after a careful study of buyers' needs and preferences**. It allows a higher price and builds **customer loyalty** (SM 3.29).
- **Risks**: the unique product may not be valued highly enough to justify the higher price, and then **cost leadership will easily defeat differentiation**; competitors may copy the features quickly, so firms need durable sources of uniqueness (Amazon Prime's two-hour delivery) (SM 3.30).

**Bases of differentiation** (SM 3.30): **product** (innovative products that meet customer needs; costly in R&D, production and marketing, but customers flock to be first: Apple iPhone); **pricing** (the lowest price, or superiority through higher prices: iPhone's high prices); **organisation** (maximising the brand or specific advantages such as location, name recognition and customer loyalty: Apple's fanbase).

**Achieving differentiation** (SM 3.31): (1) offer utility and match products with customers' tastes and preferences; (2) elevate or improve the product's performance; (3) offer a high-quality product or service; (4) rapid product innovation; (5) enhance brand image and brand value; (6) fix prices on the unique features and the buying capacity of the customer.

**Disadvantages** (SM 3.32): (1) in the long term, uniqueness is difficult to sustain; (2) too high a price may make customers switch (iPhone users moving to Android flagships); (3) it fails if its basis is not valued by customers (30-minute home delivery of packed snacks).

### Both strategies against the five forces

Each strategy helps the firm remain profitable even with rivalry, new entrants, suppliers' power, substitutes and buyers' power ([[sm.ext.porter|Porter's five forces]]):

::: compare
| Force | Cost leadership (SM 3.28) | Differentiation (SM 3.31) |
|---|---|---|
| Rivalry | Competitors avoid a price war: the low-cost firm keeps earning profits after rivals have competed theirs away | Brand loyalty is a safeguard: customers are less sensitive to price increases while their needs are met |
| Buyers | Powerful buyers cannot exploit the cost leader, and keep buying | Buyers do not negotiate on price: they get special features and have fewer options |
| Suppliers | Can absorb greater price increases from suppliers before raising prices | The premium price lets it absorb higher supply costs |
| Entrants | Continuous focus on efficiency and cost reduction creates entry barriers | Innovative features are expensive; entrants cannot match them at a comparable price |
| Substitutes | Can lower costs to keep customers, invest in developing substitutes, even buy patents | Substitutes cannot replace products with high brand value and customer loyalty |
:::

### Focus strategies

A successful focus strategy depends on an industry **segment of sufficient size, with good growth potential, not crucial to the success of other major competitors**. Midsize and large firms can pursue focus effectively **only in conjunction with differentiation or cost leadership**. "All firms in essence follow a differentiated strategy": only one firm can be the lowest-cost, so the rest must find other ways to differentiate (SM 3.32).

- Most effective **when consumers have distinctive preferences or requirements, and rivals are not specialising in the same segment**. Risks: numerous competitors may imitate a successful focus strategy, or consumer preferences may drift towards what the market as a whole wants (SM 3.32).
- A focuser may concentrate on a particular **group of customers, geographic market or product-line segment**, serving a well-defined but narrow market better than broad-market competitors (Ferrari sports cars) (SM 3.32).
- **Focused cost leadership** competes on **price** in a narrow market. It ==does not necessarily charge the lowest prices in the industry==: it charges low prices relative to other firms competing within the target market (SM 3.32).
- **Focused differentiation** offers **unique features** that fulfil the demands of a narrow market, defined for example by one sales channel (selling over the internet only) or a demographic group: Rolls-Royce's limited number of high-end, custom-built cars (SM 3.33).

**Achieving focus** (SM 3.33): (1) select specific niches not covered by cost leaders and differentiators; (2) create superior skills for catering to such niches; (3) generate high efficiencies in serving them; (4) develop innovative ways of managing the value chain.

**Advantages** (SM 3.33): (1) premium prices can be charged for the focused products or services; (2) because of the firm's tremendous expertise, rivals and new entrants may find it difficult to compete.

**Disadvantages** (SM 3.33): (1) firms lacking distinctive competencies may not be able to pursue it; (2) limited demand makes costs high; (3) in the long run the niche could disappear or be taken over by larger competitors acquiring the same distinctive competencies.

### Best-cost provider strategy

A further development of the three generic strategies, directed towards giving customers **more value for the money** by emphasising both **low cost and upscale differences**. The objective is to keep costs and prices lower than those of other sellers of **"comparable products"**. It can be done by (SM 3.34):

- (a) offering products at a **lower price** than rivals for products with **comparable quality and features**; or
- (b) charging a **similar price** as rivals for products with **much higher quality and better features**.

Android flagship phones from OnePlus, Xiaomi, Oppo and Vivo, giving better quality at the lowest prices to penetrate the market, are the Study Material's example (SM 3.34).

## Worked examples

:::: example Five ways to sell a cup
Identify the generic strategy of each player in India's café industry, and explain how it gains competitive advantage.

(a) **ChaiNation** sells one standard cup of chai at the lowest price in the country through 3,000 identical no-frills kiosks, buying tea and milk centrally in huge volumes.

(b) **BrewBucks** sells signature drinks, designed cafés and a global brand to customers across the country, at prices well above everyone else's.

(c) **Royal Kulhad Events** sets up custom chai bars only for luxury weddings in Udaipur and Jaipur, with hand-painted kulhads and rare teas.

(d) **Kota Cutting** serves students in Kota's coaching district only, at prices below the hostel canteens there, though not the lowest in India.

(e) **Tapri Smart** offers café-quality cold brews and kulhad chai at the same price as rivals' ordinary chai.

::: answer Model answer
According to Porter, strategies allow organisations to gain competitive advantage from three bases: cost leadership, differentiation and focus.

**(a) Cost leadership.** ChaiNation produces a standardised product at a very low per-unit cost for price-sensitive buyers in the broad market. Bulk procurement, standardisation and economies of scale let it charge the lowest price and still earn satisfactory profits.

**(b) Differentiation.** BrewBucks targets the broad market with products and an experience perceived as unique (design, brand image, features), directed at relatively price-insensitive customers, so it can charge a premium and build loyalty.

**(c) Focused differentiation.** It offers unique features that fulfil the demands of a narrow market (luxury weddings in two cities). Its expertise makes it difficult for rivals and new entrants to compete, and it can charge premium prices.

**(d) Focused cost leadership.** It competes on price in a narrow market, charging low prices relative to the other firms competing within that target market, not necessarily the lowest prices in the industry.

**(e) Best-cost provider.** It gives more value for the money by charging a similar price as rivals for much higher quality and better features.
:::
::::

:::: example Sasti Chuski
Tapri plans "Sasti Chuski", one standard tea premix sold through kirana stores across India at a price below every national brand. It will run one large automated plant, buy tea at auction in bulk and spend almost nothing on advertising. Kesar calls the advertising cut "pure savings, no downside".

(i) Identify the generic strategy and state the market conditions in which it is especially effective.

(ii) Discuss the disadvantages of the strategy, with reference to Tapri's plan, and comment on Kesar's view.

::: answer Model answer
**(i) Strategy: cost leadership.** According to Porter, strategies allow organisations to gain competitive advantage from three bases: cost leadership, differentiation and focus. Tapri is pursuing cost leadership: a low-cost competitive strategy aimed at the broad mass market (kirana stores across India), producing a standardised product at a very low per-unit cost for price-sensitive buyers. It requires vigorous cost reduction in procurement, production, storage and distribution and economies in overheads; bulk buying at auction and one large automated plant serve that purpose. Because of its lower costs, Tapri can charge a lower price than most competitors and still earn satisfactory profits.

**Conditions for effectiveness.** Striving to be a low-cost producer is especially effective:

- when the market is composed of many price-sensitive buyers;
- when there are few ways to achieve product differentiation;
- when buyers do not care much about differences from brand to brand; and
- when there are a large number of buyers with significant bargaining power.

**(ii) Disadvantages of cost leadership, applied to the plan:**

1. The cost advantage may not last long, as competitors may imitate cost reduction techniques: national brands can also automate and buy in bulk.
2. Cost leadership can succeed only if the firm achieves higher sales volume: one large plant pays only if Sasti Chuski sells in volume across India.
3. Cost leaders keep costs low by minimising advertising, market research and R&D, but this approach can prove expensive in the long run.
4. Technological advancement is a great threat to cost leaders: a cheaper process adopted by a rival can wipe out the advantage of Tapri's plant.

**Comment on Kesar's view.** The advertising cut is not "pure savings": the Study Material lists minimising advertising, market research and R&D as a disadvantage of cost leadership, because the saving can prove expensive in the long run. Cost leadership can give Tapri a strong competitive advantage only if the market conditions above hold and it guards against these risks.
:::
::::

:::: example Kulhad Gold
Tapri launches "Kulhad Gold": single-estate teas, hand-thrown kulhads, a recipe personalised through the TapriNow app, a free refill for members and a promise of delivery within 20 minutes. It is sold in all 40 cities at twice the price of regular Tapri chai, and buyers do not haggle.

(i) Identify and explain the generic strategy.

(ii) State the major bases of differentiation.

(iii) Outline the measures that can help Tapri achieve this strategy.

::: answer Model answer
**(i) Differentiation.** Tapri is targeting the broad market (all 40 cities) with a product perceived as unique, through its features, design, technology and service. Because of the differentiation it charges a premium, buyers do not negotiate on price, and customers can become strongly attached to the differentiated features.

**(ii) Bases of differentiation:**

- **Product**: innovative products that meet customer needs (single-estate teas, personalisation); costly in R&D, production and marketing, but customers flock to be among the first to have them.
- **Pricing**: superiority established through a higher price.
- **Organisation**: maximising the power of the brand and specific advantages such as location, name recognition and customer loyalty.

**(iii) Measures to achieve differentiation:**

1. Offer utility to customers and match products with their tastes and preferences.
2. Elevate or improve the performance of the product.
3. Offer a high-quality product or service for buyer satisfaction.
4. Rapid product innovation to keep up with the dynamic environment.
5. Take steps to enhance brand image and brand value.
6. Fix prices on the unique features of the product and the buying capacity of the customer.
:::

## Under the hood

### Three statements that look like contradictions

- "Generally, cost leadership must be pursued **in conjunction with differentiation**" (SM 3.27): a cost leader still needs a product buyers accept.
- "**All firms in essence follow a differentiated strategy**" (SM 3.32): only one firm can be the lowest-cost, so every other firm must differentiate somehow.
- Midsize and large firms can pursue focus "**only in conjunction with** differentiation or cost leadership" (SM 3.32): focus always means focused cost leadership or focused differentiation.

### Names you will see

The Study Material's text says "focused cost leadership" and "focused differentiation"; its five-strategy figure says "focused low-cost strategy" and "overall low-cost leadership" (SM 3.34). ICAI's papers also write "focus cost leadership" and "focus differentiation" (September 2024 MCQ 12), "differentiation focus" (the answer to MTP May 2025 Series II Q1(b)) and "cost focus" (an option in MTP May 2024 Series II (A)(v)). They are the same strategies; in your own answers use the Study Material's text names.

### A gloss to read with care

The focus section says market penetration and market development "offer substantial focusing advantages", glossing them in brackets as "new product for existing customers" and "new product for new customers" (SM 3.32). Chapter 4 defines them differently (existing products into existing and new markets): for Ansoff's terms, use [[sm.choice.ansoff|Ansoff's matrix]].

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: the cheap niche player called a cost leader
Kota Cutting is cheaper than the hostel canteens, so Bunty writes "cost leadership". **Fix:** it serves a narrow market and charges low prices relative to firms in that market, not the lowest prices in the industry: **focused cost leadership**.
:::

::: mistake Premium means focus
Charging a premium does not make a strategy "focus". **Fix:** a premium product sold to the broad market (all cities, all customer groups) is **differentiation**; it is focused differentiation only when the target is a narrow segment.
:::

::: mistake Best-cost provider confused with cost leadership
"Low price and good features" written as cost leadership. **Fix:** a cost leader sells standardised products cheaply; the best-cost provider gives more value for money: a lower price for comparable quality, or a similar price for much higher quality.
:::

::: mistake Advantages written as a general list
Asked "how will this strategy help the company remain profitable?", students write "more profit, more customers". **Fix:** the Study Material's answer is the five forces, one by one: rivalry, buyers, suppliers, entrants, substitutes.
:::

## Recap

- Competitive advantage: profitability above the industry average, from unique features the target market sees as superior, which rivals fail to duplicate.
- Porter's generic strategies: cost leadership, differentiation, focus; generic because any type or size of firm, even not-for-profit, can pursue them; each needs different arrangements, controls and incentives.
- Cost leadership: standardised products, very low unit cost, price-sensitive broad market; effective with many price-sensitive buyers and few ways to differentiate; 6 measures, five-force advantages, 4 disadvantages.
- Differentiation: unique industry-wide, premium price, price-insensitive buyers; bases: product, pricing, organisation; 6 measures, five-force advantages, 3 disadvantages.
- Focus: a narrow segment of sufficient size and growth, not crucial to major rivals; focused cost leadership (low prices relative to the niche's rivals) or focused differentiation (unique features for the niche); 4 measures, 2 advantages, 3 disadvantages.
- Best-cost provider: more value for money, a lower price for comparable quality or a similar price for much better quality (Android flagships).
- Answer pattern: Porter's three bases, the strategy named, its definition applied to the case, then the list asked for. (The vada pav leads on cost; the Udaipur wedding caterer focuses on uniqueness.)

## Flashcards

Q:: Competitive advantage: the Study Material's definition?
A:: A set of unique features of a company and its products perceived by the target market as significant and superior to the competition; profitability higher than the average for all companies in its industry.

Q:: Porter's three generic strategies, and why "generic"?
A:: Cost leadership, differentiation and focus; generic because any type or size of business firm, even a not-for-profit organisation, can pursue them.

Q:: Cost leadership: when is it especially effective?
A:: When the market has many price-sensitive buyers, when there are few ways to differentiate the product, when buyers do not care much about brand differences, and when there are many buyers with significant bargaining power.

Q:: Cost leadership: the six measures to achieve it?
A:: Prompt forecasting of demand; optimum utilisation of resources; economies of scale; standardisation for mass production; cost-saving and advanced technologies; resistance to differentiation till it becomes essential.

Q:: Cost leadership: the four disadvantages?
A:: Competitors may imitate cost reduction; it needs higher sales volume; minimising advertising, market research and R&D can prove expensive in the long run; technological advancement is a great threat.

Q:: Differentiation: the three bases?
A:: Product, pricing and organisation.

Q:: Differentiation: the six measures to achieve it?
A:: Offer utility matched to tastes; improve performance; high quality; rapid product innovation; enhance brand image and value; price on unique features and the customer's buying capacity.

Q:: Differentiation: the three disadvantages?
A:: Uniqueness is difficult to sustain in the long term; too high a price may make customers switch; it fails if its basis is not valued by customers.

Q:: Focused cost leadership: does it charge the lowest prices in the industry?
A:: Not necessarily: it charges low prices relative to the other firms competing within its narrow target market.

Q:: Focus strategy: its two advantages and three disadvantages?
A:: Advantages: premium prices; expertise makes it hard for rivals and entrants to compete. Disadvantages: firms without distinctive competencies cannot pursue it; limited demand means high costs; the niche may disappear or be taken over by larger competitors.

Q:: Best-cost provider strategy: the two ways?
A:: (a) A lower price than rivals for products of comparable quality and features; or (b) a similar price to rivals for products of much higher quality and better features.

Q:: Cost leader against powerful suppliers?
A:: It can absorb greater price increases from suppliers before it needs to raise prices for customers.
