## In plain words

### From one stall to five businesses

When Tapri was one roadside stall in Indore, Kesar bought the milk, made the chai, counted the cash and decided everything. Today Tapri has cafés in 40 cities, a premix plant, a samosa factory, a cold-chain fleet and a delivery app. Kesar still wants to approve every new samosa filling.

> **Kesar:** I decided everything for twenty years and we grew. Why change now?
> **Ananya:** Because you grew. One person cannot be the bottleneck for five businesses, Kesar-ji.
> **Dev:** Last month the samosa factory waited eleven days for your signature on a ₹40,000 purchase. I calculated the cost of that delay. You do not want to know.

How a company divides and coordinates its work is its **organisation structure**: who reports to whom and who decides what. Tapri began with a **simple structure** (the owner decides everything), moved to a **functional** one (production, marketing, finance), then to product **divisions**, and now needs **strategic business units**, each with its own head. Chandler saw this pattern repeat: a new strategy creates new problems, performance falls until a new structure is built, so **structure follows strategy**.

Structure is the "hard" side, drawn on a chart. **Culture** is the "soft" side: the values, beliefs and habits that decide how people behave when no one is watching. A culture that fits the strategy is its ally; one that clashes can defeat it. The **McKinsey 7S model** puts hard and soft on one picture.

::: analogy The kitchen and the cooks
Structure is the kitchen's layout: which station chops, which fries, who calls the orders. Culture is what the cooks do when the head chef is not looking: wash hands, taste before serving. Rebuild the kitchen but keep the old habits, and the food does not change.
:::

::: key
Structure should be designed to fit the strategy (Chandler: structure follows strategy), and the culture must fit it too. Changing structure cannot make a bad strategy good, and a strategy-culture conflict can defeat the best plan.
:::

## How it works

### The McKinsey 7S model

The **hard elements are directly controlled by management**; the soft elements are difficult to define as they are governed more by culture, but are equally important for success and growth (SM 5.28). **Limitations** (SM 5.28–5.29): it ignores the external environment, showing only the crucial internal elements; it does not clearly explain organisational effectiveness or performance; it is more static and less flexible for decision making; and it is criticised for missing the real gaps in conceptualisation and execution of strategy.

### Why strategy changes structure: Chandler

Changes in strategy often require changes in structure for two reasons (SM 5.29): **structure dictates how objectives and policies will be established** (in geographic terms under a geographic structure, in product terms under product groups), and **how resources will be allocated** (by customer group, or by function).

According to **Chandler**, changes in strategy lead to changes in organisational structure: structure should be designed or redesigned to facilitate the strategic pursuit of a firm, and therefore ==structure should follow strategy==. He found a particular sequence repeated as organisations grow and change strategy (SM 5.29–5.30):

There is no one optimal design for a given strategy, though firms in an industry tend to organise alike (consumer goods companies: divisional by product). **Small firms tend to be functional (centralised), medium-size firms divisional (decentralised), and large firms SBU or matrix** (SM 5.29–5.30).

**Symptoms of an ineffective structure** (SM 5.30): too many levels of management; too many meetings attended by too many people; too much attention to solving interdepartmental conflicts; too large a span of control; too many unachieved objectives. Changes in structure can facilitate implementation, but **should not be expected to make a bad strategy good, bad managers good, or bad products sell**. Structure can also influence strategy: a strategy requiring massive structural changes would not be an attractive choice.

::: definition Organisational structure
Organizational structure is the company's formal configuration of its intended roles, procedures, governance mechanisms, authority, and decision-making processes. Influenced by factors such as an organisation's age and size, it acts as a framework which reflects managers' determination of what a company does and how tasks are completed, given the chosen strategy. The structure must be congruent with or fit with the company's strategy. (SM 5.31)
:::

### A. Simple structure

Most appropriate for a **single-business strategy** with a line of products in a single geographic market, and for **focused cost leadership or focused differentiation**. The **owner-manager makes all major decisions** and monitors all activities; staff merely execute. Little specialisation, few rules, little formalisation, unsophisticated information systems; communication is frequent and direct, and new products reach the market quickly. Advantages for small firms: openness to innovation, structural flexibility, faster response to change. Growth brings more information than owner-managers can process, so the company outgrows it and should move to the functional structure (SM 5.31–5.32).

### B. Functional structure

Groups tasks and activities by **business function**: production/operations, marketing, finance/accounting, R&D, management information systems. Widely used for its **simplicity and low cost**; it also promotes specialisation of labour, encourages efficiency, minimises the need for an elaborate control system and allows rapid decision making. It is used by larger companies and by companies with **low levels of diversification** (SM 5.32).

It overcomes the growth constraints of the simple structure, but differences in functional specialisation may impede communication and coordination, so the CEO must integrate functional decision-making; specialists may develop a **myopic (narrow) perspective**, losing sight of the vision and mission. That problem is overcome by the multidivisional structure (SM 5.33).

### C. Divisional structure

As a firm grows it finds it hard to manage different products in different markets; a divisional structure becomes necessary to motivate employees, control operations and compete in diverse locations. It is organised in one of **four ways: by geographic area, by product or service, by customer, or by process**. Functional activities are performed both **centrally and in each division** separately (SM 5.33).

::: diagram Divisional structure
{"type": "tree", "gapX": 30, "root": {"label": "Chief Executive", "color": "a", "children": [{"label": "GM, Division A", "color": "b", "children": [{"label": "Marketing\nProduction\nPersonnel"}]}, {"label": "GM, Division B", "color": "b", "children": [{"label": "Marketing\nProduction\nPersonnel"}]}]}, "caption": "Each division has its own functions; corporate staff such as finance and legal stay at headquarters (SM 5.33)."}
:::

**Advantages** (SM 5.34): clear accountability (divisional managers answer for sales and profit); with extensive delegation people see their results, so morale is higher than in a centralised structure; career development for managers; local control of local situations; a competitive internal climate; new businesses and products added easily.

**Limitations** (SM 5.34): it is **costly**: each division pays its own functional specialists; staff services, facilities and personnel are duplicated (specialists are needed centrally too); well-qualified managers need higher salaries; and it needs an elaborate, headquarters-driven control system. Some regions, products or customers may get special treatment, and consistent companywide practices are hard to keep. For most firms the advantages outweigh these.

| Basis | Appropriate when (SM 5.34–5.35) |
|---|---|
| Geographic area | Customers' needs differ by area; similar branches widely dispersed |
| Product or service | Products need special emphasis; few products are offered; products differ substantially |
| Customer | A few major customers of paramount importance (airlines: passengers and cargo) |
| Process | Like functional, but departments are judged on profits or revenues |

### D. Multidivisional (M-form) structure

Operating divisions, **each representing a separate business**, to which the top corporate officer delegates day-to-day operations and business-unit strategy; the corporate office handles overall corporate strategy and manages divisions through **strategic and financial controls**. It was developed in the **1920s**, when functional departments could not handle distinct product lines, product costs and profits were unknown, and top managers were lost in short-run problems (SM 5.35).

It calls for (SM 5.36): separate divisions, each a distinct business with its own functional hierarchy; division managers responsible for day-to-day operations; and a **small corporate office** setting long-term direction and exercising financial control over the semi-autonomous divisions. A less diversified firm manages divisions by **strategic controls** (understanding each unit's strategy); a more diversified one by **financial controls** (budgets, profits), which need each division's performance to be independent of the others. So SBUs come into the picture.

### E. Strategic business unit (SBU) structure

For multi-product, multi-business enterprises it is impractical to plan each business separately; they group them into a manageable number of strategically related units. ==An SBU is a grouping of related businesses, which is amenable to composite planning treatment== (SM 5.36–5.37). Its three most important **characteristics** (SM 5.37):

1. It is a single business or a collection of related businesses which offer scope for independent planning and which might feasibly stand alone from the rest of the organisation.
2. It has its own set of competitors.
3. It has a manager who has responsibility for strategic planning and profit performance, and who has control of profit-influencing factors.

Planning by territory gave the same product varied treatments across territories, and dissimilar products in one territory identical treatment; a firm with six territorial units may be in only three businesses, or more than six (SM 5.37). The SBU structure groups similar products into SBUs, each under a senior executive who **reports directly to the CEO** (SM 5.38).

SBUs are **profit centres**; headquarters concentrates on strategic planning rather than operational control, so divisions react faster. It allows accurate monitoring of each business, simpler control, comparisons between divisions, better resource allocation, and pressure on weak divisions to improve (SM 5.38–5.39). Sony grouped ten internal companies into four SBUs (SM 5.39). The grouping principle: products related from the standpoint of **"function"** fall under one SBU.

**Attributes and benefits of the SBU structure** (SM 5.39–5.40):

1. A scientific method of grouping the businesses of a multi-business corporation, which helps in strategic planning.
2. An improvement over territorial grouping and planning based on territorial units.
3. A grouping of related businesses taken up for strategic planning distinct from the rest; products within an SBU receive the same strategic planning treatment and priorities.
4. Businesses related from the standpoint of "function" are assembled together as a distinct SBU.
5. Unrelated businesses are separated: moved to another SBU by functional relation, or made separate SBUs.
6. It removes vagueness and confusion in grouping and sets correct relative priorities and resources.
7. Each SBU is a separate business for planning: distinct in mission, objectives, competition and strategy.
8. Each SBU has its own distinct set of competitors and its own distinct strategy.
9. Each SBU has a CEO responsible for its strategic planning and profit performance, with control over most factors affecting its profit.

Relatedness between SBUs may lie in technologies or products, markets or customers (Unilever's frozen food, washing powders and margarine all sell through retail), or competences such as marketing skills (SM 5.40). The chapter's own answers add the **benefits**: coordination between divisions with common strategic interests; strategic management and control of large, diverse organisations; accountability at the level of distinct business units; planning at the most relevant level; more objective strategic review by top executives; and allocation of resources to areas with the best opportunities (SM 5.74–5.75). SBU structure becomes **imperative** in an organisation with increase in number, size and diversity (SM 5.69).

### F. Matrix structure

Used when neither functional nor divisional forms, even with horizontal links like SBUs, suit the strategy. **Functional and product forms are combined simultaneously at the same level**: employees have **two superiors**, a product or project manager and a functional manager. The functional "home" department is reasonably permanent; product units or projects are usually temporary (SM 5.41).

::: diagram Matrix structure: every cell has two bosses
{"type": "table", "head": ["Project", "Engineering", "Marketing", "Finance"], "rows": [["Project P1", "unit", "unit", "unit"], ["Project P2", "unit", "unit", "unit"], ["Project P3", "unit", "unit", "unit"]], "rowLabels": true}
:::

Each unit answers to its functional head (column) and its project manager (row). It is **the most complex of all designs**, depending on both vertical and horizontal flows of authority and communication: **higher overhead** (more management positions), dual lines of budget authority (a **violation of the unity of command principle**), dual sources of reward and punishment, shared authority, dual reporting channels, and a need for extensive communication. Yet it is widely used in construction, healthcare, research and defence (SM 5.41).

**Advantages** (SM 5.41): project objectives are clear; there are many channels of communication; workers can see the visible results of their work; shutting down a project is accomplished relatively easily. **To be effective** it needs planning, training, clear mutual understanding of roles and responsibilities, excellent internal communication, and mutual trust and confidence (SM 5.41–5.42). It combines the **stability of the functional structure with the flexibility of the product form**, suits a complex, changeable environment, and works when product, customer, technology, geography and function have roughly equal strategic priorities; but it breeds conflict over duties, authority and resources, and with vague goals a power battle between product and functional managers is likely (SM 5.42).

It is often found when **three conditions** exist (SM 5.42–5.43): ideas need to be cross-fertilised across projects or products; resources are scarce; and abilities to process information and make decisions need to be improved. **Davis and Lawrence's three phases** of matrix development (SM 5.43–5.44):

1. **Cross-functional task forces**: temporary, used when a new product line is introduced; a project manager is the key horizontal link.
2. **Product/brand management**: the task forces become more permanent; the project manager becomes a product or brand manager; function is still the primary structure, with product or brand managers as integrators of semi-permanent products.
3. **Mature matrix**: a true dual-authority structure; both functional and product structures are permanent; all employees connect to a vertical functional superior and a horizontal product manager, who have equal authority.

The matrix is still **not very popular**, because of difficulties in implementation and trouble in managing (SM 5.44).

**Changing organisational design** (SM 5.43):

| Old design | New design |
|---|---|
| One large corporation | Mini-business units and cooperative relationships |
| Vertical communication | Horizontal communication |
| Centralised top-down decision making | Decentralised participative decision making |
| Vertical integration | Outsourcing and virtual organisations |
| Work/quality teams | Autonomous work teams |
| Functional work teams | Cross-functional work teams |
| Minimal training | Extensive training |
| Specialised job design focused on the individual | Value-chain team-focused job design |

### G. Network structure

A radical design, a **"non-structure"**, virtually eliminating in-house business functions: many activities are outsourced. It is often called a **virtual organisation**: project groups or collaborations linked by constantly changing, non-hierarchical, **cobweb-like networks**. It is most useful when the **environment is unstable and expected to remain so**, needing innovation and quick response. People are contracted per project instead of salaried; long-term contracts with suppliers and distributors replace vertical integration; information systems justify "buy" over "make"; functions are scattered across locations. The organisation is in effect a **shell, with a small headquarters acting as a "broker"** (SM 5.44).

It gives flexibility and adaptability to cope with rapid technological change and shifting competition, and lets the company **concentrate on its distinctive competencies** while gathering efficiencies from specialists (Airtel subcontracts manufacturing). **Disadvantages** (SM 5.45): numerous potential partners can be a source of trouble; contracting out may keep the firm from discovering **synergies**; over-specialising in a few functions risks choosing the wrong ones. New forms also demand self-motivated continuous learners and more intense personal interactions, which may create **stress** (SM 5.45–5.46).

### H. Hourglass structure

IT is replacing the tasks of middle management. The hourglass structure has **three layers with a constricted middle**: a short and narrow middle-management level. Information technology links the top and bottom levels, and a shrunken middle layer coordinates diverse lower-level activities. Its middle managers are **generalists**, handling cross-functional issues from marketing, finance or production (SM 5.46).

**Benefits**: reduced costs; enhanced responsiveness by simplifying decision making, with authority shifted close to the source of information so decisions are faster. **Drawbacks**: promotion opportunities for the lower levels diminish significantly; continuity at the same level may bring monotony and lack of interest, making motivation hard to keep high. Organisations respond with **challenging tasks, lateral transfers and proper rewards for performance** (SM 5.46).

### Organisation culture

::: definition Corporate culture
Corporate culture refers to a company's values, beliefs, business principles, traditions, ways of operating, and internal work environment. (SM 5.47)
:::

**Where it comes from** (SM 5.47): culture is manifested in the values and business principles management preaches and practises; ethical standards and official policies; stakeholder relationships (employees, unions, stockholders, vendors, communities); traditions; supervisory practices; employees' attitudes and behaviour; legends people repeat; peer pressures; and organisational politics. It can originate anywhere, from one influential individual to a whole division, at the bottom or the top, and much of it comes from **stories told over and over** to newcomers.

**Ally or obstacle** (SM 5.47–5.48): when the beliefs and practices behind a strategy are compatible with the culture, culture is a valuable ally in implementation; when they conflict, it becomes a **stumbling block**. A strong culture promotes good execution when there is fit and impedes it when there is negligible fit:

| Shared values | Strategy they support (SM 5.48) |
|---|---|
| Frugality and thrift | Low-cost leadership |
| Creativity, embracing change, challenging the status quo | Product innovation and technological leadership |
| Listening to customers, pride in work, decision-making authority for employees | Delivering superior customer value |

A strategy-supportive culture provides **informal rules and peer pressure**, shapes the workforce's mood and motivation, and promotes identification with the vision, targets and strategy (SM 5.48).

**Perils of strategy-culture conflict** (SM 5.49): a culture out of sync with strategic success must be changed as rapidly as can be managed. Occasionally the strategy is revamped to fit the culture; more usually the mismatched cultural features are revamped. The more entrenched the mismatch, the harder implementation; a sizable, prolonged conflict may defeat the strategy. **Creating a strong fit**: the strategy maker must select a strategy compatible with the "sacred" or unchangeable parts of the culture; the strategy implementer must then change whatever facets hinder execution.

**Changing a problem culture** (SM 5.49–5.50) is among the toughest management tasks, because people cling emotionally to the old and familiar. First **diagnose which facets of the present culture are strategy supportive and which are not**; then talk openly to all concerned about what must change; then follow swiftly with **visible, aggressive actions**: revising policies and procedures, altering incentive compensation, visibly praising people who display the new traits, recruiting managers and employees with the desired values as role models, replacing key executives strongly associated with the old culture, and communicating the basis for change and its benefits at every opportunity.

It depends on a **sincere, sustained commitment by the chief executive** and persistence in word and deed; charisma is not essential, but personally talking to many groups is: changes are seldom accomplished from an office. The whole management team, first-line supervisors and employee opinion leaders must be enlisted. In large companies a significant change can take **two to five years**, and reshaping a deeply ingrained culture is usually tougher than instilling one in a new organisation (SM 5.50).

**Hard and soft, balanced** (SM 5.50–5.51): excessive focus on hard management gives at best a linear improvement in performance; the soft side can improve it exponentially; the optimal approach lies between.

## Worked examples

:::: example Nine divisions and one very tired chairman
As it grew, Tapri Tea Co. Ltd was reorganised into nine product divisions, each with its own production, marketing and finance staff, and every division head reports to Kesar: chai premix, iced chai, masala blends, kulhad cafés, highway kiosks, the delivery app, samosas, frozen snacks, and cold-chain logistics (which also carries other companies' goods). Kesar reviews every division every week. The premix, iced chai and masala divisions advertise separately against the same rivals for the same supermarket shelf; the samosa and frozen snack divisions buy potatoes from the same farmers under two different contracts; and the planning team uses one template for all nine divisions, although the logistics division's competitors and customers have nothing in common with those of the chai divisions.

Required:

(i) Identify Tapri's present structure and explain why it no longer suits Tapri.

(ii) Recommend a structure, show how Tapri's nine divisions could be grouped in it, and state the benefits Tapri may derive from it.

::: answer Model answer
**(i) Present structure.** Tapri has a **divisional structure by product**: functional activities are performed both centrally and in each division separately, and each division head is responsible for a product line. A divisional structure is costly where staff services, facilities and personnel are duplicated, as Tapri's two potato contracts show. More importantly, with the increase in the number, size and diversity of its businesses, the structure no longer serves strategic planning:

- related divisions (premix, iced chai and masala blends) are planned separately and compete with one another for the same shelf;
- unrelated businesses (cold-chain logistics and the chai divisions) get identical planning treatment from one template;
- the chairman reviews nine divisions every week, instead of concentrating on strategic planning.

**(ii) Recommended structure: strategic business units (SBUs).** An SBU is a grouping of related businesses which is amenable to composite planning treatment. The SBU structure groups similar products into strategic business units and delegates authority and responsibility for each unit to a senior executive who reports directly to the chief executive officer. It has at least three levels: corporate headquarters, SBU groups, and divisions grouped by relatedness within each SBU. SBU structure becomes imperative in an organisation with increase in number, size and diversity.

**Grouping.** Products and businesses related from the standpoint of "function" are assembled together as a distinct SBU; unrelated businesses are assigned to another SBU by the same criterion, or else made into separate SBUs:

| SBU | Divisions | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Packaged beverages | Chai premix, iced chai, masala blends | Same rivals, same retail shelf |
| Cafés and delivery | Kulhad cafés, highway kiosks, delivery app | Serve the same chai drinkers |
| Snacks | Samosas, frozen snacks | Similar products and raw materials |
| Logistics | Cold-chain logistics | Unrelated: its own competitors and customers |

**Benefits to Tapri:**

1. Coordination between divisions having common strategic interests: the three beverage divisions stop competing for one shelf.
2. Strategic management and control of a large and diverse organisation.
3. Accountability at the level of distinct business units: each SBU is a profit centre.
4. Strategic planning at the most relevant level, with each SBU's own competitors and strategy.
5. A more objective and effective strategic review by top executives: four SBU heads instead of nine division heads.
6. Resources allocated to areas with better opportunities.

Within each SBU, divisions producing similar products can be organised to achieve synergy (one potato contract for both snack divisions), and corporate headquarters can concentrate on strategic planning rather than operational control.
:::

:::: example How the iced-chai team grew two bosses
Within Tapri, the packaged beverages business is organised by function, with R&D, marketing, operations and finance departments. In 2024 it formed a temporary team, drawn from all four departments under a project manager, to launch iced chai. The launch succeeded and the team stayed on: in 2025 its project manager became the brand manager for iced chai, coordinating the departments, while each specialist still reported only to his or her department head. The business now has four brands. Ananya proposes that from 2027 every specialist should report permanently both to a department head and to a brand manager, with equal authority, because the brands need ideas from one another, there are too few specialists to give every brand its own, and decisions about the brands take too long.

Required:

(i) Identify the structure Tapri is moving towards, and the phases of its development Tapri has passed through.

(ii) Is this structure suitable for Tapri? State what Tapri will need for it to be effective, and the problems it should prepare for.

::: answer Model answer
**(i) Structure and phases.** Tapri is moving towards a **matrix structure**, in which functional and product forms are combined simultaneously at the same level of the organisation, and employees have two superiors, a product or project manager and a functional manager. Davis and Lawrence proposed three distinct phases for the development of a matrix structure:

1. **Cross-functional task forces**: temporary cross-functional task forces are initially used when a new product line is being introduced, with a project manager as the key horizontal link. *Tapri*: the 2024 iced-chai launch team.
2. **Product/brand management**: the task forces become more permanent and the project manager becomes a product or brand manager; function is still the primary organisational structure, with brand managers as integrators of semi-permanent brands. *Tapri*: 2025.
3. **Mature matrix**: a true dual-authority structure; both the functional and product structures are permanent, every employee is connected to a vertical functional superior and a horizontal product manager, and the two have equal authority. *Tapri*: Ananya's proposal for 2027.

**(ii) Suitability.** The matrix structure is often found in an organisation or within an SBU when three conditions exist: ideas need to be cross-fertilised across projects or products; resources are scarce; and abilities to process information and to make decisions need to be improved. All three exist at Tapri (brands need each other's ideas, specialists are too few, decisions are slow), so the structure is suitable.

**What Tapri will need:** planning, training, clear mutual understanding of roles and responsibilities, excellent internal communication, and mutual trust and confidence.

**Problems to prepare for:**

1. It is the most complex of all designs, depending on both vertical and horizontal flows of authority and communication.
2. Higher overhead, because it has more management positions.
3. Dual lines of budget authority (a violation of the unity of command principle), dual sources of reward and punishment, shared authority and dual reporting channels.
4. Conflicts over duties, authority and resource allocation; where goals are vague, a continuous battle for power between product and functional managers is likely.

The matrix structure is not very popular because of difficulties in implementation and trouble in managing, so Tapri should introduce the mature matrix only with the conditions above in place.
:::

:::: example BrewBucks buys a tapri
BrewBucks India acquires Chai Chaupal, a 60-outlet chain famous for regulars who chat with owner-managers for hours over unlimited refills. BrewBucks' strategy for the chain is low-cost, high-speed service: a 90-second serve target, standard menus, no free refills. Six months later, service times have not improved, staff ignore the targets, and the most popular managers say "our customers come for the adda, not for speed".

Explain the role of culture in strategy execution and advise BrewBucks how to change the problem culture.

::: answer Model answer
**Role of culture.** An organisation's culture is either an important contributor or an obstacle to successful strategy execution: compatible, it is a valuable ally; in conflict, a stumbling block. A strong culture promotes good execution when there is fit and impedes it when there is negligible fit. Chai Chaupal's culture of leisure and personal service conflicts with a speed-and-cost strategy, and a sizable, prolonged conflict may defeat the strategy.

**Changing the problem culture.** Once the strategy is chosen, the implementer must change whatever facets of the culture hinder execution:

1. Diagnose which facets of the present culture are strategy supportive and which are not.
2. Talk openly and forthrightly to all concerned about the aspects that have to be changed.
3. Follow swiftly with visible, aggressive actions: revise policies and procedures; alter incentive compensation; visibly praise people who display the new traits; recruit managers and employees with the desired values as role models; replace key executives strongly associated with the old culture; communicate the basis for change and its benefits.
4. Secure the chief executive's sincere, sustained commitment and enlist managers, supervisors and opinion leaders.

The change will take time. And since the strategy maker should select a strategy compatible with the "sacred" parts of the culture, BrewBucks should check whether the adda its regulars come for is such a part.

## Under the hood

### Divisional, M-form or SBU? How ICAI keys the MCQs

Three MCQs read for this lesson describe divisions that are "independent product centres" with their own activities, each head responsible for its product line and profitability, and every one keys **strategic business unit** over divisional or multidivisional structure (September 2024 MCQ 14; May 2025 MCQ 16; RTP May 2025 MCQ 2). Simple is outgrown; functional myopia is cured by the multidivisional form; its financial controls need independent divisions, "so, the Strategic Business Units come into picture" (SM 5.36). Leadership's role in culture is in [[sm.impl.leadership|strategic leadership]].

## Recap

- McKinsey 7S has three hard elements (strategy, structure, systems, directly controlled by management) and four soft ones (shared values, style, staff, skills). Limits: ignores the environment, does not explain effectiveness, static, misses execution gaps.
- Chandler: structure follows strategy; new strategy, new problems, performance falls, new structure, performance improves.
- Small firms functional (centralised), medium divisional (decentralised), large SBU or matrix. Structure cannot make a bad strategy good.
- Simple: owner decides. Functional: by function, cheap, myopic. Divisional: geography, product, customer, process; accountable but costly. M-form: separate businesses, small corporate office.
- SBU: related businesses, composite planning, own competitors, a manager responsible for profit; at least three levels.
- Matrix: two bosses, most complex, violates unity of command; conditions: cross-fertilisation, scarce resources, better information processing; phases: task forces, product/brand management, mature matrix.
- Network: outsourced "non-structure", broker headquarters, unstable environments. Hourglass: constricted middle, IT links top and bottom; cheaper and faster, fewer promotions.
- Culture: values, beliefs, principles, traditions, ways of operating, work environment; ally or obstacle; change it by diagnosis, talk, visible action and CEO commitment, over two to five years. (Rebuild the kitchen and the cooks still need new habits.)

## Flashcards

Q:: McKinsey 7S: which are the hard and which the soft elements?
A:: Hard: strategy, structure, systems. Soft: shared values, style, staff, skills.

Q:: McKinsey 7S: its limitations?
A:: It ignores the external environment; does not clearly explain organisational effectiveness or performance; is static and less flexible for decision making; misses the real gaps in conceptualisation and execution of strategy.

Q:: Chandler's view on strategy and structure?
A:: Changes in strategy lead to changes in organisational structure; structure should be designed to facilitate the strategy, so structure should follow strategy.

Q:: Symptoms of an ineffective organisational structure?
A:: Too many levels of management; too many meetings attended by too many people; too much attention to interdepartmental conflicts; too large a span of control; too many unachieved objectives.

Q:: Divisional structure: the four bases?
A:: Geographic area, product or service, customer, process.

Q:: Divisional structure: why is it costly?
A:: Each division needs paid functional specialists; staff, facilities and personnel are duplicated; well-qualified managers need higher salaries; it requires an elaborate headquarters-driven control system.

Q:: SBU: the three most important characteristics?
A:: A single business or collection of related businesses with scope for independent planning; its own set of competitors; a manager responsible for strategic planning and profit performance who controls profit-influencing factors.

Q:: Matrix structure: the three conditions in which it is often found?
A:: Ideas need to be cross-fertilised across projects or products; resources are scarce; abilities to process information and make decisions need to be improved.

Q:: Davis and Lawrence: three phases of matrix development?
A:: Cross-functional task forces; product/brand management; mature matrix.

Q:: Network structure: its disadvantages?
A:: Numerous potential partners can be a source of trouble; contracting out may prevent synergies from combining activities; over-specialising risks choosing the wrong functions and becoming non-competitive.

Q:: Hourglass structure: benefits and drawback?
A:: Reduced costs; faster, simpler decision making with authority close to the source of information. Drawback: fewer promotion opportunities, monotony and low motivation (met by challenging tasks, lateral transfers and rewards).

Q:: What is corporate culture?
A:: A company's values, beliefs, business principles, traditions, ways of operating and internal work environment.
