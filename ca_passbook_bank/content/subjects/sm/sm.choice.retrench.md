## In plain words

### The samosa that stopped being crispy

Tapri has two very different businesses. Its chai premix is mature and steady: the same customers, the same shops, a comfortable share. Its frozen samosa division, Tapri Frozen, has lost money for three years.

> **Kesar:** Shut Tapri Frozen down. No, sell it. No, wait, I love that samosa.
> **Ananya:** Those are three different exits, Kesar-ji: fix it, sell it, or close it. And the order matters.
> **Dev:** Whichever you choose, choose it before the next interest payment.

For the premix, Tapri follows a **stability strategy**: stay in the same business, product and market, and become a little more efficient every year. It is not "do nothing": a mature product must keep pace with the market to keep its share.

For the samosa division, Tapri needs **retrenchment**, which the Study Material calls a **strategic exit**: substantially reducing the scope of activity. There are three, in a natural order. First try to reverse the decline by improving internal efficiency: **turnaround**. If that is not enough, cut off the part that bleeds, a division, product line or function: **divestment**. If nothing works, abandon the activities totally: **liquidation**.

A firm can also mix these with growth, expanding one business while retrenching another: a **combination strategy**.

::: analogy The family car and the second car
Your family car is old but reliable: you service it on time and drive carefully (stability). The second car keeps breaking down: first a proper repair by a new mechanic (turnaround); if it still eats money, sell it so the family budget survives (divestment); and if the family can no longer run any car, sell everything for scrap (liquidation).
:::

::: key
Stability keeps the same business and improves it incrementally; it is not "do nothing". A declining business is first turned around (reverse the decline by improving internal efficiency); divestment of a part usually follows a turnaround that has failed; liquidation is the last resort, abandoning the activities totally.
:::

## How it works

### The four corporate strategies

The corporate strategies a firm can adopt fall into four broad categories (SM 4.4–4.5); their basic features, in ICAI's words:

| Strategy | Basic feature |
|---|---|
| Stability | The firm stays with its current businesses and product markets; maintains the existing level of effort; and is satisfied with incremental growth. |
| Expansion | Here, the firm seeks significant growth: maybe within the current businesses; maybe by entering new businesses that are related to existing businesses; or by entering new businesses that are unrelated to existing businesses. |
| Retrenchment | The firm retrenches some of the activities in some business(es), or drops the business as such through sell-out or liquidation. |
| Combination | The firm combines the above strategic alternatives in some permutation/combination so as to suit the specific requirements of the firm. |

Expansion is in [[sm.choice.growth|growth strategies]]. This lesson covers the other three.

### Stability strategy

A firm opts for stability to safeguard its existing interests and strengths, pursue well established and tested objectives, continue in the chosen business path, maintain operational efficiency on a sustained basis, consolidate the commanding position already reached, and optimise returns on the resources committed (SM 4.5). It is pursued when the firm continues to serve the same or similar markets with the same or similar products and services; it is typical of firms whose products have reached the **maturity stage** of the product life cycle or who have a sufficient market share but need to retain it. They must keep pace with a dynamic market, so ==stability strategy should not be confused with 'do nothing' strategy== (SM 4.5–4.6). Small organisations may also use it to consolidate before launching growth.

**Characteristics** (SM 4.6):

1. The firm stays with the same business, same product-market posture and functions, maintaining the same level of effort.
2. It enhances functional efficiencies in an incremental way, through better deployment and utilisation of resources.
3. It does not involve a redefinition of the business of the corporation.
4. It is a safe strategy that maintains status quo.
5. It does not warrant much fresh investment.
6. The risk involved is less.
7. The firm can concentrate on its resources and existing businesses, products and markets, building core competencies.
8. Firms with a modest growth objective choose it.

**Major reasons** (SM 4.6): (1) a product has reached the maturity stage of the product life cycle; (2) the staff feels comfortable with the status quo, as it involves less change and less risk; (3) the environment is relatively stable; (4) it is not advisable to expand, as expansion may be perceived as threatening; (5) after rapid expansion, a firm might want to stabilise and consolidate itself.

**Start-ups rarely aim for stability** (SM 4.7): a start-up is in the early stages of ideation and development, where speed and agility matter most; stability is meaningful when operations have expanded to full capacity and the business is mature.

### I. Turnaround strategy

Retrenchment may be done internally or externally. For internal retrenchment, emphasis is laid on **improving internal efficiency**, known as turnaround strategy (SM 4.19). The **danger signals** that a turnaround is needed if the company is to survive:

1. Persistent negative cash flow from business(es)
2. Uncompetitive products or services
3. Declining market share
4. Deterioration in physical facilities
5. Over-staffing, high turnover of employees, and low morale
6. Mismanagement

For a turnaround to succeed it must focus on the short and long-term financing needs as well as on strategic issues. A workable **action plan** has five stages (SM 4.19–4.20):

::: steps
1. **Stage One – Assessment of current problems**: assess the current problems and get to the root causes and the extent of damage the problem has caused. Once the problems are identified, focus resources on the areas essential to correcting and repairing any immediate issues.
2. **Stage Two – Analyse the situation and develop a strategic plan**: before any major change, determine the chances of the business's survival; identify appropriate strategies and develop a preliminary action plan, looking for viable core businesses, adequate bridge financing and available organisational resources. Analyse the strengths and weaknesses in the areas of competitive position, and once the major problems and opportunities are identified, develop a strategic plan with specific goals and detailed functional actions.
3. **Stage Three – Implementing an emergency action plan**: if the organisation is in a critical stage, an action plan must stop the bleeding and enable it to survive. It typically includes human resource, financial, marketing and operations actions to restructure debts, improve working capital, reduce costs, improve budgeting practices, prune product lines and accelerate high-potential products. ==A positive operating cash flow must be established as quickly as possible==, and enough funds raised to implement the turnaround.
4. **Stage Four – Restructuring the business**: the financial state of the core business is particularly important; if it is irreparably damaged, the outlook for the whole organisation may be bleak. Prepare cash forecasts, analyse assets and debts, review profits and other key financial functions. The product mix may be changed, requiring repositioning; neglected core products may need immediate attention; some facilities may be closed, or the organisation may withdraw from certain markets to become leaner or target a different niche. Morale building matters: reward and compensation systems that encourage dedication and creativity, and make employees think about profits and return on investment.
5. **Stage Five – Returning to normal**: the organisation should begin to show signs of profitability, return on investment and enhancing economic value added. Emphasis moves to strategic efforts such as carefully adding new products, improving customer service, creating alliances with other organisations and increasing the market share.
:::

The **important elements** of a turnaround (SM 4.21): changes in the top management; initial credibility-building actions; neutralising external pressures; identifying quick payoff activities; quick cost reductions; revenue generation; asset liquidation for generating cash; better internal coordination.

Turnaround is not only for losses: it is relevant whenever a company is in a period of **poor performance**, which may also mean lower than expected growth, no future clarity, or less than target profits (SM 4.22).

### II. Divestment strategy

::: definition Divestment strategy
Divestment strategy involves the sale or liquidation of a portion of business, or a major division, profit centre or SBU. Divestment is usually a part of rehabilitation or restructuring plan and is adopted when a turnaround has been attempted but has proved to be unsuccessful. The option of a turnaround may even be ignored if it is obvious that divestment is the only answer. (SM 4.21)
:::

**Reasons** (SM 4.21):

1. A business that had been acquired proves to be a mismatch and cannot be integrated within the company.
2. Persistent negative cash flows from a particular business create financial problems for the whole company.
3. Severity of competition and the inability of the firm to cope with it.
4. Technological upgradation is required for the business to survive, but the firm cannot do it.
5. A better alternative may be available for investment.

**Characteristics** (SM 4.22): it involves divestment of some of the activities in a business, or sell-out of some of the businesses as such; and it is to be viewed as an integral part of corporate strategy **without any stigma** attached.

The Study Material's "major reasons for retrenchment/turnaround strategy" (SM 4.22) repeat these five and add two at the top: the management no longer wishes to remain in business, partly or wholly, due to continuous losses and unviability; and the management feels the business could be made viable by divesting some activities or liquidating unprofitable ones.

### III. Liquidation strategy

If neither a turnaround nor a divestment works, the organisation may choose to **abandon the activities totally**, resulting in a liquidation strategy (SM 4.18–4.19). The Study Material says no more about it: it is the last of the exits. Retrenchment's basic feature also speaks of dropping a business "through sell-out or liquidation" (SM 4.5).

## Worked examples

:::: example Tapri Frozen: fix it first
Tapri Frozen Foods, a division of Tapri Tea Co. Ltd, has made frozen samosas since 2016. Since 2023 its market share has fallen from 18% to 11%, cash flow from operations has been negative for three years, the freezer plant is in poor repair, and staff turnover is high. The board refuses to close or sell the division ("Tapri without samosas is just tea", says Kesar) and has appointed a new divisional head, Meera Kulkarni, to put it back on its feet.

Which retrenchment strategy should Meera adopt? State the stages in the action plan for it.

::: answer Model answer
**Strategy.** Meera should adopt a **turnaround strategy**. Retrenchment may be internal or external; in internal retrenchment emphasis is laid on improving internal efficiency, which is turnaround. The danger signals that a turnaround is needed are present: persistent negative cash flow, declining market share, deterioration in physical facilities, high employee turnover and low morale. The board wishes to continue the business, and a change in top management, an important element of turnaround, has been made.

**Stages in the action plan.** For success, the plan must focus on the short and long-term financing needs as well as on strategic issues:

1. **Stage One – Assessment of current problems**: assess the problems, get to their root causes (here, why share and cash flow are falling) and the extent of damage.
2. **Stage Two – Analyse the situation and develop a strategic plan**: determine the chances of survival, identify appropriate strategies and develop a plan with specific goals and detailed functional actions.
3. **Stage Three – Implementing an emergency action plan**: stop the bleeding; establish a positive operating cash flow as quickly as possible and raise enough funds.
4. **Stage Four – Restructuring the business**: examine the financial state of the core business; change the product mix and reposition if needed, close facilities that cannot be repaired, and build morale through reward systems.
5. **Stage Five – Returning to normal**: show signs of profitability and return on investment; carefully add new products, improve customer service and increase market share.
:::

:::: example ChillChamp: two years, two exits
In 2022 Tapri acquired ChillChamp, a kulfi brand, which never fitted Tapri's chai business and has drained cash ever since. In 2024 the board replaced ChillChamp's management and pushed internal efficiency hard. By March 2025 the losses had not reduced, the kulfi market had become intensely competitive, and Dev had found a better use for the money: a second premix plant. The board decided to sell ChillChamp.

Identify the retrenchment strategy followed (i) in 2024 and (ii) in 2025. State the reasons for adopting the 2025 strategy.

::: answer Model answer
**(i) 2024: turnaround strategy.** The change in management and the emphasis on improving internal efficiency are features of turnaround, the internal form of retrenchment.

**(ii) 2025: divestment strategy.** Divestment involves the sale or liquidation of a portion of business, or a major division, profit centre or SBU. It is usually adopted when a turnaround has been attempted but has proved to be unsuccessful, which is exactly ChillChamp's case.

**Reasons for divestment, applied:**

1. A business that had been acquired proves to be a mismatch and cannot be integrated within the company: ChillChamp never fitted the chai business.
2. Persistent negative cash flows from a particular business create financial problems for the whole company.
3. Severity of competition and the inability to cope with it: the kulfi market became intensely competitive.
4. A better alternative may be available for investment: the second premix plant.
5. Technological upgradation: if ChillChamp needed upgradation that Tapri was unable or unwilling to fund, that too would make divestment the preferable option (the facts are silent on it).

Divestment is to be viewed as an integral part of corporate strategy, without any stigma attached.
:::

:::: example Tapri's plan for 2026-27
Tapri Tea Co. Ltd's board approves its plan for 2026-27:

(i) **Chai premix, India.** Sales are steady and customers loyal. The range, prices and markets stay as they are; route planning and inventory control are tightened to cut distribution cost.

(ii) **TapriNow**, the delivery app. It has lost money for three years, its share has fallen from 14% to 6%, users call it slow and the tech team is leaving. The board appoints a new head and gives her eighteen months to make it viable.

(iii) **Mumbai Bakes**, a bread unit acquired in 2023. It never fitted the chai business, and a revival attempted in 2025 failed. It will be sold to a bakery chain; the rest of Tapri carries on.

(iv) **Nepal.** Thirty kulhad cafés in Kathmandu and Pokhara and a premix plant in Birgunj, with ₹140 crore of fresh investment.

Required:

(a) What are corporate level strategies?

(b) Identify, with reasons, the corporate strategy in each decision; where it is retrenchment, name the strategic exit.

(c) Which corporate strategy does the plan as a whole represent?

::: answer Model answer
**(a)** Corporate level strategies are formulated by the top management and provide direction to the company as a whole. The corporate strategies a firm can adopt fall into four broad categories: stability, expansion, retrenchment and combination.

**(b)**

- **(i) Stability strategy.** The firm stays with its current business and product markets, maintains the existing level of effort and is satisfied with incremental growth; efficiency is improved incrementally, without much fresh investment and without redefining the business.
- **(ii) Retrenchment: turnaround strategy.** The danger signals are present: persistent negative cash flow, declining market share, uncompetitive services, high employee turnover. The board wants to continue the business and reverse its decline, and has changed the top management of the unit, an important element of turnaround.
- **(iii) Retrenchment: divestment strategy.** Divestment involves the sale or liquidation of a portion of business, and is adopted when a turnaround has been attempted but has proved to be unsuccessful. An acquired business that proves a mismatch and cannot be integrated within the company is a reason for divestment. Only this unit is sold; it is not liquidation, which abandons the activities totally.
- **(iv) Expansion strategy.** The business is redefined by enlarging its scope (new markets) and substantially increasing investment in it.

**(c) Combination strategy.** The firm combines stability, retrenchment and expansion in some permutation or combination to suit its specific requirements.

## Under the hood

### Internal or external retrenchment

In the chapter's own scenario on a co-working business made unviable by the pandemic, ICAI's answer contrasts the two: turnaround is adopted in internal retrenchment, where emphasis is on improving internal efficiency, while divestment is adopted "when a business turns unprofitable and unviable due to some external factors" (SM 4.44). Use the distinction when a case blames an outside shock for one business while the rest of the company is healthy.

### Closing one unit is not liquidation strategy

Divestment is "the sale **or liquidation** of a portion of business" (SM 4.21), so a company that winds up one loss-making division and carries on is divesting. Liquidation strategy is the last exit, when the organisation abandons the activities totally (SM 4.19): RTP September 2026 MCQ 5 keys it for promoters who, after evaluating all revival options, permanently close the company and sell its assets.

### "Justify: stability strategy is opposite of expansion strategy"

ICAI's suggested answer (RTP May 2024 Q14) runs on the first four points; the last row adds the Study Material's own contrast (SM 4.7):

::: compare
| Point | Stability strategy | Expansion strategy |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Safeguards existing interests and strengths | Redefines the business by substantially adding to its scope |
| Business | Same or similar markets and products; few functional changes | New products, markets, technology; diversifying, acquiring, merging |
| Character | Not a "do nothing" strategy, but a safe one | An aggressive strategy: dynamism, vigour, promise and success |

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: the stability strategy that bought more farms
Asked which initiative supports stability, Bunty picks "buy more farms to raise output". **Fix:** stability keeps the same business and improves **functional efficiency** incrementally. Buying farms, new segments and new channels are growth moves (January 2025 MCQ 14).
:::

::: mistake "Restructure the organisation chart" as Stage Four
**Fix:** Stage Four is *restructuring the business*: the core business, cash forecasts, assets and debts, product mix, facilities, markets and morale. In May 2026 MCQ 12, "restructuring organizational hierarchy" (with "providing innovative transport service") was keyed as an action that did *not* pertain to the turnaround plan.
:::

::: mistake Divestment and liquidation used as synonyms
**Fix:** divestment sells or liquidates a **portion** (a division, profit centre or SBU) and the company carries on; liquidation **abandons the activities totally**.
:::

::: mistake Jumping straight to divestment
A declining business with a board that wants to continue is given "divest". **Fix:** if the board wants to continue and improve internal efficiency, the answer is **turnaround**; divestment usually follows a turnaround that has proved unsuccessful, unless it is obvious that divestment is the only answer.
:::

## Recap

- Four corporate strategies: stability, expansion, retrenchment, combination (SM 4.4).
- Stability: same business, product-market posture and level of effort; incremental efficiency; no redefinition; low risk; typical of mature products; not "do nothing".
- Stability reasons: maturity; staff comfort; stable environment; expansion seen as threatening; consolidation after rapid expansion.
- Strategic exits: turnaround (reverse the decline), divestment (cut off a unit), liquidation (abandon totally).
- Turnaround signals: negative cash flow, uncompetitive products, falling share, poor facilities, over-staffing and low morale, mismanagement.
- Turnaround stages: assessment; analysis and strategic plan; emergency action plan; restructuring; return to normal.
- Divestment: sale or liquidation of a portion, usually after a failed turnaround; reasons: mismatch, negative cash flows, competition, technology, better investment.
- Combination: mixing the alternatives, such as expanding one business while closing another. (The samosa gets one honest repair first.)
