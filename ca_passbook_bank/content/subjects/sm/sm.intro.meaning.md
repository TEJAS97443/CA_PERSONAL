## In plain words

### One stall, no strategy department

In 2009 Tapri Tea Co. Ltd was one roadside tea stall near the Indore bus stand. Today it is a listed company with kulhad cafés in 40 cities, and BrewBucks, the global coffee chain, has just opened in twelve of them. Ananya Rao, Tapri's head of strategy, wants a full strategy review before the board meeting.

> **Kesar:** Ananya, when I had one stall I had no strategy. Good chai, fair price, people came.
> **Ananya:** You had a strategy, Kesar-ji. You chose the corner near the bus stand, you chose kulhads over plastic cups, and when the college moved across the river, you moved the stall after it.
> **Kesar:** That was common sense.
> **Ananya:** That was strategy. It just lived in your head. Forty cities need it written down.

A **strategy** is a business's game plan: how it takes a market position, attracts and satisfies customers, competes and reaches its objectives while the world outside keeps changing, often against it. **Strategic management** is the process that keeps this plan alive: decide where the business wants to go, set objectives, craft the strategy, carry it out, check how it is working, correct course, and start again. It never ends.

Look again at Kesar's three choices. The bus-stand corner and the kulhads were planned in advance: a **proactive** strategy. Following the college across the river was a response to something he did not plan: a **reactive** (adaptive) strategy. Every real company's strategy is a blend of the two.

Why bother? Because strategic management helps a firm do better than its rivals and its own past, and steer through change. But it is no magic shield: the environment can surprise anyone, planning eats time and money, and rivals keep their plans secret.

::: analogy The captain's game plan
Before a match, a cricket captain plans the bowling and the field for every batter: that is the proactive part. When the pitch starts turning or the other side sends in a pinch-hitter, he changes the bowler: the reactive part. The plan wins no match by itself (rain, a dropped catch and the rival captain's surprise are outside his control), but a team that walks out with no plan is, in Michael Porter's words, "willing to try anything".
:::

::: key
Strategy is the game plan for competing; strategic management is the never-ending process of setting direction, crafting, implementing, evaluating and correcting that plan. A company's actual strategy is always partly proactive (planned) and partly reactive (adaptive), and strategic management improves the odds of success without guaranteeing it.
:::

## How it works

### First, what "management" means

The Study Material uses the term **management** in two senses (SM 1.3):

1. **A key group** in charge of an organisation's affairs: the chief organ entrusted with making it a purposeful and productive entity, by bringing together and integrating the disorganised resources of manpower, money, material and technology into a functioning whole. The survival and success of an organisation depends to a large extent on the competence and character of its management.
2. **A set of interrelated functions and processes** carried out by that group to attain its objectives: planning, organising, directing, staffing and control, ranging from determining goals and designing the organisation to installing a control system that ensures what is planned is achieved.

Management is an **influence process** to make things happen, backed by power, competence, knowledge and resources. Managers formulate goals, values and strategies to cope with, adapt and adjust to changes in the environment (SM 1.3).

### Strategy: the game plan

In business, strategy relates to the ways the business decides to **respond to dynamic and often hostile external forces** while pursuing its vision, mission and ultimate objectives. It is meant to **unravel complexity and reduce uncertainty** caused by changes in the environment, and it relates the goals of the organisation to the means of achieving them (SM 1.4).

::: definition Strategy
"A long-range blueprint of an organization's desired image, direction and destination, i.e., what it wants to be, what it wants to do, how it wants to do things, and where it wants to go." Strategy is ==the game plan that the management of a business uses to take market position, conduct its operations, attract and satisfy customers, compete successfully, and achieve organizational objectives==. (SM 1.4)
:::

Two classic definitions the Study Material quotes (SM 1.4):

| Author | Strategy is |
|---|---|
| Igor H. Ansoff | The common thread among the organisation's activities and product-markets that defines the essential nature of business that the organisation has or planned to be in future |
| William F. Glueck | A unified, comprehensive and integrated plan designed to assure that the basic objectives of the enterprise are achieved |

### Six facts about the nature of strategy

From SM 1.5, in the Study Material's order:

1. Strategy is a **consciously considered and flexibly designed scheme** of corporate intent and action to mobilise resources, direct human effort and behaviour, handle events and problems, perceive and utilise opportunities, and meet challenges and threats for corporate survival and success.
2. It fills the organisation's need for a **sense of dynamic direction, focus and cohesiveness**. Objectives and goals give direction, but they do not fill that need alone.
3. It provides an **integrated framework** for top management to search for, evaluate and exploit beneficial opportunities, to perceive and meet potential threats and crises, to make full use of resources and strengths, and to offset weaknesses.
4. Strategy is **no substitute for sound, alert and responsible management**.
5. Strategy **can never be perfect, flawless and optimal**. It is flexible and pragmatic, to take care of sudden emergencies and pressures; in a sound strategy, allowances are made for possible miscalculations and unanticipated events.
6. In large organisations strategies are formulated at the **corporate, divisional and functional levels**: corporate strategies (expansion and growth, integration, diversification, takeovers and mergers, new investment and divestment areas, R&D projects) are operationalised by divisional and functional strategies (product lines, production volumes, quality, prices, promotion, purchasing, personnel development). The levels have their own lesson: [[sm.intro.levels|strategic levels in organisations]].

The Study Material's own test, "Is this a strategy?": a ketchup brand cuts sugar and preservatives so that parents feel safe about their children eating it. Yes: it is a business strategy to fight competition and to adapt to a changing external environment, people becoming health conscious (SM 1.7).

### Partly proactive, partly reactive

A company's strategy is typically a blend of (SM 1.5–1.6):

- **Proactive actions** on the part of managers to improve the company's market position and financial performance. A proactive strategy is a **planned** strategy.
- **Reactions** to unanticipated developments and fresh market conditions in the dynamic business environment. A reactive strategy is an **adaptive reaction** to changing circumstances.

The current strategy flows both from previously initiated actions and approaches that are working well enough to merit continuation, and from newly initiated decisions that strengthen the company's position. That part is **deliberate and proactive**: the product of management's analysis and strategic thinking. But things happen that cannot be fully anticipated: when market and competitive conditions take an unexpected turn, or some part of the strategy hits a stone wall, a strategic reaction or adjustment is required. So ==crafting a strategy involves stitching together a proactive/intended strategy== based on prior successful experience and then adapting pieces of successful reactions as circumstances change or better options emerge: a reactive/adaptive strategy (SM 1.6–1.7).

| Basis | Proactive strategy | Reactive strategy |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Planned actions by managers to improve market position and performance | Adaptive reactions to unanticipated developments and fresh market conditions |
| Nature | Deliberate, intended | Responsive, adaptive |
| Trigger | Management's own analysis and strategic thinking | A change in the environment the plan did not foresee |
| Purpose | Gain advantage through advance planning | Cope with change or seize an opportunity that has just appeared |

The Study Material's example of a well-planned strategy is **UPI**: a clear identification of an existing problem, a strategy built on it, and near-perfect execution changed India's digital payments landscape (SM 1.7).

### Strategic management: the process

::: definition Strategic management
"The managerial process of developing a strategic vision, setting objectives, crafting a strategy, implementing and evaluating the strategy, and finally initiating corrective adjustments where deemed appropriate." The process does not end; it keeps going on in a cyclic manner. (SM 1.8)
:::

Put another way, strategic management involves developing the company's vision, **environmental scanning** (both external and internal), strategy formulation, strategy implementation, and evaluation and control. Its activities are developing the firm's vision and mission, strategic analysis, developing objectives, creating, choosing and implementing strategies, and measuring and evaluating performance (SM 1.4, 1.8). The discipline was **originally called business policy**; it emphasises monitoring and evaluating external opportunities and threats in the light of the company's strengths and weaknesses, and designing strategies for survival and growth (SM 1.8). The five stages are worked through in [[sm.impl.matrix|formulation versus implementation]].

### Importance: why bother

The importance of strategic management lies in enabling an organisation to **perform better than its competitors and its own past and present performance**: superior returns to investors, superior value to customers, and superior performance against the expectations of employees, suppliers, government and society (SM 1.7–1.8).

::: definition The overall objectives of strategic management (two-fold)
1. To **create competitive advantage** (something unique and valued by the customer), so that the company can outperform the competitors in all aspects of organisational performance.
2. To **guide the company successfully through all changes in the environment**, that is, to react in the right manner. (SM 1.8)
:::

"Survival of the fittest" is the principle of survival for all organisations, and the fittest are not the largest or strongest but **those who can change and adapt** to changes in the business environment. Bajaj Scooters, LML Scooters, Murphy Radio, BPL Television, Videocon, Nokia and Kodak are the Study Material's examples of giants that failed to manage drastic change. Business follows the war principle of "win or lose" (only a few cases are win-win), so each organisation must build competitive advantage over its competitors in this **business warfare**, and that can be done only through the process of strategic management: strategic analysis, formulation, implementation, evaluation and control (SM 1.8–1.9).

### The seven benefits, in ICAI's order

From SM 1.9–1.10:

::: steps
1. **Gives direction** to the company to move ahead: it helps define the goals and mission, and realistic objectives in line with the vision.
2. **Proactive instead of reactive** in shaping its future: organisations analyse and act instead of being mere spectators, control their own destiny better, and work within the vagaries of the environment and shape it instead of being carried away by its turbulence.
3. **Framework for all major decisions**: businesses, products, markets, manufacturing facilities, investments and organisational structure; better guidance to the entire organisation on what it is trying to achieve.
4. **Prepares the organisation to face the future** and acts as a pathfinder to business opportunities: it identifies the available opportunities and the ways and means to reach them.
5. **Corporate defence mechanism** against mistakes and pitfalls: it helps avoid costly mistakes in product-market choices or investments.
6. **Enhances the longevity** of the business: it helps the organisation take a clear stand in its industry and ensures it is not just surviving on luck. Actions over expectations.
7. **Develops core competencies and competitive advantages** that assist in the fight for survival and growth.
:::

**Not every worthy decision is strategic.** In search of meaning and purpose, organisations take decisions that may not measure up to being "strategic": belief in diversity, inclusion and equity; improving the availability, affordability and accessibility of products and services for opportunity-deprived sections; greater workplace democracy. They are worthy on their own count but may not serve a strategic purpose in the strict sense of the term (SM 1.11).

### The four limitations, in ICAI's order

"The presence of strategic management cannot counter all hindrances and always achieve success" (SM 1.11):

1. **The environment is highly complex and turbulent.** It is difficult to understand it and pinpoint how it will shape up; the organisation's estimate of the future may go badly wrong and jeopardise all its plans. The Study Material's example: electric two-wheeler brands counted on the government's push for e-mobility, but buyers turned reluctant after frequent battery fires. Strategy cannot overcome a turbulent environment (SM 1.11).
2. **It is a time-consuming process.** Preparing and communicating strategies takes so much time that it may impede daily operations and hurt routine business: like a student who spends so long planning what, where and when to study that there is no energy left to study (SM 1.11–1.12).
3. **It is a costly process.** Expert strategic planners must be engaged and the external and internal environments analysed; this is really costly for organisations with limited resources, particularly small and medium ones (SM 1.12).
4. **It is difficult to estimate the competitive responses** to a firm's strategies when every organisation is moving strategically and decisions are taken behind closed doors. The Study Material's example: when Apple removed the 3.5 mm audio jack from iPhones, speaker brands had to rush into true wireless products to stay relevant (SM 1.12).

**Why do it anyway?** Because its importance outweighs its shortcomings: a business cannot operate and succeed without proper strategic management (SM 1.13).

## Worked examples

:::: example The summer Tapri's plan met BrewBucks
In March 2025 Tapri's board approved its plan for 2025-26:

1. keep the ₹15 "cutting chai" price promise that has run in every café since 2019;
2. open 60 highway kiosks on the Indore–Mumbai and Delhi–Jaipur routes;
3. launch a sugar-free chai premix for the growing number of diabetes-conscious buyers.

In April BrewBucks cut its iced coffee to ₹59 in every Tapri city. Within three weeks Tapri launched a ₹49 "chai frappe" in 300 cafés and an app coupon for college students. In May a record heat wave halved footfall at the 22 kiosks already open; the board stopped the remaining 38 kiosks and moved that money into air-conditioning for its busiest cafés.

At the next meeting Kesar says: "Half the plan changed in three months. Our strategy failed."

**Required:**

(i) Identify the proactive and the reactive elements in Tapri's 2025-26 strategy, and the element that was abandoned.

(ii) Explain the statement "strategy is partly proactive and partly reactive" with reference to the case.

(iii) Comment on Kesar's view that the strategy failed.

::: answer Model answer
**(i) Elements of the strategy**

| Element | Nature | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| ₹15 cutting-chai promise | Proactive | Ongoing element continued from prior periods because it works |
| 60 highway kiosks; sugar-free premix | Proactive | New initiatives planned in advance from management's analysis |
| ₹49 chai frappe and student coupon | Reactive | Adaptive reaction to BrewBucks' price cut |
| Air-conditioning for busy cafés | Reactive | Adaptive reaction to the heat wave |
| Remaining 38 kiosks | Abandoned | Part of the plan that hit a stone wall |

**(ii) Partly proactive, partly reactive.** A company's strategy is typically a blend of proactive actions on the part of managers to improve its market position and financial performance, and reactions to unanticipated developments and fresh market conditions. A proactive strategy is a planned strategy; a reactive strategy is an adaptive reaction to changing circumstances. Tapri's plan of March 2025 (the continued price promise, the kiosks and the premix) is its deliberate, proactive strategy. BrewBucks' price cut and the heat wave could not be fully anticipated, so Tapri adjusted with the frappe, the coupon and the air-conditioning. Its actual strategy for 2025-26 is therefore the proactive elements, plus the reactive elements, minus the abandoned kiosks.

**(iii) Kesar's view.** It is in the very nature of strategy that it is flexible and pragmatic, to take care of sudden emergencies and pressures; strategy can never be perfect, flawless and optimal, and in a sound strategy allowances are made for possible miscalculations and unanticipated events. A change of plan in response to a competitor's move and a heat wave is not a failure of strategy: crafting a strategy involves stitching together an intended strategy and then adapting pieces of successful reactions as circumstances change. The strategy would have failed had Tapri kept building kiosks nobody visited, or ignored BrewBucks. Tapri's strategy is working as a strategy should.
:::

:::: example Pinky's sweet shops and the expensive plan
Kesar's cousin Pinky Mehra owns Mehra Mithai Bhandar, six sweet shops in Bhopal. Last year she paid a consultant ₹6 lakh for a three-year strategic plan. Its workshops took her and her shop managers away every Saturday for four months, including the Diwali order season, when customer complaints doubled. The plan bet on premium dry-fruit gift boxes for corporate clients. Then cashew prices rose sharply after a poor harvest abroad, squeezing the boxes' margin, and a national sweets brand launched an almost identical box at a lower price within three weeks of hers.

Pinky now says: "Strategy is a waste of money. From now on I run the shops on instinct, like my father did."

**Required:**

(i) Identify the limitations of strategic management illustrated in the case.

(ii) Advise Pinky whether she should give up strategic management.

::: answer Model answer
**(i) Limitations illustrated.** The presence of strategic management cannot counter all hindrances and always achieve success:

1. **The environment is highly complex and turbulent.** It is difficult to pinpoint how it will shape up, and the organisation's estimate may go wrong. The plan did not foresee the jump in cashew prices after a poor harvest abroad.
2. **It is a time-consuming process.** Preparing and communicating strategies may impede daily operations. Four months of Saturday workshops, in the Diwali season, doubled customer complaints.
3. **It is a costly process.** Expert strategic planners need to be engaged, which is costly for an organisation with limited resources: ₹6 lakh is a large sum for six shops.
4. **It is difficult to estimate competitive responses.** The national brand's plans were made behind closed doors; its copy at a lower price within three weeks was not anticipated.

**(ii) Advice.** Pinky should not give up strategic management. Its importance outweighs its limitations: a business cannot operate and succeed without proper strategic management. It would give her shops a direction and realistic objectives; help her be proactive rather than a mere spectator to cashew prices and rivals; provide a framework for decisions on products, markets and investments; act as a pathfinder to opportunities such as corporate gifting; serve as a defence mechanism against costly mistakes; enhance the longevity of the business so that it does not survive on luck; and build core competencies, such as her family recipes, that a national brand cannot copy. She can reduce the limitations instead of abandoning the process: plan outside the festival season, keep reviews short, and make the reactive part of the strategy explicit (for example, alternative suppliers and a second product line if cashew prices rise again). Running on instinct alone would leave her exposed to every change in a turbulent environment.
:::

## Under the hood

### Two definitions, one idea

The chapter defines strategic management twice: as the **managerial process** of developing a strategic vision, setting objectives, crafting, implementing and evaluating the strategy and making corrective adjustments (SM 1.8), and as a **dynamic process of formulation, implementation, evaluation and control** of strategies to realise the organisation's strategic intent (SM 1.13). The first is the one ICAI's suggested answers quote for "Define strategic management"; the second leads into [[sm.intro.intent|strategic intent: vision, mission, goals and values]].

### Everyone has a strategy

"All firms have a strategy, even if it is informal, unstructured, and sporadic"; some simply do not know where they are going. The process is now used by small firms, large companies, non-profit institutions, governmental organisations and multinational conglomerates alike (SM 1.28). Kesar's stall-in-his-head was an informal strategy; Tapri's forty cities need a formal one.

### Strategy versus good management

Point 4 of the nature of strategy deserves a second look: strategy is **no substitute for sound, alert and responsible management**. A brilliant plan run by a careless team still fails, which is why the implementation half of strategic management gets a whole chapter later ([[sm.impl.matrix|formulation versus implementation]]).

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: "reactive" written as a polite word for "failed"
Students treat a reactive move as proof that the plan was bad. **Fix:** a reactive strategy is an *adaptive reaction to changing circumstances*, and every company needs one, because strategy can never be perfect, flawless and optimal. The failure would be not reacting.
:::

::: mistake Calling a quick copy of a rival "proactive" because it was fast
Speed does not decide the label; the trigger does. **Fix:** a move planned from management's own analysis is proactive; a move forced by an unanticipated development (a rival's price cut, a heat wave, a new rule) is reactive, however fast it is.
:::

::: mistake Mixing up strategy and strategic management
"Strategic management is the game plan to compete" earns nothing. **Fix:** *strategy* is the game plan (the long-range blueprint); *strategic management* is the cyclic process of developing a vision, setting objectives, crafting, implementing, evaluating and correcting it.
:::

::: mistake Inventing limitations
Answers list "lack of employee commitment" or "rigid plans". **Fix:** ICAI's four limitations, in its order: turbulent environment, time, cost, competitors' responses. Rigidity is the opposite of the Study Material's point: strategy is meant to be flexible and pragmatic.
:::

## Recap

- Strategy: the game plan management uses to take market position, conduct operations, attract and satisfy customers, compete and achieve objectives; a long-range blueprint of desired image, direction and destination.
- Strategic management: the managerial process of developing a strategic vision, setting objectives, crafting, implementing and evaluating the strategy, and making corrective adjustments; it never ends.
- A company's strategy is partly proactive (planned) and partly reactive (adaptive reactions to changing circumstances); it can never be perfect, flawless and optimal.
- Two-fold objective: create competitive advantage; guide the company through all changes in the environment.
- Seven benefits: direction, proactive, framework for decisions, prepares for the future, defence mechanism, longevity, core competencies.
- Four limitations: turbulent environment, time-consuming, costly, competitive responses hard to estimate; its importance still outweighs them.
- Not every worthy decision (diversity, workplace democracy) is strategic in the strict sense.
- Ansoff: the common thread of activities and product-markets; Glueck: a unified, comprehensive and integrated plan. (The captain still changes the bowler.)

## Flashcards

Q:: Define strategic management (SM 1.8).
A:: The managerial process of developing a strategic vision, setting objectives, crafting a strategy, implementing and evaluating the strategy, and initiating corrective adjustments where deemed appropriate; it keeps going in a cyclic manner.

Q:: Define strategy as the Study Material does.
A:: A long-range blueprint of an organisation's desired image, direction and destination: what it wants to be, what it wants to do, how it wants to do things and where it wants to go; the game plan to take market position, conduct operations, attract and satisfy customers, compete and achieve objectives.

Q:: Glueck's definition of strategy?
A:: A unified, comprehensive and integrated plan designed to assure that the basic objectives of the enterprise are achieved.

Q:: Ansoff's definition of strategy?
A:: The common thread among the organisation's activities and product-markets that defines the essential nature of business it is in or plans to be in.

Q:: What is a proactive strategy, and what is a reactive strategy?
A:: Proactive: planned actions by managers to improve market position and financial performance. Reactive: an adaptive reaction to unanticipated developments and fresh market conditions.

Q:: What are the two overall objectives of strategic management?
A:: To create competitive advantage so that the company can outperform competitors, and to guide the company successfully through all changes in the environment.

Q:: The seven benefits of strategic management, in order?
A:: Direction; proactive instead of reactive; framework for major decisions; prepares for the future (pathfinder); corporate defence mechanism; enhances longevity; develops core competencies and competitive advantages.

Q:: The four limitations of strategic management, in order?
A:: The environment is complex and turbulent; it is time-consuming; it is costly; it is difficult to estimate competitive responses.

Q:: Why do businesses use strategic management despite its limitations?
A:: Because its importance outweighs its shortcomings; a business cannot operate and succeed without proper strategic management.

Q:: What was strategic management originally called?
A:: Business policy.

Q:: Can a strategy be perfect, flawless and optimal?
A:: No. Strategy is flexible and pragmatic; a sound strategy makes allowances for possible miscalculations and unanticipated events.
