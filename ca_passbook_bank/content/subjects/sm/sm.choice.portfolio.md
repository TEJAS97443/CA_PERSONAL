## In plain words

### Kesar loves all his children equally

Tapri now runs four businesses: the chai premix, the kulhad cafés, a young delivery app called TapriNow, and a cold-chain truck fleet. There is ₹120 crore to invest this year.

> **Kesar:** Split it equally. I love all my businesses the same.
> **Dev:** The premix makes the most profit. Give it the most.
> **Ananya:** Neither, Dev. The premix makes the cash; the question is who should spend it.

A **portfolio** is the set of businesses a company owns, and portfolio models help the head office decide which business should receive more, less or no investment. The best known is the **BCG growth-share matrix**. It asks two questions about each business: is its market growing fast (**market growth rate**), and is it big compared with its rivals (**relative market share**)? The answers give four types:

- **Stars**: high share in a fast-growing market. The cafés: they need heavy investment, and they are the best opportunity for expansion.
- **Cash cows**: high share in a slow-growing market. The premix: it generates cash and needs little investment to keep its share.
- **Question marks**: low share in a fast-growing market. TapriNow: it eats cash; it may become a star, or a cash trap.
- **Dogs**: low share in a slow market. The truck fleet: not much future.

So the most profitable business is not always the one that gets the money: the cash cow's cash is often what pays for the stars and question marks.

The **GE matrix** does the same job with richer measures. Instead of growth alone it rates **market attractiveness** (size, growth, profitability, competition and more), and instead of share alone it rates **business strength** (share, margins, brand, cost position and more). The nine cells are coloured like a traffic light: green, go and invest; amber, careful; red, stop, harvest or divest.

::: analogy The cricket selector
A selector has an established opener who scores steadily in a format nobody new is watching (cash cow), a young batsman scoring heavily in the fast-growing T20 format (star), a newcomer with talent but few runs yet in that same format (question mark), and an out-of-form veteran in a fading format (dog). The selector builds the star, keeps giving the newcomer chances (or not), takes the runs from the veteran opener while they last, and lets the out-of-form veteran go.
:::

::: key
Portfolio matrices allocate money across businesses. BCG uses market growth rate and relative market share: stars (build), question marks (hold), cash cows (harvest), dogs (divest), in the pairing ICAI's suggested answers use. GE uses the broader market attractiveness and business strength, in a nine-cell green-amber-red grid.
:::

## How it works

### Why portfolio models

Portfolio models help strategists take decisions about individual products or businesses in a firm's portfolio, primarily for competitive analysis and corporate strategic planning in multi-product and multi-business firms (they may also be used in less-diversified firms with a main business and minor complementary interests). The main advantage of the portfolio approach is that ==resources could be channelised at the corporate level to those businesses that possess the greatest potential==: for instance, a diversified company may divert resources from its cash-rich businesses to more prospective ones that hold promise of faster growth. Management analyses its current portfolio and decides which business should receive more, less or no investment (SM 4.23). Ansoff's and the ADL matrix are in [[sm.choice.ansoff|Ansoff's matrix and the ADL matrix]].

### The BCG growth-share matrix

The BCG growth-share matrix is the simplest way to portray a corporation's portfolio of investments. Known for its cow and dog metaphors, it is popularly used for **resource allocation in a diversified company** (SM 4.27). The Boston Consulting Group developed it in the early 1970s for companies large enough to be organised into strategic business units (SM 4.45). A company classifies its businesses on two dimensions:

- The **vertical axis** represents **market growth rate** and provides a measure of market attractiveness.
- The **horizontal axis** represents **relative market share** and serves as a measure of company strength in the market.

::: diagram BCG growth-share matrix
{"type": "table", "head": ["Market growth rate", "High relative market share", "Low relative market share"], "rows": [["High", "Stars", "Question marks"], ["Low", "Cash cows", "Dogs"]], "rowLabels": true}
:::

High relative market share is on the **left**, as in the Study Material's figure (SM 4.28). The four types, in ICAI's words (SM 4.28–4.29):

- **Stars** are products or SBUs that are growing rapidly. They also need heavy investment to maintain their position and finance their rapid growth potential. They represent best opportunities for expansion.
- **Cash cows** are low-growth, high market share businesses or products. They generate cash and have low costs. They are established, successful, and need less investment to maintain their market share. In the long run, when the growth rate slows down, stars become cash cows.
- **Question marks**, sometimes called problem children or wildcats, are low market share businesses in high-growth markets. They require a lot of cash to hold their share; they need heavy investments with low potential to generate cash. Question marks if left unattended are capable of becoming **cash traps**. Since the growth rate is high, increasing share should be relatively easier; it is for business organisations to turn them into stars, and then into cash cows when the growth rate reduces.
- **Dogs** are low-growth, low-share businesses and products. They may generate enough cash to maintain themselves, but do not have much future; sometimes they may need cash to survive. Dogs should be minimised by means of divestment or liquidation.

### Post-identification strategies

After classifying its products or SBUs, a firm must determine what role each will play in the future. The four strategies (SM 4.29):

1. **Build**: the objective is to increase market share, even by forgoing short-term earnings in favour of building a strong future with large market share.
2. **Hold**: the objective is to preserve market share.
3. **Harvest**: the objective is to increase short-term cash flow regardless of long-term effect.
4. **Divest**: the objective is to sell or liquidate the business because resources can be better used elsewhere.

::: note Which strategy for which quadrant?
The Study Material lists the four strategies without pairing them to quadrants. ICAI's suggested answers pair them: **Stars, Build; Question marks, Hold; Cash cows, Harvest; Dogs, Divest** (RTP May 2026 Q14; MTP January 2025 Series II Q4(b)). Write that pairing. For a Star alone, the January 2025 suggested answer (Q5(c)) accepted both Build and Hold and rejected Harvest and Divest.
:::

### Is the BCG matrix really helpful?

It has done much to help strategic planning, but it has problems and limitations (SM 4.29):

1. It can be **difficult, time-consuming and costly** to implement.
2. Management may find it difficult to **define SBUs and measure market share and growth**.
3. It focuses on **classifying current businesses** but provides little advice for future planning.
4. It can lead the company to place **too much emphasis on market-share growth** or growth through entry into attractive new markets, causing unwise expansion into hot, new, risky ventures, or divesting established units too quickly.

One parameter of market share cannot define the status of an SBU; it should be categorised on its inherent characteristics. The Study Material's Coufix case (SM 4.29–4.30): a new cough medicine with heavy R&D, marketing and licensing spend gains 60% market share in six months and is profitable from day one. It is a **Star**, not a cash cow, because it is growing rapidly and needs heavy investment to maintain its position.

### The GE matrix ("Stop-Light" strategy model)

This model has been used by the General Electric Company (developed by GE with the assistance of the consulting firm McKinsey and Company). It is also known as the **Business Planning Matrix, GE Nine-Cell Matrix and GE Model**. Its approach is inspired by traffic control lights: green for go, amber or yellow for caution, and red for stop. It uses two factors: **business strength** and **market attractiveness** (SM 4.30).

The **vertical axis** indicates market attractiveness and the **horizontal axis** business strength in the industry. Market attractiveness is measured by a number of factors, and business strength by typical drivers, like these (SM 4.30–4.31):

| Market attractiveness factors | Business strength drivers |
|---|---|
| Size of the market | Market share |
| Market growth rate | Market share growth rate |
| Industry profitability | Profit margin |
| Competitive intensity | Distribution efficiency |
| Availability of technology | Brand image |
| Pricing trends | Ability to compete on price and quality |
| Overall risk of returns in the industry | Customer loyalty |
| Opportunity for differentiation of products and services | Production capacity |
| Demand variability | Technological capability |
| Segmentation | Relative cost position |
| Distribution structure (e.g. direct marketing, retail, wholesale) etc. | Management calibre, etc. |

::: diagram GE nine-cell matrix: rows are market attractiveness, columns business strength
{"type": "table", "head": ["Attractiveness", "Strong", "Average", "Weak"], "rows": [["High", "Invest/Expand", "Invest/Expand", "Select/Earn"], ["Medium", "Invest/Expand", "Select/Earn", "Harvest/Divest"], ["Low", "Select/Earn", "Harvest/Divest", "Harvest/Divest"]], "rowLabels": true, "cells": {"0,1": "ok", "0,2": "ok", "0,3": "hl", "1,1": "ok", "1,2": "hl", "1,3": "bad", "2,1": "hl", "2,2": "bad", "2,3": "bad"}}
:::

The cells as in the Study Material's figure (SM 4.31): green (here green), yellow (here amber) and red (here rose). What each zone means (SM 4.32):

- **Green**: the business is at an advantageous position; to reap the benefits, the strategic decision can be to **expand, to invest and grow**.
- **Amber or yellow**: it needs **caution, and managerial discretion** is called for in making the strategic choices.
- **Red**: it will eventually lead to losses that would make things difficult; the appropriate strategy should be **retrenchment, divestment or liquidation**.

The GE model is similar to the BCG matrix, with two differences (SM 4.32): first, **market attractiveness replaces market growth** as the dimension of industry attractiveness and includes a broader range of factors than just the market growth rate; second, **competitive strength replaces market share** as the dimension by which the competitive position of each SBU is assessed.

## Under the hood

### Relative share and the Study Material's own illustration

The Study Material names the horizontal axis "relative market share" but gives no formula or dividing line. The worked example above states its measure (own share ÷ largest rival's share) and its dividing lines in the question; do the same in an answer when you use numbers. The chapter's own question on Soorya Ltd. (an industry of only two firms) uses market shares of 80%, 40%, 60% and 5% with industry growth of +15%, +10%, −20% and −10%, and ICAI's answer places the products as Star, Question mark, Cash cow and Dog (SM 4.37, 4.47). In a two-firm industry a share above 50% means the firm is bigger than its only rival, so B's 40% is a low relative share. The answer also treats B's +10% growth as high: with only positive and negative rates in the data, the line falls between them. With no dividing line given, state the one you use.

### When ICAI's answers word the GE zones differently

RTP January 2025 Q13 (InnovaTech) described the GE zones as "grow", "hold" and "harvest". The Study Material's figure uses Invest/Expand, Select/Earn and Harvest/Divest, and its zone wording is "expand, invest and grow", "caution and managerial discretion", and "retrenchment, divestment or liquidation". Write the Study Material's words.

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: the new product that was "obviously a Star"
A product launched last month in a booming market is called a Star. **Fix:** it does not yet have a high share: a new product in a rapidly growing market is a **Question mark** (MTP May 2024 Series I; MTP September 2025 Series II). It becomes a Star only once it wins a high share, as Coufix and "Say No to Sugar" did.
:::

::: mistake "Most profitable, so cash cow"
**Fix:** BCG classifies on growth and relative share, not profit. Coufix is profitable from day one and is a **Star**, because its market is growing rapidly and it needs heavy investment (SM 4.29–4.30).
:::

::: mistake Axes swapped or flipped
Putting growth along the bottom, or high share on the right. **Fix:** vertical: market growth rate; horizontal: relative market share, high on the left. For GE: vertical, market attractiveness; horizontal, business strength (May 2026 MCQ 16 asks for these two dimensions).
:::

::: mistake Writing "Divest" for every question mark
**Fix:** question marks need heavy investment, and the Study Material says it is for the organisation to turn them into stars; ICAI's answers pair them with **Hold**. Divest is the strategy for dogs.
:::

## Recap

- Portfolio models channel resources at the corporate level to the businesses with the greatest potential; cash-rich businesses can fund more prospective ones (SM 4.23).
- BCG: vertical axis market growth rate (market attractiveness), horizontal axis relative market share (company strength), high share on the left.
- Stars: rapid growth, heavy investment, best opportunity for expansion. Cash cows: low growth, high share, generate cash, need less investment.
- Question marks: low share, high growth, need a lot of cash, can become cash traps; turn them into stars. Dogs: low growth, low share; minimise by divestment or liquidation.
- Strategies: build, hold, harvest, divest; ICAI's answers pair stars–build, question marks–hold, cash cows–harvest, dogs–divest.
- BCG limitations: difficult and costly; hard to define SBUs and measure share and growth; classifies the present, little advice on the future; over-emphasis on share growth.
- GE (stop-light, with McKinsey): market attractiveness (vertical) × business strength (horizontal); nine cells; green invest and grow, yellow caution, red retrenchment, divestment or liquidation.
- GE differs from BCG: attractiveness replaces growth; competitive strength replaces share. (Kesar still loves all four businesses; the budget does not.)

## Flashcards

Q:: BCG matrix: what do its two axes represent?
A:: Vertical axis: market growth rate (a measure of market attractiveness). Horizontal axis: relative market share (a measure of company strength in the market).

Q:: BCG: Stars?
A:: Products or SBUs growing rapidly; they need heavy investment to maintain their position and finance rapid growth; the best opportunities for expansion.

Q:: BCG: Cash cows?
A:: Low-growth, high market share businesses; they generate cash, have low costs, are established and need less investment to maintain their share.

Q:: BCG: Question marks?
A:: Low market share businesses in high-growth markets (problem children, wildcats); they need a lot of cash to hold their share; left unattended they can become cash traps.

Q:: BCG: Dogs?
A:: Low-growth, low-share businesses; may generate enough cash to maintain themselves but have not much future; minimised by divestment or liquidation.

Q:: BCG: the four post-identification strategies and their objectives?
A:: Build (increase market share, even forgoing short-term earnings); Hold (preserve share); Harvest (increase short-term cash flow regardless of long-term effect); Divest (sell or liquidate, as resources can be better used elsewhere).

Q:: BCG: the pairing of strategies with quadrants in ICAI's suggested answers?
A:: Stars: build; question marks: hold; cash cows: harvest; dogs: divest.

Q:: BCG: four limitations?
A:: Difficult, time-consuming and costly; hard to define SBUs and measure share and growth; classifies current businesses with little advice for future planning; may over-emphasise market-share growth, leading to unwise expansion or divesting too quickly.

Q:: GE matrix: its other names and its two dimensions?
A:: "Stop-Light" strategy model, Business Planning Matrix, GE Nine-Cell Matrix, GE Model; dimensions: market attractiveness (vertical) and business strength (horizontal).

Q:: GE matrix: what does each zone call for?
A:: Green: expand, invest and grow. Amber/yellow: caution and managerial discretion. Red: retrenchment, divestment or liquidation.

Q:: GE matrix: the cells of the top row (high attractiveness), from strong to weak?
A:: Invest/Expand, Invest/Expand, Select/Earn.

Q:: GE versus BCG: the two differences in the Study Material?
A:: Market attractiveness replaces market growth and covers a broader range of factors; competitive strength replaces market share in assessing each SBU's position.
