## In plain words

### BrewBucks is not the only problem

BrewBucks has opened forty cafés in Tapri's cities, and Kesar has called a war council.

> **Kesar:** One enemy, one plan. We beat BrewBucks and the profits come back.
> **Ananya:** Five pressures are squeezing our margin, Kesar-ji, and BrewBucks is only one of them.
> **Dev:** And each of them has a present value. Mostly negative.

Ananya is doing **industry analysis**: looking at the whole industry Tapri competes in, to estimate how much **competitive pressure** it faces now and will face soon. Michael Porter's model says the pressure comes from five places:

- **Rivalry among existing firms**: the other café chains already fighting for the same customers with prices, offers and advertising.
- **New entrants**: firms that might enter (BrewBucks was one; a quick-commerce app planning its own chai counters is the next). They add capacity and push prices down.
- **Substitutes**: products from other industries that do the same job (cold coffee, energy drinks, chai made at home).
- **Suppliers**: if only two estates grow the special Assam blend, they can raise prices.
- **Buyers**: if a few corporate canteens and food apps buy most of the volume, they can squeeze prices.

The **collective strength** of these five forces decides how much profit an industry lets its firms earn. Where the forces are weak, the industry is **attractive**; where they are fierce, profits are thin for all. Attractiveness is **relative**: an industry that is unattractive to a weak firm may be attractive to a strong one.

One more weapon for the incumbents: the **experience curve**. The more samosas Tapri's factory has made in total, the cheaper each new one becomes, because people learn, processes improve and scale grows. A newcomer starts at the top of the curve, and that cost gap is itself a barrier to entry.

::: analogy Your stall at the Diwali mela
You sell pani puri at the mela. The stall next door is the rivalry. The empty plot that a new stall could take tomorrow is the threat of entry. The family that brought its own tiffin is the substitute. The only ice-seller at the mela, who doubles his price on the busiest night, is supplier power. The school group asking for forty plates at a discount is buyer power. Your profit is not decided by your neighbour alone: it is what all five leave you. And having made pani puri for twenty Diwalis, you are faster and cheaper than any first-timer: that is the experience curve.
:::

::: key
The state of competition in an industry is a composite of five competitive pressures: rivalry among existing sellers, the threat of new entrants, substitutes from other industries, and the bargaining power of suppliers and of buyers. ==The collective strength of these five competitive forces determines the scope to earn attractive profits==, and that attractiveness is relative, not absolute.
:::

## How it works

### Industry environment analysis

Industry analysis builds a picture of key industry traits, competition intensity, industry change drivers, rivals' market positions and tactics, competitive success and profit forecasts, and helps decide whether the industry is lucrative. Its goal, typically an important step of strategic analysis, is ==to estimate the amount of competitive pressures the business is presently facing and is expected to face in the near future==. It sees the firm in the context of a bigger framework and is the foundation for aligning strategy with changing industry circumstances (SM 2.26).

### The five forces model

Porter's five forces analysis is a simple but efficient way of determining the key sources of competition in a business or industry, and a powerful and widely used tool to **systematically diagnose the significant competitive pressures in a market and assess the strength and importance of each** (SM 2.26). For Porter, the basic unit of analysis is **a group of competitors producing goods or services that compete directly with each other**: it is the industry where competitive advantage is ultimately won or lost (SM 2.27).

The character, mix and intricacies of competitive forces are never the same from one industry to another. The model holds that the state of competition in an industry is a composite of competitive pressures operating in **five areas** of the overall market (SM 2.27):

1. Competitive pressures associated with the market manoeuvring and jockeying for buyer patronage that goes on among **rival sellers** in the industry.
2. Competitive pressures associated with the threat of **new entrants** into the market.
3. Competitive pressures coming from the attempts of companies in other industries to win buyers over to their own **substitute products**.
4. Competitive pressures stemming from **supplier bargaining power** and supplier-seller collaboration.
5. Competitive pressures stemming from **buyer bargaining power** and seller-buyer collaboration.

Strategists use the model in **three steps** (SM 2.27):

::: steps
1. **Identify** the specific competitive pressures associated with each of the five forces.
2. **Evaluate** how strong the pressures comprising each of the five forces are (fierce, strong, moderate to normal, or weak).
3. **Determine** whether the collective strength of the five competitive forces is conducive to earning attractive profits.
:::

The model is one of the most effective and enduring frameworks to assess the nature of the competitive environment and describe an industry's structure; the interrelationship among the five forces gives each industry its own competitive environment, and by applying it managers can gauge their own firm's strengths, weaknesses and future opportunities (SM 2.28).

### I. The threat of new entrants

New entrants can **reduce industry profitability** because they add new production capacity, increasing the supply of the product even at a lower price, and can substantially erode existing firms' market share. They are always a powerful source of competition; **the bigger the new entrant, the more severe the competitive effect**; they also place a limit on prices. A firm's profitability tends to be higher when other firms are blocked from entering (SM 2.28–2.29).

::: definition Barriers to entry
"Barriers to entry represent economic forces (or 'hurdles') that slow down or impede entry by other firms." Existing firms try to raise them to discourage new entrants (SM 2.29).
:::

The common barriers to entry, in the Study Material's order (SM 2.29–2.30):

1. **Capital requirements**: when a large amount of capital is required to enter, firms lacking funds are effectively barred, enhancing the profitability of existing firms.
2. **Economies of scale**: the decline in the per-unit cost of production (or other activity) as volume grows. A large firm producing high volumes at successively lower costs discourages new entrants.
3. **Product differentiation**: the physical or perceptual differences, or enhancements, that make a product special or unique in the eyes of customers (personal care and cosmetics). The cost of creating genuine product differences may be too high for new entrants.
4. **Switching costs**: a new entrant must persuade customers of other companies to switch; buyers may have to test the product, negotiate new contracts, train personnel or modify facilities, incurring substantial financial (and psychological) costs. When switching costs are high, buyers are reluctant to change.
5. **Brand identity**: particularly important for infrequently purchased products that carry a high unit cost to the buyer; building it needs substantial resources over a long period.
6. **Access to distribution channels**: despite the internet, many firms rely on their control of physical distribution channels; existing firms can retard or impede their use by new firms.
7. **Possibility of aggressive retaliation**: the mere threat of retaliation by incumbents (cutting prices, raising advertising budgets when a new firm launches) can deter entry.

### II. Bargaining power of buyers

This force becomes heavier where buyers can form **groups or cartels**, mostly seen in industrial products, where users come together formally or informally and pressure the producer. Powerful buyers influence not only the prices the producer can charge but also its **costs and investments**, because they bargain for better services. Buyers can exert considerable pressure to secure lower prices or better services particularly when (SM 2.30–2.31):

1. buyers have **full knowledge** of the sources of products and their substitutes;
2. they spend a lot of money on the industry's products, i.e. they are **big buyers**;
3. the industry's product is **not perceived as critical** to the buyer's needs and buyers are **more concentrated** than the firms supplying the product; they can easily switch to the substitutes available.

### III. Bargaining power of suppliers

The more **specialised** the supplier's offering, the greater its clout; if suppliers are also **limited in number**, they stand a still better chance to exercise bargaining power. Supplier power determines the cost of raw materials and other inputs, and therefore industry attractiveness and profitability. Suppliers command bargaining power over a firm when (SM 2.31):

1. their products are **crucial** to the buyer and **substitutes are not available**;
2. they can erect **high switching costs**;
3. they are **more concentrated than their buyers**.

### IV. The nature of rivalry in the industry

Rivalry among existing players is what is normally understood as competition; its impact shows most at the functional level, in prices, advertising and pressures on costs and product. Its intensity is a significant determinant of industry attractiveness and profitability: **the more intensive the rivalry, the less attractive the industry**. Rivalry tends to be cut-throat and profitability low under these conditions (SM 2.31–2.32):

| Condition | What the Study Material says |
|---|---|
| (i) Industry leader | A strong leader can discourage price wars by disciplining initiators; with greater financial resources it can outlast smaller rivals, so they avoid starting one |
| (ii) Number of competitors | The leader's ability to exert pricing discipline diminishes as the number of rivals grows: communicating expectations becomes harder |
| (iii) Fixed costs | Rivals with high fixed costs are inclined to cut prices when they have excess capacity, so profitability is lower in high fixed cost industries |
| (iv) Exit barriers | Specialised assets find no buyer, so firms that want to leave stay and press down profitability; profitability is higher with few exit barriers |
| (v) Product differentiation | Differentiated products insulate firms from price wars; undifferentiated commodities (memory chips, natural resources, processed metals, railroads) earn lower profits |
| (vi) Slow growth | As industry growth slows, rivals fight harder to grow or even keep their share, reducing profitability for all |

### V. Threat of substitutes

Substitute products are a **latent source of competition** that often becomes a major one. Substitutes offering a **price advantage and/or performance improvement** can drastically alter the competitive character of an industry, and all of a sudden: coir suffered at the hands of synthetic fibre. Wherever substantial investment in R&D is taking place, threats from substitutes can be expected; substitutes usually limit the prices and profits in an industry. To predict this pressure, firms must search for products that perform **the same, or nearly the same, function**: real estate, insurance, bonds and bank deposits are substitutes for common stocks, because they are alternative ways to invest funds (SM 2.33).

**The five forces together** determine industry attractiveness and profitability, because they govern the elements (such as the cost and investment needed to be a player) that decide industry profitability. Their collective strength determines the scope to earn attractive profits, and varies from industry to industry (SM 2.33).

### Attractiveness of the industry

Industry analysis culminates in conclusions about the relative attractiveness or unattractiveness of the industry, both near-term and long-term: companies invest capital, their promoters' or the public's, and must choose an industry with care. The factors on which management may base its conclusion (SM 2.33–2.34):

1. The industry's **growth potential**: is it futuristically viable?
2. Whether competition currently permits **adequate profitability**, and whether the competitive forces will become stronger or weaker.
3. Whether industry profitability will be favourably or unfavourably affected by the prevailing **driving forces**.
4. The **competitive position** of the organisation in the industry and whether it is likely to grow stronger or weaker (a well-entrenched leader in a lacklustre industry can still earn good profits; an uphill battle against much stronger rivals can make an attractive industry unattractive).
5. The potential to **capitalise on the vulnerabilities of weaker rivals**.
6. Whether the company can **defend against or counteract** the factors that make the industry unattractive.
7. The degrees of **risk and uncertainty** in the industry's future.
8. The **severity of problems** confronting the industry as a whole.
9. Whether continued participation adds importantly to the firm's ability to succeed in **other industries** in which it has business interests.

As a general proposition, an industry whose overall profit prospects are above average is attractive, and one below average unattractive. But it is a mistake to think of an industry as attractive or unattractive to all firms and all potential entrants: **attractiveness is relative, not absolute**; environments unattractive to weak competitors may be attractive to strong ones (SM 2.34).

### The experience curve

::: definition Experience curve
Akin to a learning curve, which explains the efficiency increase gained by workers through repetitive productive work. It is based on the commonly observed phenomenon that **unit costs decline as a firm accumulates experience in terms of a cumulative volume of production**: "we learn as we grow" (SM 2.35).
:::

**Implication**: larger firms in an industry tend to have lower unit costs than smaller companies, gaining a competitive cost advantage. **Causes**: learning effects, economies of scale, product redesign and technological improvements in production (SM 2.35).

**Features** (SM 2.35):

- As business organisations grow, they gain experience.
- Experience may provide an advantage over the competition. Experience is a key **barrier to entry**.
- Large and successful organisations possess stronger "experience effect".

## Worked examples

:::: example Five pressures on a bottle of chai
Tapri is studying the ready-to-drink bottled chai industry before entering it. Ananya's notes:

1. Four large beverage companies already sell bottled chai. Their products taste nearly the same, industry growth has slowed to 3% a year, and their bottling plants, which carry high fixed costs, run at 60% of capacity.
2. Two global soft-drink companies have announced bottled chai launches for next year.
3. Food-grade bottles come from two manufacturers only, and the regulator has approved no other packaging for hot-filled tea.
4. Three national supermarket chains and two quick-commerce apps buy 70% of the industry's volume. They track every brand's price and shift shelf space between brands at will.
5. Cold coffee, iced tea, lassi and energy drinks all compete for the same "cold drink on the go" occasion, often at lower prices.

Required:

(a) Identify the competitive force reflected in each note and evaluate its strength, following the steps in Porter's model.

(b) Conclude whether the industry is attractive, and advise Tapri.

::: answer Model answer
**(a)** Porter's five forces model holds that the state of competition in an industry is a composite of competitive pressures operating in five areas: rivalry among existing sellers, the threat of new entrants, substitutes from other industries, and the bargaining power of suppliers and of buyers. Following its steps, the pressures are identified and their strength evaluated:

| Note | Force | Strength and reason |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Rivalry among existing firms | Fierce: undifferentiated products, slow growth, and high fixed costs with excess capacity all incline rivals to cut prices |
| 2 | Threat of new entrants | Strong: large entrants add capacity and push prices down; the bigger the entrant, the more severe the effect |
| 3 | Bargaining power of suppliers | Strong: suppliers are few and concentrated, and their product is crucial with no substitutes available |
| 4 | Bargaining power of buyers | Strong: big, concentrated buyers with full knowledge of sources who can switch easily |
| 5 | Threat of substitutes | Strong: products performing nearly the same function, with a price advantage, limit prices and profits |

**(b)** The collective strength of the five forces determines the scope to earn attractive profits. Here every force is strong or fierce, so the industry's profit prospects appear below average: it is unattractive for an average firm. Attractiveness is, however, relative, not absolute: an industry unattractive to weak competitors may be attractive to strong ones. Tapri should therefore enter only if it can defend against or counteract the factors that make the industry unattractive, for example a differentiated product (kulhad-style chai the four rivals cannot copy quickly) sold also through its own cafés and app, which reduces its dependence on the five big buyers. Otherwise it should invest cautiously or look for a more attractive opportunity.
:::

:::: example The hurdles in front of BrewBucks
BrewBucks wants to enter India's packaged chai premix industry, where Tapri Classic is a leading brand. It learns that:

1. A national premix business needs blending plants, a national distribution system and three years of advertising before it breaks even.
2. Tapri blends 40,000 tonnes a year, so its cost per kilogram is far below what a small plant could achieve.
3. Shoppers buy premix every week and ask for Tapri by name; Tapri spent fifteen years building the brand.
4. Tapri's kirana distributors in 600 towns carry its stock on exclusive terms.
5. When a regional brand entered last year, Tapri cut its price by 15% in that brand's towns within a month.

Identify the barriers to entry BrewBucks faces and explain how each restrains its entry.

::: answer Model answer
According to Porter, new entrants reduce industry profitability, so existing firms try to raise **barriers to entry**: economic forces (or hurdles) that slow down or impede entry by other firms. In BrewBucks' case:

1. **Capital requirements**: plants, distribution and years of advertising need a large amount of capital; firms lacking funds are effectively barred.
2. **Economies of scale**: Tapri's per-unit cost declines as its volume grows; an entrant producing small volumes at higher unit costs is discouraged.
3. **Brand identity**: Tapri's brand is built by committing substantial resources over a long period, which an entrant must match. Because premix is a frequently purchased, low-cost product, brand identity is a weaker barrier here than it would be for an infrequently purchased product with a high unit cost.
4. **Access to distribution channels**: Tapri's exclusive control of kirana distributors can retard or impede their use by a new firm.
5. **Possibility of aggressive retaliation**: Tapri's past price cut shows that incumbents may reduce prices and raise advertising when a new firm enters; the mere threat can deter entry.

Product differentiation may also act as a barrier if Tapri's blends are genuinely different, as creating genuine differences may be too costly for an entrant. Taken together, these barriers keep the threat of new entrants in this industry moderate, even against an entrant as large as BrewBucks.
:::

## Under the hood

### Writing the "industry leader" condition

The Study Material lists the industry leader first among the conditions under which rivalry tends to be cut-throat, but its explanation says that a **strong** leader **discourages** price wars (SM 2.31–2.32). The condition is therefore read as: where no strong leader disciplines price-cutters, or where the leader's discipline weakens as rivals multiply, rivalry is fiercer. ICAI's suggested answers write it as "while a strong industry leader can help maintain pricing discipline, the effectiveness diminishes as the number of competitors increases" (January 2025 Q8(a); RTP January 2026 Q10). Write the condition that way, and never "a strong leader makes rivalry cut-throat".

### Economies of scale and the experience curve

| Point | Economies of scale | Experience curve |
|---|---|---|
| What lowers unit cost | Volume grows (per-unit cost declines as volume grows) | Cumulative volume of production grows (experience accumulates) |
| Where the Study Material places it | A barrier to entry under the threat of new entrants (SM 2.29) | A concept of its own; economies of scale are one of its causes (SM 2.35) |
| Common ground | Both make larger firms cheaper and act as barriers to entry | |

### When a fact fits two forces

ICAI's suggested answers name one force per fact, and their choice is worth knowing. E-commerce firms building their own delivery networks were treated as the **threat of new entrants** to a logistics company, and an 18% rise in fuel prices as **supplier power** (RTP September 2026 Q9). "Vocal for Local" changing customer preferences was keyed as the **threat of substitutes** (MTP September 2024 Series I), and partnerships with international firms as mitigating the **threat of new entrants** (MTP September 2024 Series II). Where a fact could fit two forces, name the one the facts point to most directly and say why in one line.

## Answer frame

### A five forces case (5 marks)

1. **Model (1 line)**: "Porter's five forces model holds that the state of competition in an industry is a composite of competitive pressures operating in five areas ..."
2. **Force by force**: name the force, the fact from the case, the Study Material's condition it meets, and its strength (fierce, strong, moderate, weak). A table works well.
3. **Forces the case is silent on**: say so; add an inference only if the facts support it.
4. **Conclusion**: the collective strength of the forces decides the scope to earn attractive profits; is the industry attractive for this firm (relative, not absolute), and what should it do?

### "Explain the conditions in which rivalry is cut-throat" (5 marks)

Open with "the more intensive the rivalry, the less attractive the industry", then the six conditions in order: industry leader, number of competitors, fixed costs, exit barriers, product differentiation, slow growth, each with its one-line reason.

### "Explain the experience curve and its relevance" (5 marks)

Concept (learning curve; unit costs decline with cumulative volume; "we learn as we grow"); implication (larger firms, lower unit costs, cost advantage); causes (learning effects, economies of scale, product redesign, technological improvements); the three features; a sketch of cost per unit against cumulative production; relevance (barrier to entry, builds market share and discourages competition; Maruti Suzuki; rivals choose a niche or segmentation by demography or geography).

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: "Bargaining power of unions"
Bunty's list of five forces includes trade unions and the government. **Fix:** the five are the threat of new entrants, the bargaining power of buyers, the bargaining power of suppliers, rivalry among existing firms and the threat of substitutes. The Study Material's own MCQ uses "bargaining power of unions" as the wrong option (SM 2.47).
:::

::: mistake Calling a rival's similar product a "substitute"
A competing bottled chai is rivalry; cold coffee is a substitute. **Fix:** substitutes come from **companies in other industries** and perform the same, or nearly the same, function; products of rivals in the same industry belong to rivalry.
:::

::: mistake Mixing up who has the power
"The suppliers are many and small, so supplier power is high." **Fix:** suppliers are powerful when they are few and more concentrated than their buyers, their product is crucial with no substitutes, and they can erect high switching costs; buyers are powerful when they are big, concentrated, fully informed and able to switch.
:::

::: mistake "This industry is attractive", full stop
**Fix:** attractiveness is relative, not absolute. State for whom: an industry unattractive to weak competitors may be attractive to strong ones, depending on the firm's competitive position and its ability to counteract the unattractive factors.
:::

Q: Case scenario. Shakti Cement Ltd is one of nine cement makers in Madhya Pradesh. Cement is a near-identical product, demand growth in the state has slowed, every plant has heavy fixed costs and spare capacity, and plants are so specialised that no buyer can be found for one. Which force is most significant for Shakti Cement?
- [ ] Threat of substitutes
- [ ] Bargaining power of suppliers
- [x] Rivalry among existing firms
- [ ] Threat of new entrants
> Undifferentiated products, slow growth, high fixed costs with excess capacity and high exit barriers (specialised assets) are four of the six conditions under which rivalry tends to be cut-throat.

Q: Which of the following is NOT one of the common barriers to entry listed in the Study Material?
- [ ] Switching costs
- [ ] Possibility of aggressive retaliation
- [ ] Access to distribution channels
- [x] High exit barriers
> Exit barriers are a condition of rivalry: specialised assets keep firms that want to leave in the industry. The seven barriers to entry are capital requirements, economies of scale, product differentiation, switching costs, brand identity, access to distribution channels and aggressive retaliation.

Q: Suppliers can command bargaining power over a firm when: Select all that apply.
- [x] their products are crucial to the buyer and substitutes are not available
- [x] they can erect high switching costs
- [ ] they are many, small and compete for the firm's orders
- [x] they are more concentrated than their buyers
> The Study Material's three conditions. Many small suppliers competing for orders have little power, as ICAI's suggested answer to RTP September 2024 Q10 notes for an extensive supplier base.

Q: A spice exporter's unit cost was ₹200 a kilogram when its cumulative output was 1,000 tonnes, and each doubling of cumulative output has cut unit cost by 20%. What is its unit cost, in ₹ per kg, at a cumulative output of 4,000 tonnes?
= 128
> 4,000 tonnes is two doublings: 200 × 0.8 = ₹160, then 160 × 0.8 = ₹128. This fall in unit cost as cumulative volume grows is the experience curve.

Q: An insurance company's analyst lists products that compete for its customers' savings. Which of these is a substitute in Porter's sense?
- [ ] A rival insurer's similar policy
- [x] Bank fixed deposits
- [ ] A reinsurer that sells cover to the company
- [ ] A large employer that buys group policies for its staff
> Substitutes come from other industries and perform the same or nearly the same function: bank deposits are an alternative way to invest funds. A rival insurer is rivalry, the reinsurer a supplier, and the employer a buyer.

## Recap

- Industry analysis estimates the competitive pressures the business faces now and will face in the near future; the industry is where competitive advantage is won or lost.
- Five areas of pressure: rivalry among sellers, threat of new entrants, substitutes from other industries, supplier power and supplier-seller collaboration, buyer power and seller-buyer collaboration.
- Three steps: identify the pressures, evaluate their strength (fierce, strong, moderate to normal, weak), determine whether their collective strength permits attractive profits.
- Barriers to entry: capital requirements, economies of scale, product differentiation, switching costs, brand identity, access to distribution channels, aggressive retaliation.
- Buyers are powerful when fully informed, big, concentrated and able to switch; suppliers when their product is crucial with no substitutes, switching costs are high and they are more concentrated than their buyers.
- Rivalry is cut-throat with no strong leader to discipline it, many competitors, high fixed costs, high exit barriers, little differentiation and slow growth.
- Substitutes perform the same function from another industry and limit prices and profits. Attractiveness is relative, not absolute.
- Experience curve: unit costs decline with cumulative volume ("we learn as we grow"); a barrier to entry; rivals respond with a niche or segmentation by demography or geography. (The twentieth-Diwali pani puri stall wins on cost.)

## Flashcards

Q:: Porter's five forces: in which five areas do competitive pressures operate?
A:: Rivalry among existing sellers; threat of new entrants; substitutes from companies in other industries; supplier bargaining power and supplier-seller collaboration; buyer bargaining power and seller-buyer collaboration.

Q:: What are the three steps in using the five forces model?
A:: (1) Identify the specific competitive pressures of each force; (2) evaluate how strong each is (fierce, strong, moderate to normal, weak); (3) determine whether their collective strength is conducive to attractive profits.

Q:: Define barriers to entry.
A:: Economic forces (or hurdles) that slow down or impede entry by other firms; existing firms try to raise them to discourage new entrants.

Q:: Name the seven common barriers to entry in ICAI's order.
A:: Capital requirements, economies of scale, product differentiation, switching costs, brand identity, access to distribution channels, possibility of aggressive retaliation.

Q:: When do buyers have bargaining power over an industry?
A:: When they have full knowledge of sources and substitutes; when they are big buyers; when the product is not critical to them and they are more concentrated than the suppliers, so they can easily switch.

Q:: When do suppliers command bargaining power?
A:: When their products are crucial to the buyer and substitutes are not available; when they can erect high switching costs; when they are more concentrated than their buyers.

Q:: Under what six conditions does rivalry tend to be cut-throat?
A:: Industry leader (no strong leader to discipline price wars), number of competitors, high fixed costs, exit barriers, lack of product differentiation, slow growth.

Q:: What are substitute products in Porter's model?
A:: Products of companies in other industries that perform the same or nearly the same function; a latent source of competition that limits prices and profits (coir suffered at the hands of synthetic fibre).

Q:: Is industry attractiveness absolute?
A:: No. Attractiveness is relative, not absolute: an industry unattractive to weak competitors may be attractive to strong competitors.

Q:: Define the experience curve.
A:: Akin to a learning curve: unit costs decline as a firm accumulates experience in terms of a cumulative volume of production ("we learn as we grow").

Q:: What causes the experience curve?
A:: Learning effects, economies of scale, product redesign and technological improvements in production.

Q:: Experience curve: relevance in strategic management?
A:: A barrier to entry for new firms; used to build market share and discourage competition (Maruti Suzuki); competitors may choose a market niche or segmentation based on demography or geography.
