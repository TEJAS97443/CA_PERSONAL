## Lesson 11.1: Apportioning joint costs: physical units, sales value and net realisable value

*One process, several products, one bill. How ICAI's methods share the joint cost up to the split-off point, and why the method changes each product's cost and profit.*

### Key idea

A joint cost cannot be traced to any one joint product; it can only be apportioned on a rational basis. The method (by quantity, by sales value, by net realisable value) changes each product's cost, profit and inventory value, never the total joint cost, so a product's "loss" after apportionment proves nothing by itself.

### Formats and formulas

*Reproduce the NRV statement and the formulas from memory; they are the working notes ICAI's answers start with.*

**[formula] Physical units (and average unit cost)**
$$\text{Share of a product} = \frac{\text{Joint cost}}{\text{Total units}} \times \text{Units of the product}$$
Units are the quantities **produced** at split-off, in one common unit.

**[formula] Sales value at split-off: the multiplying factor**
$$\text{Multiplying factor} = \frac{\text{Joint cost}}{\text{Total sales revenue}} \times 100$$
Total sales revenue = Σ (quantity produced × market price at split-off). Joint cost of a product = its sales value at split-off × multiplying factor (SM 11.8).

**[formula] Net realisable value**
$$\text{NRV} = \text{Final sales} - \text{Profit margin} \qquad - \text{S\&D costs} - \text{Post split-off cost}$$
Joint cost of a product = total joint cost × its NRV ÷ total NRV. Final sales value = units after processing × selling price. S&D = directly attributable selling and distribution costs.

**NRV statement** (ICAI's pro-forma, SM 11.7):

| Particulars | Product A (₹) | Product B (₹) | Product C (₹) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Sales value (units × final price) | xxx | xxx | xxx |
| Less: Profit margin | (xxx) | (xxx) | (xxx) |
| Less: Selling and distribution costs | (xxx) | (xxx) | (xxx) |
| Less: Post split-off cost | (xxx) | (xxx) | (xxx) |
| **Net realisable value** | **xxx** | **xxx** | **xxx** |
| Joint cost apportioned (NRV ratio) | xxx | xxx | xxx |

The profit margin line is used only when the question gives a margin; most questions give none, and the line drops out.

**[formula] Contribution margin method**
$$\text{Contribution} = \text{Sales} - \text{Variable cost}$$
Variable cost = variable joint cost shared on physical units + variable cost after split-off. Fixed joint cost of a product = total fixed joint cost × its contribution ÷ total contribution. Its joint cost = its variable share + its fixed share.

**[formula] Constant gross-margin percentage NRV method**
$$\text{Gross margin \%} = \frac{\text{Final sales} - \text{All costs}}{\text{Final sales}} \times 100$$
All costs = joint cost + separable (post split-off) costs; final sales = final sales value of the whole production.

Joint cost of a product = its final sales value × (1 − gross margin %) − its separable costs, so every product earns the same gross margin percentage (SM 11.38–11.39, Case Scenario 1).

**[exam] The traps ICAI set**
- Quantities **sold** given with opening and closing stocks: the base is quantity **produced** = sold + closing − opening (RTP May 2026 MCQ 5; RTP January 2025 MCQ 6, whose wrong option (a) uses units sold).

**[trick] Check the total**
Whatever the method, the shares must add back to the total joint cost, and (with no closing stock) the product profits must add to the same total profit. Condition: the same joint cost, and no by-product credit taken in one method but not the other.

### Answer frame

### "Describe briefly how joint costs are apportioned under …"

The chapter's own theory question (SM 11.23, Q4). Write each method as a definition, the basis and one limitation, in this order:

1. **Average unit cost**: total process cost up to separation ÷ total units produced; all products get a uniform cost per unit; if used for pricing, all products may have more or less the same price.
2. **Contribution margin**: joint cost split into variable and fixed; variable apportioned on physical quantities, plus variable cost after split-off; contribution = sales − variable cost; fixed apportioned in the ratio of contributions.
3. **Market value at the point of separation**: joint cost apportioned in the ratio of sales value at split-off (multiplying factor = joint cost ÷ total sales value × 100); difficult when the market value at separation is not available; useful when further processing costs are disproportionate.
4. **Market value after further processing**: ratio of final sales values; unfair when further processing costs are disproportionate or not all products are processed further.
5. **Net realisable value**: from final sales value deduct estimated profit margin, selling and distribution expenses and post split-off costs; joint cost apportioned in the ratio of NRV.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the joint cost that followed the sales invoice** **Fix:** produced = sold + closing stock − opening stock, then apportion.
- **NRV that forgot to subtract anything** **Fix:** NRV = final sales value − post split-off cost − attributable selling and distribution costs (− profit margin if given).
- **The contribution margin method done in one step** **Fix:** variable joint cost on physical units first; contribution = sales − variable cost; only the fixed joint cost follows the contribution ratio.
- **Kilos and litres added together** **Fix:** convert to a common unit first (litres to kg through the density, for example); if no common unit exists, the method cannot be applied (SM 11.5).

### Recap

- Joint products: two or more products of equal importance, produced simultaneously from the same process, each with a significant relative sale value (SM 11.2).
- Joint cost is everything up to the split-off point; after it, costs belong to the product concerned.
- Apportionment affects inventory valuation, pricing and product profit; the cost cannot be identified, only apportioned on a rational basis.
- Commonly used: physical units, NRV at split-off, technical estimates. Other: market value at separation, market value after further processing, average unit cost, contribution margin.
- Multiplying factor = joint cost ÷ total sales value at split-off × 100.
- NRV = final sales − profit margin − selling and distribution costs − post split-off cost.
- Contribution margin method: variable joint cost on units, fixed joint cost on contribution.
- Base is units produced (sold + closing − opening). The handi cost the same; only the slicing changed.

## Lesson 11.2: Sell at split-off or process further

*At the split-off point the joint cost is already spent. Process a joint product further only if the extra revenue beats the extra cost; ICAI's statement and its traps.*

### Key idea

At the split-off point the joint cost is already spent and is the same whichever way you go, so it is irrelevant. Process a product further only if its incremental revenue (sales after further processing minus sales value at split-off) exceeds its incremental cost (every cost the further processing adds, less any it saves).

### Formats and formulas

*Reproduce the incremental statement and the plan statement from memory; ICAI's suggested answers use exactly these layouts.*

**[formula] Incremental profit from further processing**
$$\text{Incremental profit} = (\text{Sales after processing} - \text{Sales at split-off}) \qquad - \text{Incremental cost}$$
Sales after processing = output after any loss × final price. Sales at split-off = quantity at split-off × split-off price. Process further if the result is positive.

**[formula] Lowest final price that justifies further processing**
$$\text{Minimum final price} = \frac{\text{Sales value at split-off} + \text{Incremental cost}}{\text{Output after further processing}}$$
At this price the incremental profit is nil.

**Statement of incremental profit or loss** (per product; per unit or in total):

| Particulars | Product X (₹) | Product Y (₹) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Sales value after further processing | xxx | xxx |
| Less: Sales value at split-off | (xxx) | (xxx) |
| **Incremental revenue** | **xxx** | **xxx** |
| Less: Further processing cost | (xxx) | (xxx) |
| Less: Further marketing cost | (xxx) | (xxx) |
| Add: Selling expenses saved | xxx | xxx |
| **Incremental profit/(loss)** | **xxx** | **xxx** |

**Statement of profit under a production plan** (SM 11.31–11.33; one column a product, then a total):

| Line | Particulars |
|---|---|

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the joint cost that sneaked back in** **Fix:** the joint cost is the same whether the product is sold at split-off or processed further; compare only incremental revenue with incremental cost.
- **The 5% that vanished** **Fix:** final sales = output **after** the loss × final price; split-off sales = quantity **before** the process × split-off price.
- **Comparing the final sales with the further processing cost alone** **Fix:** incremental revenue is the *difference* between the two sales values; the split-off sales given up are part of the comparison.
- **Forgetting the costs on either side of the factory gate** **Fix:** every cost that changes with the choice goes in, with its sign.

### Recap

- Joint cost is spent before the split-off point and is the same whatever is decided after it: irrelevant to sell-or-process-further.
- Incremental revenue = sales after further processing − sales value at split-off.
- Incremental cost = further processing cost + extra selling, packing, marketing − selling expenses saved; fixed costs only if they arise because of the processing.
- Process further if incremental revenue > incremental cost; otherwise sell at the split-off point.
- A loss in further processing cuts the output: final sales use the units after the loss.
- An offer for the product at split-off replaces the split-off price in the comparison.
- ICAI's advice: "It is advisable to further process only product ___ and to sell product ___ at the point of separation."
- The coconut cost ₹40 either way; only the malai's ₹10 and the minute matter.

## Lesson 11.3: Treatment of by-products

*What makes a by-product, ICAI's four ways of valuing it, and its three treatments in cost accounts: small value, considerable value, and value only after further processing.*

### Key idea

A by-product is recovered incidentally and is of small value compared with the main product. Its realisation, net of the costs of processing and selling it, is credited (to the cost of the main product, or to the Costing Profit and Loss Account when small) instead of the by-product carrying a share of joint cost; once its value is considerable, it is treated as a joint product.

### Formats and formulas

*Reproduce these two working notes from memory; ICAI's suggested answers open with one of them.*

**[formula] Net realisable value of a by-product**
$$\text{NRV} = \text{Sales value} - \text{Further processing cost} \qquad - \text{Selling, distribution, admin. cost}$$
The admin. cost counted is the administration expense attributable to the by-product (SM 11.16).

**[formula] Net joint cost to apportion over the main products**
$$\text{Net joint cost} = \text{Joint cost} - \text{NRV of by-product}$$
Then apportion the net joint cost over the joint products by the method the question names ([[cma.joint.apportion|apportioning joint costs]]).

| Calculation of net joint cost | Amount (₹) |
|---|---:|
| Joint costs | xxx |
| Less: Net realisable value of by-product | (xxx) |
| **Net joint costs to be apportioned** | **xxx** |

**[formula] Joint cost carried by a by-product, when an estimated profit is given**
$$\text{Joint cost of by-product} = \text{Sales} - \text{Estimated profit} \qquad - \text{Selling expenses} - \text{Cost after separation}$$
The main product bears the rest: total joint cost − joint cost of the by-products. The by-product is left with exactly its estimated profit.

### Answer frame

### "Distinguish between joint products and by-products"

Write the compare table from How it works: ICAI's two points (importance; simultaneous against incidental production) first, then cost treatment and one example on each side.

### "Discuss the treatment of by-product cost in cost accounting"

1. **When they are of small total value**: (1) credit the sales value to the Costing Profit and Loss Account, no credit in the cost accounts, as miscellaneous income or additional sales revenue; (2) deduct the sale proceeds from the total costs, from the production cost or the cost of sales.
2. **When they are of considerable total value**: regard them as joint products; apportion the costs up to the point of separation on a logical basis (relative market values, physical output at split-off, or ultimate selling prices).
3. **Where they require further processing**: NRV at split-off = realisable value − further processing cost; small, treat as in 1; considerable, as in 2.

### "Discuss the NRV method of apportioning joint costs to by-products"

1. The realisation on disposal of the by-product is deducted from the total cost of production to arrive at the cost of the main product (molasses reduces the cost of sugar).
2. If the by-product needs additional processing, the expenses are deducted from its realisation, and only the net realisation is deducted from the cost of production.
3. Separate accounts collect the additional expenses on (a) further processing of the by-product and (b) its selling, distribution and administration.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the by-product credited at its price tag** **Fix:** credit only the net realisable value: sales value − further processing − attributable selling, distribution and administration costs (SM 11.16).
- **A by-product with a share of the joint cost on physical units** **Fix:** a by-product of small value is credited at its NRV; only a by-product of considerable value is treated as a joint product, and then by the basis the question gives.
- **The estimated profit left inside the by-product's cost** **Fix:** when the question gives an estimated profit on the by-product, deduct it first; the by-product keeps that profit and only the rest reduces the main product's joint cost.

### Recap

- By-product: recovered incidentally from the material used for the main product; value relatively insignificant against the main product (molasses, tar, glycerin).
- Joint products: equal importance, produced simultaneously; by-products: small economic value, produced incidentally.
- ICAI's four methods for by-products: NRV; standard cost in technical estimates; comparative price; re-use basis.
- NRV of a by-product = sales value − further processing − attributable selling, distribution and administration costs.
- Small total value: credit Costing P&L (miscellaneous income or additional sales) or deduct from production cost or cost of sales.
- Considerable total value: treat as a joint product and apportion the joint cost.
- Needs further processing: NRV at split-off first, then small or considerable.
- With an estimated profit: by-product's joint cost = sales − profit − selling expenses − cost after separation; the main product bears the rest. The barber still sells haircuts.
