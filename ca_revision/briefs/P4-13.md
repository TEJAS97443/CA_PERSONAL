## Lesson 13.1: Standards, their types and standard costing

*What a standard cost is, the four types of standards, how quantity and price standards are set, controllable and favourable variances, and the advantages and criticism of standard costing.*

### Key idea

A standard cost is a planned cost, set in advance in quantities and prices, for the actual output. Standard costing compares actual cost with it, reports the variances and investigates the significant ones. The standard that controls cost best is one that good, efficient work can attain.

### Formats and formulas

ICAI's numerical questions open with a **standard cost card** (September 2024 Q4(a): "The standard cost card per pack is as follows"). *Reproduce the layout from memory.*

| Standard cost card: one tray of 50 frozen samosas | Quantity × price | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|---:|
| Direct material: dough and filling | 2.5 kg × ₹80 per kg | 200 |
| Direct labour | 0.25 hour × ₹160 per hour | 40 |
| Variable overhead | 0.25 hour × ₹40 per hour | 10 |
| Fixed overhead | 0.25 hour × ₹80 per hour | 20 |
| **Standard cost per tray** | | **270** |

**[formula] Standard cost of actual output and the variance**
Standard cost of actual output = standard cost per unit × actual output.

Cost variance = standard cost of actual output − actual cost.

Positive: F; negative: A. For 6,000 trays: 6,000 × ₹270 = ₹16,20,000, whatever the budget said.

**[formula] Reading a basic standard**
$$\frac{\text{Actual cost}}{\text{Basic cost}} \times 100$$
The current standard cost is expressed the same way; the two percentages are compared with each other and with earlier periods (SM 13.4).

### ICAI's glossary (SM 13.52–13.53)

The chapter summary's definitions, in its order:

| Term | ICAI's definition |
|---|---|
| Standard costing | A technique which uses standards for costs and revenues for the purposes of control through variance analysis |
| Standard price | A predetermined price fixed on the basis of a specification of a product or service and of all factors affecting that price |
| Standard time | The total time in which a task should be completed at standard performance |
| Variance | A divergence from the predetermined rates, expressed ultimately in money value, generally used in standard costing and budgetary control systems |
| Variance analysis | The analysis of variances arising in a standard costing system into their constituent parts |
| Revision variance | The difference between the original standard cost and the revised standard cost of actual production |
| Basic standard | A standard fixed for a fairly long period |
| Current standard | A standard fixed for a short period |
| Estimated cost | An estimate of what the cost is likely to be during a given period of time |
| Ideal cost | A cost which should be incurred during a period under ideal conditions |

### "Distinguish between controllable and uncontrollable variances"

| Point | Controllable | Uncontrollable |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Can be controlled under normal operating conditions if the responsibility centre takes preventive measures and acts prudently | Due to conditions beyond the responsibility centre's control; cannot be controlled even with all preventive measures |
| Responsibility | Answerable for adverse variances | Not answerable |
| Standard | Corrective action on performance | If significant and persistent, the standard may need revision |

### "Calculation of variances is not an end in itself, but a means to an end"

ICAI's MTP answer, in order: standard costing stresses what the cost should be; the variance may be favourable or unfavourable; knowing the figures is not enough, their origin and causes must be traced; a detailed probe, particularly into controllable variances, helps management ascertain (i) the amount of the variance, (ii) the factors or causes of its occurrence, (iii) the responsibility to be laid on executives and departments, and (iv) the corrective actions to obviate or reduce it; success depends on how quickly and effectively corrective action is taken; ==information is the means and the action taken on it is the end==.

### Advantages of standard costing (SM 13.49–13.51)

1. A basis for **measuring operating performance and cost control**: only exceptional variances are reported, a signal for prompt action.
2. It **aids price fixing**: stable standard costs are a basis for the selling price.
3. It facilitates **evaluation of jobs and introduction of incentives**.
4. It facilitates the **estimation of the cost of new products** with greater accuracy.
5. A basis for **inventory valuation**; material stock can be recorded in quantities only.
6. **Measurement of profit**: it eliminates variations in profit due to changes in stock values from one period to another.
7. Use in **planning, budgeting and decision making**.
8. **Standardisation of products, operations and processes**.
9. **Objectives and targets for each level of management**, defining managers' responsibilities.
10. A **uniform basis for comparison**; the standard hour compares dissimilar products.
11. **Maximum use of working capital, plant facilities and current assets**, as wastage and idle time are closely controlled.

### "Discuss the criticism of standard costing" (SM 13.51–13.52)

The Study Material answers each criticism but the last, so they do not invalidate the usefulness of the system (SM 13.51). Write both halves:

| Criticism | Answer |
|---|---|
| (i) Variation in price: prices are hard to estimate precisely | Forecasting covers fluctuations to some extent; uncontrollable variances are isolated and dealt with separately |
| (ii) Varying levels of output: standard costs not realised if the standard output is missed | True only in some jobbing industries; forecasting estimates output reasonably; prime cost is unaffected; variance analysis measures idle time |
| (iii) Changing standard of technology | Does not affect the system: standards are revised; cost reduction and control remain its cardinal feature |
| (iv) Attitude of technical people: used to physical standards, misled by standard costs | Educated through orientation courses: not insurmountable |
| (v) Mix of products: a pre-determined combination is presupposed | Standards are set for a short period and revised |
| (vi) Level of performance: too strict or too liberal (theoretical maximum, attainable good, average past performance) | Select a suitable type: attainable good performance |
| (vii) Cannot reflect the true value in exchange | True standards analyse economic and technical factors into quantities and prices reflecting optimum operations: realistic measures of the sacrifices involved |
| (viii) Fixation of standards may be costly: high skill needed, hard for small concerns | No answer given |

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the standard cost of the budgeted output** **Fix:** the variance is always against the standard cost of the **actual** output, 20,000 × ₹9.
- **Calling every adverse variance someone's fault** **Fix:** classify first; centres answer only for adverse variances they could have controlled; significant, persistent uncontrollable ones may mean revising the standard.
- **Basic standards producing variances** **Fix:** express actual and current costs as percentages of basic cost and compare; keep current standards for the variances.

### Recap

- Standard cost: "the planned unit cost of the product, component or service produced in a period"; set in quantities and prices.
- Standard costing: compare actual costs with pre-set standards; report variances; manage by exception.
- Process: set standards, ascertain actual costs, compare, investigate, dispose of variances (Costing P&L).
- Types: ideal (three criticisms), normal, basic or bogey (no variances; percentages of basic cost), current. Attainable good performance controls cost best.
- Material steps: standardisation, product study, specification list, test runs. Labour steps: product study, labour specification, methods, layout, time and motion study, training and trial.
- Price standards: the average price expected in the coming period, or the normal price over a cycle of seasons. Material price (small fluctuations): stock held and its price, orders placed, minimum support price, anticipated fluctuation.
- Controllable vs uncontrollable; F means actual cost below standard; significant, persistent uncontrollable variances: the standard may need revision. (The map app does not blame you for the flood.)

## Lesson 13.2: Material variances

*Why the material bill differs from the standard: price, usage, and usage split into mix and yield, with standard loss, purchases against consumption, and the checks ICAI expects.*

### Key idea

Compare the actual cost with the standard cost of the **actual output**, then explain the gap: price (what each kg cost) and usage (how many kg), with usage split into mix (the proportions) and yield (what the inputs produced). The parts must add back to the whole.

### Formats and formulas

Every material variance answer starts from a working table with the standard for actual output on one side and the actual on the other. *Reproduce the formulas and the table from memory.* A variance computed as standard minus actual is favourable when positive and adverse when negative; write F or A after every figure (SM 13.12).

**[formula] Material cost variance**
$$\text{MCV} = (SQ \times SP) - (AQ \times AP)$$
SQ = standard quantity for actual output, SP = standard price, AQ = actual quantity, AP = actual price. In words: standard cost of actual output − actual cost (SM 13.14).

**[formula] Material price variance**
$$\text{MPV} = AQ \times (SP - AP) \qquad = (SP \times AQ) - (AP \times AQ)$$
AQ here is the quantity **purchased**; if purchases are not given, take it as equal to the quantity consumed (SM 13.15). The Study Material calls the quantity used "also correct", and its own worked answers use it (Illustration 4, Practical Problem 6): when purchases and consumption differ, state the basis you use (Under the hood).

**[formula] Material usage variance**
$$\text{MUV} = SP \times (SQ - AQ) \qquad = (SQ \times SP) - (AQ \times SP)$$
AQ here is the quantity **used** (SM 13.16).

**[formula] Material mix variance**
$$\text{MMV} = SP \times (RSQ - AQ) \qquad = (RSQ \times SP) - (AQ \times SP)$$

**[formula] Material yield (sub-usage) variance**
$$\text{MYV} = SP \times (SQ - RSQ) \qquad = (SQ \times SP) - (RSQ \times SP)$$

**[formula] Standard quantity with a standard loss, and the revised standard quantity**
$$SQ = \text{Actual output} \times \frac{\text{Std input}}{\text{Std output}} \qquad RSQ_{\text{A}} = \text{Total AQ} \times \frac{SQ_{\text{A}}}{\text{Total SQ}}$$
Std input and std output: the standard batch's input and output.
Then split SQ by the standard mix: with a 15% standard loss, 1,700 kg of output needs 1,700 × 100 ÷ 85 = 2,000 kg of input (SM 13.23).

**[formula] The checks**
$$\text{MCV} = \text{MPV} + \text{MUV} \qquad \text{MUV} = \text{MMV} + \text{MYV}$$
The first holds when the quantity purchased equals the quantity consumed (SM 13.17).

### The working table

| Material | SQ × SP (₹) | RSQ × SP (₹) | AQ × SP (₹) | AQ × AP (₹) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A | xxx | xxx | xxx | xxx |
| B | xxx | xxx | xxx | xxx |
| **Total** | **xxx** | **xxx** | **xxx** | **xxx** |

Yield = column 1 − column 2; mix = column 2 − column 3; price = column 3 − column 4; usage = column 1 − column 3; cost = column 1 − column 4. Show the quantities (SQ, RSQ, AQ) and prices in a working note above it.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the standard quantity for the output Kesar planned** **Fix:** SQ is always the standard quantity for the **actual** output; the budget plays no part in material variances.
- **The revised standard quantity built from the standard total** **Fix:** RSQ = total AQ × standard proportion; its total must equal the total AQ.
- **The standard loss forgotten** **Fix:** standard input = actual output × standard input ÷ standard output (here × 100 ÷ 90 = 51,000 kg).
- **Price variance on the wrong quantity, without a word** **Fix:** purchases unless told otherwise (SM 13.15); state the basis, and give the consumption-basis figure in a note so that MPV + MUV reconciles to MCV.

### Recap

- MCV = (SQ × SP) − (AQ × AP): standard cost of **actual** output minus actual cost.
- MPV = AQ × (SP − AP), on the quantity **purchased** (consumed if purchases are not given); the purchase department's.
- MUV = SP × (SQ − AQ), on the quantity **used**; the production department's.
- MMV = SP × (RSQ − AQ); MYV (sub-usage) = SP × (SQ − RSQ); RSQ = actual total input in the standard proportion.
- Checks: MUV = MMV + MYV; MCV = MPV + MUV when purchases equal consumption.
- Standard loss: SQ = actual output × standard input ÷ standard output, then split by the standard mix.
- Ladder: SQ × SP → RSQ × SP → AQ × SP → AQ × AP gives yield, mix, price. (Kesar's sweeter recipe: mix favourable, yield adverse.)

## Lesson 13.3: Labour variances, including idle time

*Why the wage bill differs from the standard: rate on hours paid, idle time at the standard rate, efficiency on hours worked, and efficiency split into mix (gang) and yield.*

### Key idea

Compare the wages paid with the standard cost of the **actual output**, then explain the gap: rate on the hours **paid**, idle time on the hours paid but not worked, at the standard rate, and efficiency on the hours **worked**, split into mix and yield. The parts add back to the whole.

### Formats and formulas

*Reproduce the formulas and the five-rung ladder from memory.* SH = standard hours for actual output, SR = standard rate, AR = actual rate, RSH = revised standard hours. Hours **paid** and hours **worked** are different numbers whenever there is idle time: write the subscript every time.

**[formula] Labour cost variance**
$$\text{LCV} = (SH \times SR) - (AH_{\text{paid}} \times AR)$$
The standard labour cost minus the actual labour cost incurred for the production achieved (SM 13.25).

**[formula] Labour rate variance**
$$\text{LRV} = AH_{\text{paid}} \times (SR - AR)$$
The difference between the standard and actual rate per hour for the actual hours **paid** (SM 13.25).

**[formula] Labour idle time variance**
$$\text{ITV} = SR \times (AH_{\text{paid}} - AH_{\text{worked}}) \qquad = SR \times \text{Idle hours}$$
The difference between the actual hours paid and the actual hours worked, at standard rate (SM 13.27). Idle hours cannot be negative, so this variance is either nil or adverse.

**[formula] Labour efficiency variance**
$$\text{LEV} = SR \times (SH - AH_{\text{worked}})$$
The difference between the standard hours specified for actual production and the actual hours **worked**, at standard rate (SM 13.26). The Study Material's middle line of this formula marks AH with the asterisk it uses for hours paid, but its bracket form, its words and its footnote say hours worked, and Illustrations 6 and 7 use hours worked (SM 13.29–13.32): use hours worked.

**[formula] Labour mix (gang) variance**
$$\text{LMV} = SR \times (RSH - AH_{\text{worked}})$$

**[formula] Labour yield (sub-efficiency) variance**
$$\text{LYV} = SR \times (SH - RSH)$$
Also called the labour revised-efficiency variance (SM 13.26–13.27).

**[formula] Revised standard hours and the checks**
RSH of a grade = total hours worked × (that grade's SH ÷ total SH).
$$\text{LCV} = \text{LRV} + \text{ITV} + \text{LEV} \qquad \text{LEV} = \text{LMV} + \text{LYV}$$
With no idle time, LCV = LRV + LEV (SM 13.28).

| Term | Meaning (SM 13.37) |
|---|---|
| Standard hours (SH) | Hours required to produce the actual output |
| Actual hours (AH) | Actual hours taken to produce the actual output |
| Revised standard hours (RSH) | If actual labour hours worked were worked by standard mix (combination) of labour |
| Actual yield (AY) | Actual hours worked |
| Standard yield (SY) | Actual hours if labour worked in standard ratio |
| Standard labour cost (SLC) | Standard labour cost for actual output |

| Skilled | xxx | xxx | xxx | xxx |
| Unskilled | xxx | xxx | xxx | xxx |

- **Rate variance on hours worked** **Fix:** LRV = (SR − AR) × AH **paid**.
- **Idle time valued at the actual rate** **Fix:** ITV = idle hours × SR.
- **RSH from hours paid** **Fix:** RSH total = total hours worked.

### Recap

- LCV = (SH × SR) − (AH paid × AR): standard cost of the **actual** output minus the wages paid.
- LRV = AH **paid** × (SR − AR); the personnel manager negotiates rates.
- ITV = idle hours × SR = SR × (AH paid − AH worked): nil or adverse.
- LEV = SR × (SH − AH **worked**); the production manager or foreman answers for it.
- LMV (gang) = SR × (RSH − AH worked); LYV (sub-efficiency) = SR × (SH − RSH); RSH = hours worked in the standard ratio.
- Checks: LCV = LRV + ITV + LEV; LEV = LMV + LYV.
- Normal idle time is built into the standard; the idle time variance is for abnormal idle hours. (Cutting wages does not bring the electricity back.)

## Lesson 13.4: Variable overhead variances

*Why variable overheads differ from the standard for the output made: a rate paid per hour (expenditure) and hours used (efficiency), worked on actual hours worked.*

### Key idea

Compare the actual variable overheads with the standard variable overheads for the **actual output**, then split the gap: the rate per hour, on the hours actually worked (expenditure), and the hours worked against the standard hours, at the standard rate (efficiency). The two must add back to the cost variance.

### Formats and formulas

Every variable overhead answer starts with two working notes: the standard rate (per hour, and per unit if needed) and the standard hours for actual output. *Reproduce the formulas and the working notes from memory.* A variance computed as standard minus actual is favourable when positive and adverse when negative; write (F) or (A) after every figure (SM 13.12).

**[formula] Variable overhead cost variance**
$$\text{Cost} = (SH \times SR) - \text{Actual VOH}$$
SH = standard hours for actual output, SR = standard variable overhead rate per hour. In words: standard variable overheads for actual production − actual variable overheads (SM 13.36). With a rate per unit: actual output × standard rate per unit − actual variable overheads.

**[formula] Variable overhead expenditure (spending) variance**
$$\text{Expenditure} = (SR - AR) \times AH \qquad = (SR \times AH) - (AR \times AH)$$
AH = actual hours worked, AR = actual variable overhead rate per hour (actual variable overheads ÷ AH). In words: standard variable overheads for actual hours − actual variable overheads (SM 13.36).

**[formula] Variable overhead efficiency variance**
$$\text{Efficiency} = (SH - AH) \times SR \qquad = (SH \times SR) - (AH \times SR)$$
In words: standard variable overheads for production − standard variable overheads for actual hours (SM 13.36).

**[formula] The working notes and the check**
$$SR = \frac{BV}{BH} \qquad SH = \frac{BH}{BO} \times AO$$
BV = budgeted variable overheads, BH = budgeted hours, BO = budgeted output, AO = actual output. A rate per unit is BV ÷ BO (SM 13.42). Check: cost variance = expenditure variance + efficiency variance (SM 13.78).

The Study Material's summary names the three amounts **recovered overhead** (SH × SR), **standard overhead** (AH × SR) and **actual overhead**: cost = recovered − actual, expenditure = standard − actual, efficiency = recovered − standard (SM 13.54).

### The working table

| Particulars | Hours | Rate (₹) | Amount (₹) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Recovered: standard hours for actual output × SR | SH | SR | xxx |
| Standard: actual hours worked × SR | AH | SR | xxx |
| Actual variable overheads | AH | AR | xxx |

Efficiency = row 1 − row 2; expenditure = row 2 − row 3; cost = row 1 − row 3.

## Lesson 13.5: Fixed overhead variances

*Why absorbed fixed overheads differ from actual: spending against the budget (expenditure) and output against the budget (volume), with volume split into efficiency, capacity and calendar.*

### Key idea

Fixed overheads are absorbed on output at a rate fixed from the budget. The cost variance (absorbed − actual) splits into **expenditure** (budgeted − actual: spending) and **volume** (absorbed − budgeted: output), and volume splits into **efficiency**, **capacity** and **calendar**. A fixed overhead variance is an under- or over-absorption.

### Formats and formulas

*Reproduce the formulas and the working-note table from memory.* SH = standard hours for actual output, AH = actual hours, BH = budgeted hours, RBH = revised budgeted hours, SR = standard fixed overhead rate per hour, BF = budgeted fixed overheads, AF = actual fixed overheads, AD and BD = actual and budgeted working days. Each variance is the first amount minus the second: positive is (F), negative is (A).

**[formula] Fixed overhead cost variance**
$$\text{Cost} = (SH \times SR) - AF$$
Absorbed (recovered) fixed overheads − actual fixed overheads. Absorbed = SH × SR, or actual output × standard rate per unit (SM 13.41–13.42).

**[formula] Expenditure and volume variances**
$$\text{Expenditure} = BF - AF \qquad \text{Volume} = (SH \times SR) - BF$$
Budgeted − actual; absorbed − budgeted. BF = BH × SR (SM 13.41).

**[formula] Efficiency, capacity and calendar variances**
$$\text{Efficiency} = SR\,(SH - AH) \qquad \text{Capacity} = SR\,(AH - BH)$$
$$\text{Calendar} = SR_{\text{day}} \times (AD - BD)$$
SR per day = standard fixed overhead rate per day. With a calendar variance, use the revised capacity variance = SR (AH − RBH) (SM 13.41).

**[formula] Rates and revised budgeted hours**
$$SR = \frac{BF}{BH} \qquad RBH = \frac{BH}{BD} \times AD$$
Rate per unit = BF ÷ budgeted output; rate per day = BF ÷ BD. Revised budgeted fixed overheads (the Study Material also calls them "possible fixed overheads") = RBH × SR = BF ÷ BD × AD (SM 13.42, 13.76, 13.87).

**[formula] Verification**
$$\text{Cost} = \text{Expenditure} + \text{Volume}$$
And volume = efficiency + capacity + calendar (SM 13.42).

The summary in the Study Material uses the names **recovered**, **standard**, **budgeted** and **actual** overhead: cost = recovered − actual, expenditure = budgeted − actual, volume = recovered − budgeted, efficiency = recovered − standard, capacity = standard − budgeted (SM 13.54).

|---|---:|---:|
| Fixed overheads (₹) | xxx | xxx |
| Working days | xxx | xxx |
| Hours | xxx | xxx |
| Output (units) | xxx | xxx |

Then: standard hours for actual output, standard rate per hour, per unit and per day, and the revised budgeted hours.

### Answer frame

### "Discuss the various types of fixed overhead variances" (theory)

1. **Why they arise**: the recovery of fixed overheads depends on capacity utilisation; producing less than the projected level leaves part of the budgeted fixed overheads unrecovered (SM 13.38).
1. **Fixed overhead cost variance**: the difference between actual fixed overhead and absorbed fixed overhead; overhead variance is an under- or over-absorption.
1. **Expenditure variance**: actual fixed overhead incurred against budgeted fixed overhead.
1. **Volume variance**: arises due to the volume of production; absorbed against budgeted. It shows the cost of failing to produce at the planned activity, or the benefit of producing above it (SM 13.39).
1. Volume splits into **efficiency** (absorbed against standard fixed overhead), **capacity** (standard fixed overhead against budgeted) and **calendar** (actual against standard working days).
1. When the calendar variance is computed, the capacity variance is revised to (actual hours − revised budgeted hours) × standard rate.
1. Verification: cost = expenditure + volume; volume = efficiency + capacity + calendar.
1. The formula chart, or a tree.

## Lesson 13.6: Reconciling budgeted and actual profit, and accounting for variances

*How every variance adds up to the gap between standard and actual cost, and between budgeted and actual profit; and where the variances go in the books.*

### Key idea

A reconciliation explains the whole gap, budget to actual or standard to actual, as a list of variances, each measured with everything else at standard; they must add to the gap. Variances are then disposed of, normally to the Costing Profit and Loss Account, because stocks are carried at standard cost.

### Formats and formulas

*Reproduce these layouts from memory.* Each variance is written with (F) or (A); in a profit bridge, (F) is added and (A) deducted.

**[formula] The reconciliation identities**
$$AC = SC - NV$$
AC = actual cost; SC = standard cost of actual output; NV = net cost variance = MCV + LCV + VOCV + FOCV, the material, labour, variable overhead and fixed overhead cost variances, with (F) as plus and (A) as minus (SM 13.13).

**[formula] The two revenue steps**
$$\text{Volume} = (AU - BU) \times c \qquad \text{Price} = (AP - SP) \times AU$$
AU, BU = actual and budgeted units sold; c = standard contribution per unit; AP, SP = actual and standard selling price. Volume is the master budget profit against the flexible budget profit.

**[definition] Controllable and uncontrollable variances**
**Controllable variances** are those which can be controlled under the normal operating conditions if a responsibility centre takes preventive measures and acts prudently. **Uncontrollable variances** are those which occur due to conditions which are beyond the control of a responsibility centre and cannot be controlled even though all preventive measures are in place. (SM 13.11–13.12)

**[definition] Revision variance**
The difference between the original standard cost and the revised standard cost of actual production. (SM 13.53)

### Answer frame

### "Calculation of variances is not an end in itself, but a means to an end. Discuss."

The points ICAI's suggested answers give (MTP January 2025 Series II Q2(b); MTP September 2025 Series I Q6(c); MTP May 2026 Series II Q4(b)):

1. The crux of standard costing lies in variance analysis: standard costs are predetermined and compared with actual costs; the difference between standard cost and actual cost of actual output is the variance.
2. A variance is favourable when actual cost is less than standard, unfavourable (adverse) when more.
3. The figures alone do not give control: their origin and causes must be traced to take remedial steps.
4. A detailed probe, particularly into **controllable** variances, helps management ascertain (i) the amount of the variance, (ii) the causes of its occurrence, (iii) the responsibility of executives and departments, (iv) the corrective action to reduce or remove it.
5. Success depends on how quickly and effectively corrective action is taken: the variance is information, the means; action on it is the end.
