## Lesson 4.1: Cost of debt and of preference capital

*What a loan, a debenture or a preference share really costs: net proceeds, after-tax interest, the approximation formula, YTM, convertibles and preference capital.*

### Key idea

The cost of debt is the rate at which the net proceeds received today equal the present value of the after-tax interest and the redemption value. Interest is tax-deductible, so Kd is an after-tax cost; preference dividend is not, so Kp gets no tax adjustment.

### Formats and formulas

*Reproduce each formula, its symbols and the YTM layout from memory.*

**[formula] Irredeemable debentures (SM 4.7)**
$$K_d = \frac{I\,(1-t)}{NP}$$
Kd = cost of debt after tax; I = annual interest payment; NP = net proceeds of debentures or current market price; t = tax rate.

**[formula] Redeemable debentures: Formula 1, only interest tax-deductible (SM 4.8)**
$$K_d = \frac{I\,(1-t) + \dfrac{RV - NP}{n}}{\dfrac{RV + NP}{2}}$$
RV = redemption value; n = remaining life of the debentures.

**[formula] Redeemable debentures: Formula 2, discount and premium also tax-deductible (SM 4.9)**
$$K_d = \frac{\left(I + \dfrac{RV - NP}{n}\right)(1-t)}{\dfrac{RV + NP}{2}}$$

**[formula] YTM by interpolation (SM 4.12)**
$$K_d = L + \frac{NPV_L}{NPV_L - NPV_H} \times (H - L)$$
L and H = the lower and higher trial rates; $NPV_L$ and $NPV_H$ = the NPVs of the cash flows at those rates.

**[formula] Zero coupon and deep discount bonds (SM 4.12)**
$$RV = NP \times (1 + K_d)^{n}$$
No interest is paid, so the whole return is the gap between issue price and redemption value.

**[formula] Value of an amortised bond (SM 4.13)**
$$V_B = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{C_t}{(1+K_d)^{t}}$$
$C_t$ = principal repaid in year t + interest on the amount outstanding; the cost of such a bond is the rate that equates these uneven flows with its net proceeds.

**[formula] Convertible debentures: redemption value (SM 4.15)**
$$RV = \text{higher of} \qquad \big(\text{cash value},\ N \times P_0 (1+g)^{n}\big)$$
N = shares per debenture; $P_0$ = current share price; g = its expected growth rate; n = years to maturity. Then apply Formula 1 or the YTM.

**[formula] Preference shares (SM 4.17–4.18)**
$$K_p = \frac{PD}{P_0} \qquad K_p = \frac{PD + \dfrac{RV - NP}{n}}{\dfrac{RV + NP}{2}}$$
PD = annual preference dividend; $P_0$ or NP = net proceeds from the issue. The first is for irredeemable shares, the second for redeemable ones.

**[definition] Flotation cost**
The new issue of a security (debt or equity) involves some expenditure in the form of underwriting or brokerage fees, legal and administrative charges, registration fees, printing expenses etc. The sum of all these costs is known as floatation cost. (SM 4.7)

**[definition] Net proceeds**
Issue price less issue expenses or floatation cost. If the issue price is not given, it may be assumed equal to the current market price; if issue expenses are not given, they are taken as zero. (SM 4.7)

**[definition] Yield to maturity (YTM)**
The annual return of an investment from the current date till maturity date: the internal rate of return at which the current price of a debt equals the present value of all cash flows. (SM 4.11)

### The YTM working, ICAI's layout

| Year | Cash flow | PV at L (₹) | PV at H (₹) |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| 0 | NP (new issue) or P0 (existing debt) | (NP) × 1.000 | (NP) × 1.000 |
| 1 to n | Interest net of tax, I(1 − t) | × PVAF (L, n) | × PVAF (H, n) |
| n | Redemption value, RV | × PVF (L, n) | × PVF (H, n) |
| | **NPV** | **+ (NPV at L)** | **− (NPV at H)** |

ICAI prints each discount factor in its own column (six columns in all); write it that way on paper. Then interpolate.

**[trick] When NP equals RV**
If a debt is issued at par (net of costs) and redeemed at par, (RV − NP) is zero and both the approximation and the YTM give exactly I(1 − t) ÷ NP: the term-loan case. Condition: interest paid annually on the face value.

### Answer frame

**EXPLAIN the YTM approach of calculating cost of debt** (SM 4.11–4.12), in this order:

1. Kd is found by discounting the relevant cash flows using the internal rate of return; YTM is the annual return from the current date till maturity, the IRR at which the current price equals the present value of all cash flows.
2. Cash flows: year 0, net proceeds (new issue) or current market price (existing debt); years 1 to n, interest net of tax, I(1 − t); year n, redemption value.
3. Steps: identify the cash flows; calculate NPVs at two discount rates; calculate the IRR by interpolation.
4. It is superior to the approximation method and preferred in finance; the wider the gap between the two rates, the lower the accuracy.

**DISCUSS amortisation of bond** (SM 4.13): principal repaid every year rather than at maturity; interest on the outstanding amount, so the cash flows are uneven; value = the sum of each year's interest plus principal discounted at Kd; a two-line example.

**EXPLAIN any four features of debentures or bonds** (4 marks in MTP Series II for May 2025; SM 4.5–4.6). One heading and two lines each, in ICAI's order:

1. **Face value**: the denominated value of the debenture; interest is calculated on it (9% debentures of ₹100: interest @ 9% on ₹100).
2. **Interest (coupon) rate**: a fixed rate on the face value (except zero coupon and deep discount bonds), paid periodically (annually, semi-annually, etc.).
3. **Maturity period**: a fixed period for redemption; for irredeemable debentures it is not defined and is taken as infinite.
4. **Redemption value**: determined by the debt covenants and paid on the maturity date; it may vary from the face value.
5. **Benefit of tax shield**: interest is allowed as an expense for corporate tax, so it saves tax; the saving is the tax shield.

Write four of the five; ICAI's answer gave all five.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the preference dividend that got a tax refund** **Fix:** preference dividend is an appropriation of after-tax profit and saves no tax; only interest gets the (1 − t).
- **The face value in the denominator** **Fix:** divide by the net proceeds, or the current market price for existing debt: 6.75 ÷ 90 = 7.50%.
- **The convertible that forgot to grow** **Fix:** grow the price to maturity, ₹24 × (1.06)⁶, multiply by the shares per debenture, then take the higher of that and the cash value.
- **Interest on the wrong base, life from the wrong date** Interest is the coupon on the **face value**, not on the issue price or NP; n is the **remaining** life on the date of costing, not the original tenure.

### Recap

- Cost of capital: the return expected by the providers of capital; cost is the market's expectation, not the coupon (SM 4.2–4.3).
- NP = issue price − flotation cost; no issue price, use the market price; existing debt, the market price.
- Irredeemable: Kd = I(1 − t) ÷ NP; Kp = PD ÷ NP.
- Redeemable, approximation: [I(1 − t) + (RV − NP) ÷ n] ÷ [(RV + NP) ÷ 2]; if the discount and premium are tax-deductible, [I + (RV − NP) ÷ n](1 − t) ÷ average.
- YTM: year 0 NP, years 1 to n I(1 − t), year n RV; NPVs at two rates; interpolate. Superior to the approximation.
- Convertible: RV = higher of cash and shares × P₀(1 + g)ⁿ.
- No tax shield on a preference dividend, or in a tax holiday. (The taxman only chips in on interest.)

## Lesson 4.2: Cost of equity and of retained earnings

*Why equity and retained earnings are not free: the five ICAI approaches to the cost of equity (dividend price, earnings price, growth, realised yield, CAPM) and the cost of retained earnings.*

### Key idea

Equity has a cost even though no payment is fixed: it is the return shareholders expect, read off the share price (dividend or earnings approaches, Gordon's model, realised yield) or built from risk (CAPM). Retained earnings cost the shareholders' forgone return, normally a little below Ke because there is no flotation cost.

### Formats and formulas

*Reproduce each formula and what its symbols mean from memory.*

**[formula] Dividend price approach (SM 4.20)**
$$K_e = \frac{D}{P_0}$$
D = expected dividend (also written D1); $P_0$ = market price of equity (ex-dividend).

**[formula] Earnings price approach (SM 4.21)**
$$K_e = \frac{E}{P}$$
E = current earnings per share; P = market price per share.

**[formula] Growth approach, Gordon's model (SM 4.21)**
$$K_e = \frac{D_1}{P_0} + g \qquad K_e = \frac{D_1}{P_0 - F} + g$$
$D_1 = D_0(1+g)$, the next expected dividend; $P_0$ = current market price per share; g = constant growth rate of dividend; F = flotation cost per share (second form, for newly issued shares).

**[formula] Growth rate (SM 4.22–4.23)**
$$g = \sqrt[n]{\frac{D_0}{D_n}} - 1 \qquad g = b \times r$$
$D_0$ = current dividend; $D_n$ = dividend n years ago; b = earnings retention rate; r = rate of return on funds invested.

**[formula] Realised yield, year-by-year data (SM 4.25)**
$$1 + Y_t = \frac{D_t + P_t}{P_{t-1}} \qquad K_e = \big[(1+Y_1)(1+Y_2)\cdots(1+Y_n)\big]^{1/n} - 1$$
Each year's yield is dividend plus closing price over opening price; Ke is their geometric mean. With a purchase price, a series of dividends and a sale price instead, Ke is the IRR of those flows.

**[formula] CAPM (SM 4.26–4.27)**
$$K_e = R_f + \beta\,(R_m - R_f)$$
$R_f$ = risk-free rate of return; β = beta coefficient; $R_m$ = rate of return on market portfolio; $(R_m - R_f)$ = market risk premium.

**[formula] Cost of retained earnings (SM 4.29)**
$$K_r = \frac{D}{P} \qquad K_r = \frac{EPS}{P} \qquad K_r = \frac{D_1}{P_0} + g$$
P = current market price (sometimes the issue price); no flotation cost.

**[definition] Systematic and unsystematic risk**
Unsystematic risk: company-specific risk related to the company's performance, which can be reduced or eliminated by diversification (diversifiable risk). Systematic risk: the macro-economic or market-specific risk under which a company operates, which cannot be eliminated by diversification (non-diversifiable risk). (SM 4.26)

**[definition] Cost of retained earnings**
The opportunity cost of dividends foregone by shareholders: the expected rate of return shareholders could have obtained by investing in financial assets. (SM 4.28)

**[exam] The traps ICAI used**

**[trick] Gordon's two pieces**
Ke = dividend yield + growth: 12.96 ÷ 240 = 5.4% yield plus 8% growth = 13.4%. If an MCQ's options include the bare yield (5.4%), it is the "forgot g" distractor. Condition: constant growth forever, with g below Ke.

### Answer frame

**DISCUSS the dividend price approach and the earnings price approach** (2 marks; SM 4.20–4.21):

1. Dividend price approach (dividend valuation model): assumes the dividend per share remains constant forever; cost of equity = expected dividend ÷ market price per share (ex-dividend); Ke = D1 ÷ P0, symbols defined.
2. Earnings price approach: co-relates the earnings of the company with the market price of its share; each investor expects a certain earning, distributed or not; Ke = E ÷ P (EPS ÷ market price); assumes EPS constant forever.
3. The difference: the earnings price approach seeks to nullify the effect of changes in the dividend policy.

**EXPLAIN any four methods of computing the cost of equity**: RTP January 2025's answer opens with one line, "the rate of return which equates the present value of expected dividends with the market share price", then gives a heading, one or two lines and the formula for each method. Choose four of the dividend price, earnings price, growth (Gordon) and realised yield approaches and CAPM, and add when each is used (SM 4.19).

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: last year's dividend in Gordon's numerator** **Fix:** D1 = D0 × (1 + g) = ₹12.96, and Ke = 13.40%.
- **Flotation cost charged to retained earnings** **Fix:** retained earnings involve no issue, so no flotation: use the current market price. Flotation belongs only to the new-issue Ke.
- **The cum-dividend price used as P0** **Fix:** P0 is ex-dividend: subtract the dividend payable from the cum-dividend price first.
- **Beta times the market return** **Fix:** beta multiplies the premium, Rm − Rf: 7% + 0.9 × 6% = 12.40%. Read the question: "market risk premium" or "equity risk premium" is already Rm − Rf; "market return" is Rm.

### Recap

- Ke = what equity shareholders expect; found as a balancing figure from the price, or from risk (CAPM).
- Dividend price: D ÷ P0; earnings price: EPS ÷ P (nullifies dividend policy); both assume constancy forever.
- Gordon: Ke = D1 ÷ P0 + g; new issue D1 ÷ (P0 − F) + g; D1 = D0(1 + g).
- g = ⁿ√(D0 ÷ Dn) − 1 (or the FVIF table), or g = b × r.
- Realised yield: the IRR of purchase price, dividends and sale price, or the geometric mean of yearly yields.
- CAPM: Ke = Rf + β(Rm − Rf); beta prices systematic risk only; usable even when the entity is suffering losses.
- Kr: same formulas at the market price, no flotation; normally Kr < Ke. (Free money has the longest invoice.)

## Lesson 4.3: WACC and the marginal cost of capital

*Why a company's hurdle rate is the weighted average of its sources' costs, how book and market value weights change it, and how the marginal cost of capital rises as new funds get dearer.*

### Key idea

WACC is the after-tax cost of each source weighted by its proportion of total capital, and it is the minimum return at which the company creates value. Market value weights reflect today's prices; marginal weights are the proportions of new funds, and the marginal cost rises at each break point where a cheaper source runs out.

### Formats and formulas

*Reproduce this layout and the formulas from memory; ICAI's Suggested Answers set out every WACC this way.*

| Source of capital | Amount (₹) | Weight (a) | After-tax cost (b) | (a) × (b) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Equity share capital | xxx | 0.xxxx | Ke | xxx |
| Retained earnings | xxx | 0.xxxx | Kr | xxx |
| Preference share capital | xxx | 0.xxxx | Kp | xxx |
| Debentures / long-term debt | xxx | 0.xxxx | Kd | xxx |
| **Total** | **xxx** | **1.0000** | | **WACC** |

Book value weights take the balance sheet amounts. Market value weights take number × market price for each security, with the market value of equity split between share capital and retained earnings in their book value ratio. Marginal weights take the proportions of the new funds.

**[formula] WACC**
$$K_o = W_e K_e + W_r K_r + W_p K_p + W_d K_d$$
$K_o$ = WACC (overall cost of capital); each W = that source's amount ÷ total capital, so the weights add up to 1; each K = that source's after-tax cost.

**[formula] Market value of equity split (SM 4.33)**
$$MV_{EC} = MV_{E} \times \frac{BV_{EC}}{BV_{EC} + BV_{RE}}$$
$MV_E$ = market value of equity shares (number × price); $BV_{EC}$, $BV_{RE}$ = book values of paid-up equity capital and retained earnings. The rest of $MV_E$ is the market value of retained earnings.

**[formula] Marginal cost of capital and break point (SM 4.38; Illustration 18, SM 4.39–4.41)**
$$MCC = \sum (\text{proportion of new funds} \times \text{marginal cost of the source}) \qquad \text{Break point} = \frac{\text{cheaper source available}}{\text{its proportion}}$$
Below the first break point, retained earnings and the cheapest debt are used; above it, the dearer component cost replaces the cheaper one.

**[definition] Weighted average cost of capital**
The weighted average after-tax costs of the individual components of the firm's capital structure: the after-tax cost of each debt and equity is calculated separately and added together to a single overall cost of capital. (SM 4.31)

**[definition] Marginal cost of capital**
The cost of raising an additional rupee of capital; since capital is raised in substantial amount in practice, it is the cost incurred in raising new funds. It is derived when the average cost of capital is calculated using the marginal weights, which represent the proportion of funds the firm intends to employ. (SM 4.38)

**[trick] Sanity checks on any WACC**
The weights must add up to 1, and the WACC must lie between the cheapest and the dearest after-tax cost. Market value weights raise the WACC when equity (the dearest source) trades further above its book value than the other sources do. Conditions: all costs after tax and every source included once.

### Answer frame

**DISCUSS the meaning of weighted average cost of capital. ILLUSTRATE with an example** (SM 4.31–4.32):

1. A company mixes sources to balance financial risk, ownership control and cost, so the cost of total capital is the weighted average of the costs of the individual sources.
2. Definition: the weighted average after-tax costs of the individual components of the firm's capital structure; also called the overall cost of capital; it depends on the capital structure.
3. The four steps (total capital; proportions; proportion × cost; add).
4. A four-line example table (ICAI's: retained earnings 25% × 10%, equity 10% × 11%, preference 15% × 9%, debt 50% × 6% = 7.95%).
5. Use: the discount rate for DCF and NPV, the hurdle rate; returns above WACC create value.

**What is the DIFFERENCE between book value and market value weights?** The five-point table above, in ICAI's order, with one line on retained earnings (included in book value of equity; under market values, apportioned out of the market value of equity).

**DISCUSS marginal cost of capital** (SM 4.38): definition; marginal weights = proportions of new funds, so no BV/MV choice; equal to the average cost if proportions and component costs are unchanged; component costs rise beyond certain levels (debt example, external equity with flotation costs); then the average cost rises and the marginal cost rises faster.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the cheapest source as the hurdle rate** **Fix:** the hurdle rate is the WACC of the mix that funds the project, not the cost of the source used this time.
- **Market value weights with retained earnings at book** **Fix:** split the market value of equity in the book value ratio of equity capital to retained earnings.
- **Pre-tax interest in the WACC** **Fix:** every cost in a WACC is after tax: Kd = I(1 − t) or its redeemable form; preference and equity costs need no adjustment.
- **The break point that forgot the proportion** **Fix:** retained earnings fund only the equity part (60%) of each rupee: the break point is ₹72 lakh ÷ 0.60 = ₹120 lakh of total new funds.

### Recap

- Cost of capital: the return expected by providers of capital; also called the cut-off rate, hurdle rate, minimum rate of return; used for investment evaluation, financing decisions and credit policy.
- Five factors: supply and demand, investor preferences, risk and return, inflation, exchange rates.
- WACC = Σ weight × after-tax cost; weights add up to 1; it is the minimum return that creates value.
- BV weights: balance sheet figures, reserves inside equity. MV weights: number × market price; split the market value of equity between share capital and retained earnings in their book value ratio.
- MCC: the cost of an additional rupee, with marginal weights (proportions of new funds); no BV/MV choice.
- Break point = cheaper source available ÷ its proportion; beyond it, the MCC steps up, faster than the average.
- Each tranche keeps its own cost; after new debt, recompute Ke at the new price. (The blend costs more once the Assam runs out.)
