## Lesson 3.1: Procurement procedure, GeM, ERP and stock verification

*How material travels from the bill of materials to the store shelf and out again: every document and who prepares it, GeM, ERP, store records and surprise stock checks.*

### Key idea

No material moves without the right document from the right department: bill of materials, material requisition, purchase requisition, purchase order, goods received note, then the bill passed against them. Someone independent checks the records against the shelves, without prior notice.

### Formats and formulas

Asked as "distinguish" questions. *Reproduce the points, in this order, from memory.*

**[compare] Bill of materials vs material requisition note (SM 2.8)**
| Bill of materials | Material requisition note |
|---|---|
| Prepared by the engineering or planning department. | Prepared by the production or other consuming department. |
| A complete schedule of component parts and raw materials required for a particular job or work order. | A document asking the store keeper to issue materials to the consuming department. |
| Often serves as a material requisition: it can replace material requisitions. | Cannot replace a bill of materials. |
| Can be used for the purpose of quotations. | Useful in arriving at historical cost only. |
| Helps in keeping a quantitative control on materials drawn through material requisitions. | Shows the material actually drawn from stores. |

**[compare] Bin card vs stores ledger (SM 2.21)**
| Bin card | Stores ledger |
|---|---|
| Maintained by the store keeper in the store. | Maintained in the cost accounting department. |
| Contains only quantitative details of material received, issued and returned to stores. | Contains information both in quantity and value. |
| Entries are made when the transaction takes place. | Always posted after the transaction. |
| Each transaction is individually posted. | Transactions may be summarised and then posted. |
| Inter-department transfers do not appear. | Material transfers from one job to another are recorded for costing purposes. |

| Document | Usually prepared by | Copies |
|---|---|---|
| Purchase requisition | Store keeper (regular); departmental head (special) | — |
| Purchase order | Purchase manager or concerned officer | Store or indenting dept, receiving dept, cost accounting dept; one on file with the requisitions |
| Goods received note | Receiving department | Quadruplicate: purchase, store or indenting, receiving, accounting |
| Material transfer note | Not named by ICAI; made at the time of transfer | Duplicate: cost accounting dept, department making the transfer |
| Shop credit note | Department returning the surplus | Triplicate: store room, cost accounting dept, department returning it |

**[definition] Stores ledger**
A ledger containing a separate account for each item of material and component stocked in store giving details of the receipts, issues and balance both in terms of quantity and value. A subsidiary ledger to the main cost ledger, posted from goods received notes, material requisition notes etc. (SM 2.20, 2.73)

**[formula] Consumption of materials in the financial accounts**
$$\text{Consumption} = \text{Opening stock} + \text{Purchases} \qquad - \text{Closing stock}$$
Stock is taken at cost or net realisable value, whichever is less (SM 2.72).

**[exam] The traps**
- The store keeper prepares the **purchase** requisition (regular items), never the material requisition.
- Bin card: quantity, store keeper. Stores ledger: quantity and value, cost department.
- Two returns, two papers: to the **supplier**, a material returned note; back to the **store**, a shop credit note (which ICAI's summary also calls a material return note). Name the direction.
- The invoice: quantity against the goods received note, price against the purchase order.

**[trick] Plan, Pull, Push, Promise, Proof**
The first five documents in order: **Plan** (bill of materials), **Pull** from the store (material requisition), **Push** to the buyer (purchase requisition), **Promise** to the supplier (purchase order), **Proof** of receipt (goods received note). Holds for a regular item bought for stock; for a special item the purchase requisition comes from the departmental head.

### Answer frame

### "Distinguish" and "uses" questions

For "distinguish", use the two tables in *Formats and formulas*: five points each, starting with who prepares or maintains it. For "uses of the bill of materials" (SM 2.7), open with the definition, then:

| Department | Use |
|---|---|
| Marketing (purchase) | Materials are procured on the basis of the specifications mentioned in it. |
| Production | Production is planned according to the nature and volume of materials required; material requisition lists are prepared accordingly. |

| Cost / accounting | Used to estimate cost and profit; purchase, issue and usage are compared and verified against it. |

### "Write a note on continuous stock verification"

1. Definition: physical verification at appropriate intervals, without prior notice, by staff independent of the store and production; the element of surprise is essential.
2. The shortcoming of annual stock-taking: shutdown, or a count of limited character.
3. The five advantages, in ICAI's order (Example 2).

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the store keeper who wrote his own permission slip** **Fix:** the consuming department prepares the material requisition; the store keeper issues against it.
- **The return that got the wrong paper** **Fix:** to the supplier, a material returned note, with a books adjustment only if the goods were already in the receiving report; to the store, a shop credit note (stores debit note) in triplicate, made out by the returning department. ICAI's summary calls the note for returns to the store a "material return note", so always name the direction.
- **The surprise check that was announced** **Fix:** continuous verification is by independent staff, at intervals, without notice.

### Recap

- Material control: systematic control over procurement, storage and usage to keep an even flow of material.
- A supplier's invoice is passed after checking quantity against the goods received note and price against the purchase order.
- Bin card: quantity only, kept by the store keeper. Stores ledger: quantity and value, kept by the cost department.
- Documents in order: bill of materials (planning) → material requisition (production) → purchase requisition (store keeper, or departmental head for special items) → RFP / NIT → comparative statement → purchase order → goods received note or material returned note → bill passed.
- GeM: e-market for government buyers; e-bidding, reverse e-auction, demand aggregation; mandatory for government users; L1 bidder selected.
- Copies: GRN quadruplicate; material transfer note duplicate (no store copy); shop credit note triplicate.
- ERP: one entry for all departments; e-copies instead of multiple copies.
- Continuous stock verification: independent staff, intervals, no prior notice; annual counts mean shutdown or a limited count. (Cardamom does not adjust.)

## Lesson 3.2: Valuing material receipts

*What one unit of purchased material really costs: which invoice items are added, deducted or kept out, and how normal and abnormal shortages change the rate.*

### Key idea

Material cost = purchase price net of trade discounts, rebates, subsidies and recoverable taxes + the costs of bringing it to its present location and condition. Cash discount, demurrage, fines and penalties stay out. A normal shortage raises the cost of each good unit; an abnormal one goes to the Costing Profit and Loss Account.

### Formats and formulas

### ICAI's table of treatments (SM 2.13–2.15)

*Learn the groups and the order: "state the treatment of" questions use exactly these words.*

| Item | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Discounts and subsidy** | |
| (i) Trade discount | Deducted from the purchase price if not shown as deduction in the invoice. |
| (ii) Quantity discount | Like trade discount: deducted if not shown as deduction. |
| (iii) Cash discount | Not deducted; treated as interest and finance item. Ignored. |
| (iv) Subsidy / grant / incentives | Deducted from the cost of purchase. |
| **Duties and taxes** | |
| (v) Road tax / toll tax | Included in the cost of purchase if paid by the buyer. |
| (vi) GST | Excluded if credit is available; unless mentioned specifically, not part of cost. |
| (vii) Customs duty | Added to the purchase cost. |
| **Penalty and charges** | |
| (viii) Demurrage | Penalty imposed by the transporter for delay in loading or unloading: abnormal cost, not included. |
| (ix) Detention charges / fine | Imposed by a statutory authority for non-compliance: abnormal cost, not included. |
| (x) Penalty | Of any type, not included. |
| **Other expenditures** | |
| (xi) Insurance charges | Paid for protecting goods in transit: added. |
| (xii) Commission or brokerage | Added. |
| (xiii) Freight inwards | Added: directly attributable to procurement. |
| (xiv) Cost of containers | Non-returnable: added. Returnable and refunded: not considered. Refund less than amount paid: only the shortfall added. |
| (xv) Shortage | Normal: absorbed by good units. Abnormal: not absorbed, debited to Costing P&L. |

Notes below the statement explain each item left out (cash discount, demurrage, GST with credit).

**[formula] Cost per unit of material received**
$$\text{Rate} = \frac{\text{Total cost of material}}{\text{Good units}}$$
Good units = units purchased − normal shortage. An abnormal shortage is valued at this rate and charged to the Costing Profit and Loss Account.

**[formula] "Other expenses at x% of total cost"**
$$\text{OE} = C \times \frac{x}{100 - x}$$
OE = other expenses; C = the cost before them. The total cost includes the other expenses themselves, so 2% of total cost is 2/98 of the cost before them (ICAI's convention in MTP May 2024 and RTP September 2024).

**[exam] The traps**
- The cash discount was received: still ignored. The statement uses the price after trade discount, never after cash discount.
- GST is worked out on the price after trade discount (18% of ₹2,70,000, not ₹3,00,000).
- "Other expenses @ 2% of total cost" means 2/98 of the cost before them.
- A normal shortage is not deducted from cost: only the units fall.
- Read who the buyer is: "unregistered" decides the GST.

**[trick] Price, Path, Penalty**
**Price** net of trade discounts and subsidies: in. Every cost of the **path** to the store (freight, insurance, duty, toll, commission, containers kept): in. Every **penalty** for a mistake (demurrage, fines, detention) and every finance reward (cash discount): out. Holds for any purchase; taxes are the one exception to check, in only when their credit is not available.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the cash discount that went shopping** **Fix:** ignore it in material cost, whether or not it was received.
- **The demurrage that sneaked into stock** **Fix:** a penalty for delay or non-compliance is an abnormal cost; it never enters the cost of material.
- **The shortage that was taken out twice** **Fix:** keep the total cost and divide by the good units; the shortage's cost is spread over them.

### Recap

- Material cost: purchase price net of trade discounts, rebates and recoverable taxes, plus costs of bringing it to its present location and condition.
- Added: freight inwards, transit insurance, customs duty, toll tax paid by buyer, commission, non-returnable containers.
- Kept out: cash discount (finance item), demurrage, detention charges and fines, penalties.
- Deducted: trade and quantity discounts (if not already on the invoice), subsidies, grants and incentives.
- GST: out if credit is available (and when the question is silent); in for a buyer who cannot take credit.
- Returnable containers: only the shortfall of the refund is added.
- Rate = total cost ÷ (units − normal shortage); abnormal shortage at that rate to Costing P&L.
- "x% of total cost" = cost before it × x/(100 − x). (The forklift pays for the wedding.)

## Lesson 3.3: Pricing material issues: FIFO, LIFO and average methods

*The same kilos bought at different prices: how FIFO, LIFO and the average methods price each issue and the closing stock, and how returns, transfers and shortages enter the stores ledger.*

### Key idea

A pricing method decides which purchase price each issue carries; the total of issues and closing stock is always the same. In rising prices, FIFO gives the lowest cost of issues and the highest closing stock, LIFO the reverse, and the weighted average falls in between.

### Formats and formulas

### The stores ledger

ICAI's layout: Date | Receipts (GRN or return note no., Qty, Rate, Amount) | Issues (requisition no., Qty, Rate, Amount) | Balance (Qty, Rate, Amount). *Rule these columns from memory; in this lesson each group is written as "qty @ rate = amount" so it reads on a phone.*

| Date | Receipts | Issues | Balance |
|---|---|---|---|
| | GRN no., qty @ rate = amount | MR no., qty @ rate = amount | qty @ rate = amount |

**[formula] Weighted average price**
$$\text{Rate} = \frac{\text{Total cost in stock}}{\text{Total units in stock}}$$
Recompute after every receipt (SM 2.61, 2.75).

**[formula] Simple average price**
$$\text{Rate} = \frac{\text{Sum of the lot prices}}{\text{Number of lots}}$$
ICAI: total of unit price of each purchase ÷ total number of purchases. Lots already fully issued are left out of the average (SM 2.59, 2.75).

**[compare] FIFO, LIFO and weighted average when prices are rising**
| Point | FIFO | LIFO | Weighted average |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost of material issued | Lowest (old prices) | Highest (latest prices) | In between |
| Closing stock | Highest, near current price | Lowest, at oldest prices | In between |
| Profit shown | Highest | Lowest | In between |

In falling prices all three rows swap between FIFO and LIFO.

### Exam angle

### What ICAI asks from this topic

**[exam] Where pricing of issues appeared, May 2024 to September 2026 (and the old scheme)**
- **One-step MCQs, 2 marks**: January 2026 MCQ 14 (value of material consumed under LIFO, one issue against three lots); September 2026 MCQ 13 (value of one issue under FIFO after several receipts).
- **Inside a case scenario**: MTP Series I for September 2025, MCQs 1 to 5: closing raw material under FIFO when the price rose mid-period, feeding a cost sheet.
- **Full priced stores ledger, 10 marks**: May 2023 (old scheme) Q2(a), FIFO with month-wise purchases; MTP Series II for November 2022 (old scheme) Q5(b), weighted average with shortages reported by the stock verifier; RTP May 2020 (old scheme) Q1, weighted average with goods received and material returned notes, plus stock levels.
- **True or false**: November 2023 (old scheme) Q6(b): "Under LIFO, in falling prices, lower income is reported" (false: profit tends to rise) and "Simple average suits lots of different quantity with fluctuating prices" (false).

**[exam] The traps**
- Under the weighted average, **recompute after every receipt**, including a return from a department; issues between receipts use the same rate.
- A transfer between jobs or departments is **not** a stores ledger entry.
- Shortages: ICAI's answers differ. The Study Material enters a verification shortage in the issue column at the method's rate (SM 2.63); one old MTP treated unexplained shortages as absorbed (quantity cut, value kept). If the cause is given, use it: abnormal to Costing P&L, normal absorbed. If not, state your assumption.
- Rates to two decimals; amounts = quantity × the rounded rate, as in ICAI's Illustration 14.

**[trick] FIFO fills the cupboard with the latest prices**
Whatever the dates, FIFO's closing stock is priced at the most recent purchases (as far back as needed to cover the quantity), and LIFO's at the oldest lots still standing. So for a FIFO closing stock you can skip the ledger: take the closing quantity from the latest receipts backwards. Holds as long as no returns or shortages intervene; LIFO's closing stock depends on the timing of issues, so it always needs the ledger.

### Answer frame

### "Which method would you prefer, and why?" (as in TYK 9, SM 2.96)

1. Compare the cost charged to each job and the closing stock under each method (a small table).
2. Rising prices: FIFO charges least and leaves the highest closing stock (higher profit); LIFO the reverse (lower profit).
3. The weighted average evens out fluctuations: issues and stock show greater uniformity.
4. Conclude with the method you prefer and why: ICAI's own answer prefers the weighted average for the reason in point 3. Add that LIFO is not permitted under AS 2 and Ind AS 2.

### "When and why would you recommend LIFO?" (Illustration 12, SM 2.55)

When prices are rising: issues are close to current market price, so the cost of production matches current sales revenue; profit is not unduly high; in falling prices the products appear competitive; and in inflation it shows the correct profit and avoids paying undue taxes to some extent. Add that it is not permitted under AS 2 and Ind AS 2.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the average that never moved** **Fix:** a new rate after every receipt; issues carry the rate standing on their date.
- **The return priced at today's rate** **Fix:** returns to stores at the original issue price; returns to the vendor at the price of that lot, as ICAI's solutions do.
- **The transfer that was booked twice** **Fix:** no entry in the stores ledger; adjust the two jobs in the cost accounts.

### Recap

- A pricing method decides which purchase price each issue carries; issues + closing stock = opening stock + receipts under every method.
- FIFO: oldest prices to issues, closing stock at latest prices; suits falling prices.
- LIFO: latest prices to issues, closing stock at oldest prices; suits rising prices; not permitted under AS 2 and Ind AS 2.
- Weighted average = total cost in stock ÷ total quantity, recomputed after every receipt; smooths fluctuations.
- Simple average = total of lot prices ÷ number of lots, ignoring quantities and fully issued lots.
- Rising prices: issues FIFO < weighted average < LIFO; closing stock FIFO > weighted average > LIFO.
- Returns to stores at the original issue price; returns to vendor in the issue column; transfers between jobs: no stores ledger entry.
- Shortages: abnormal in the issue column at the current rate, to Costing P&L; normal absorbed (quantity cut, value kept). (Sugar is sugar; price tags are not.)

## Lesson 3.4: Stock levels: re-order, minimum, maximum, average and danger levels

*When to order, the least and most to hold, the stock normally carried and the emergency line: ICAI's stock-level formulas, the stock control chart and the traps in reverse questions.*

### Key idea

Order when stock falls to what the maximum usage could need over the maximum lead time (re-order level). The other levels follow from it: minimum = re-order level less normal usage during normal lead time; maximum = re-order level plus re-order quantity less the least usage during the shortest lead time.

### Formats and formulas

*Write each formula with its words first, then the figures, as ICAI's answers do.* Below, usage = consumption and lead time = re-order period, as ICAI uses both pairs of words.

**[formula] Re-order level (ROL)**
$$\text{ROL} = \text{Max. usage} \times \text{Max. lead time}$$
Alternatively ROL = minimum level + (average consumption × average re-order period) (SM 2.22–2.23).

**[formula] Minimum stock level**
$$\text{Minimum} = \text{ROL} \qquad - (\text{Avg. usage} \times \text{Avg. lead time})$$

**[formula] Maximum stock level**
$$\text{Maximum} = \text{ROL} + \text{ROQ} \qquad - (\text{Min. usage} \times \text{Min. lead time})$$
Here the re-order quantity may be the EOQ (SM 2.27).

**[formula] Average stock level**
$$\text{Average} = \text{Minimum} + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\text{ROQ} \qquad \text{or} \qquad \frac{\text{Maximum} + \text{Minimum}}{2}$$

**[formula] Danger level**
$$\text{Danger} = \text{Avg. usage} \qquad \times \text{Emergency lead time}$$
"Some time minimum consumption is also used" (SM 2.27).

**[formula] Economic order quantity (when ROQ is not given)**
$$\text{EOQ} = \sqrt{\frac{2AO}{C}}$$
A = annual requirement, O = cost per order, C = carrying cost per unit per annum (SM 2.24). Details: [[cma.mat.eoq|EOQ and quantity discounts]].

**[definition] Re-order stock level**
The level which lies between minimum and maximum levels in such a way that before the material ordered is received into the stores, there is sufficient quantity in hand to cover both normal and abnormal consumption situations; the level at which fresh order should be placed for replenishment of stock. (SM 2.22)

**[definition] Minimum, maximum and danger levels (SM 2.27)**
**Minimum**: the lowest level of material stock which must be maintained in hand at all times, so that there is no stoppage of production due to non-availability of inventory. **Maximum**: the highest level of quantity for any material which can be held in stock at any time; any quantity beyond it causes extra expenditure on funds, storage and obsolescence. **Danger**: the level at which normal issues of the raw material inventory are stopped and emergency issues only are made. **Buffer stock**: stock kept for contingency, used in case of sudden order.

### Exam angle

### What ICAI asks from this topic

**[trick] ROL is "worst × worst", minimum is "ROL less normal", maximum is "ROL plus order less best"**

### "Distinguish between re-order level and re-order quantity" (SM 2.22–2.23)

### "Explain the methods of inventory control by setting quantitative levels"

### Short notes: danger level; maximum and minimum levels

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the re-order level built on a good day** **Fix:** ROL = maximum consumption × maximum re-order period (or minimum level + average × average).
- **The maximum level with the wrong subtraction** **Fix:** the maximum level subtracts the **minimum** consumption over the **minimum** lead time: the least that can be used before the order arrives.

### Recap

- Re-order level = maximum consumption × maximum re-order period (or minimum level + average consumption × average period).

- Average level = minimum + ½ ROQ, or (maximum + minimum) ÷ 2. Danger level = average consumption × emergency lead time.
- Re-order quantity not given: use the EOQ, √(2AO/C).
- Missing minimum consumption: 2 × average − maximum. Same time units throughout.
- Perishable material: maximum level is the lower of the formula and shelf-life consumption.
- Safety stock: optimum where expected stock-out cost + carrying cost is least; ROL = safety stock + lead time consumption. (Kesar's lakh packs: no.)

## Lesson 3.5: Economic Order Quantity and quantity discounts

*How much to order at a time: the order size at which ordering cost plus carrying cost is lowest, and how to test a supplier's quantity discount on total cost.*

### Key idea

EOQ is the order size at which annual ordering cost plus annual carrying cost is lowest; in the basic model the two are equal there. A quantity discount is worth taking only if the total cost, purchase price included, is lower at the discount lot than at the EOQ.

### Formats and formulas

*Reproduce the formula, the four assumptions and the comparison statement from memory.*

**[formula] Economic Order Quantity**
$$\text{EOQ} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times A \times O}{C}}$$
A = annual requirement (the demand for raw material or input for a year); O = cost per order (the cost of placing an order for purchase); C = carrying cost per unit per annum (the cost of carrying average inventory on an annual basis) (SM 2.24).

**[formula] Carrying cost per unit per annum**
$$C = r \times P + F$$
r = sum of the carrying cost rates per annum; P = price per unit; F = any fixed carrying cost per unit per annum. Storage 2%, interest 12% and obsolescence 6% on a ₹20 unit give C = 20% × ₹20 = ₹4 (SM 2.26). A storage cost of ₹2.5 a kg a month is ₹30 a year.

**[formula] Orders, interval and annual costs at order size Q**
$$\text{Number of orders} = \frac{A}{Q} \qquad \text{Order interval} = \frac{\text{Days in the year}}{\text{Number of orders}}$$
$$\text{Ordering cost} = \frac{A}{Q} \times O \qquad \text{Carrying cost} = \frac{Q}{2} \times C \qquad \text{Total cost} = A \times \text{price} \qquad + \frac{A}{Q} \times O + \frac{Q}{2} \times C$$
Q ÷ 2 is the average inventory.

**[definition] Economic Order Quantity (EOQ)**
The size of an order for which total of ordering and carrying cost are minimum. (SM 2.23)

**[definition] Ordering cost**
Costs associated with the purchase or order of materials, such as cost to invite quotations, documentation works like preparation of purchase orders, employee cost directly attributable to the procurement of material, transportation and inspection cost. (SM 2.23)

**[definition] Carrying cost**
Costs for holding or carrying inventories in store, such as the cost of fund invested in inventories, cost of storage, insurance cost and obsolescence. (SM 2.23)

**Assumptions underlying EOQ**, in ICAI's order (SM 2.24):

1. Ordering cost per order and carrying cost per unit per annum are known and they are fixed.
2. Anticipated usage of material in units is known.
3. Cost per unit of the material is constant and is known as well.
4. The quantity of material ordered is received immediately, i.e. the lead time is zero.

### The comparison statement for a quantity discount

|---|---:|---:|
| Purchase cost (A × price) | xxx | xxx |
| Ordering cost (A ÷ Q × O) | xxx | xxx |
| Carrying cost (Q ÷ 2 × C) | xxx | xxx |
| **Total cost** | **xxx** | **xxx** |

End with "Advise – …". With a schedule of price breaks, ICAI uses one row per order size (SM 2.90); on paper write the columns: annual requirement, order size, number of orders, cost of inventory, ordering cost, carrying cost, total cost.

- C = (sum of annual rates × price) + fixed ₹ per unit; annualise monthly and quarterly rates first.
- Orders = A ÷ Q; ordering cost = A ÷ Q × O; carrying cost = Q ÷ 2 × C; total = A × price + both.
- Assumptions: costs known and fixed; usage known; price constant; lead time zero.
- Quantity discount: compare total cost including purchase cost, carrying cost at the discounted price; with price breaks (EOQ in the base band), test EOQ and each band's lowest quantity.
- Fractional orders: round up, and say so. (Two mandi trips a week, not one a month.)

- HML: by the cost of an individual item, unlike ABC's overall value.
- JIT: zero inventory, demand pull; two principles: produce only when required, deliver only when customers want.

- Two bin: order when issues reach the smaller part. (Watch the rent, not the matchboxes.)

## Lesson 3.7: Inventory turnover ratios and stock discrepancies

*How fast each material moves (inventory turnover ratio and days held), what the input-output ratio shows, and how shortages found on stock verification are valued and charged.*

### Key idea

The turnover ratio counts how many times a year the average stock is used up: a high ratio means fast-moving material, a low one over-investment and working capital locked up. A shortage found on verification is priced like an issue; a normal one stays in product cost, an abnormal one goes to the Costing Profit and Loss Account.

### Formats and formulas

*Reproduce the statement and the formulas from memory.*

**[formula] Inventory turnover ratio**
$$\text{ITR} = \frac{\text{Cost of materials consumed}}{\text{Cost of average stock held}}$$
ITR = inventory turnover ratio; both terms for the same period. Average stock = ½ (opening stock + closing stock) (SM 2.43).

**[formula] Average number of days of inventory holding**
$$\text{Days held} = \frac{365}{\text{ITR}}$$
Average number of days of inventory holding = 365 days (or 12 months) ÷ inventory turnover ratio (SM 2.43); 360 if the question fixes it.

**[formula] Materials consumed**
$$\text{Consumption} = O + P - C$$
O = opening stock, P = purchases, C = closing stock of materials (SM 2.72).

**[formula] Input-output ratio**
$$\text{Input-output ratio} = \frac{I}{S}$$
I = quantity of material input to production; S = standard material content of the actual output (SM 2.42). The Study Material sets no threshold, but the comparison reads directly: above 1, more material went in than the standard allows (adverse usage); below 1, less (favourable).

### Answer frame

### "Explain inventory control by ratio analysis"

1. Input-output ratio: input of material ÷ standard material content of actual output; compares actual and standard consumption; shows favourable or adverse usage.
2. Inventory turnover ratio: cost of materials consumed ÷ cost of average stock; average stock = ½ (opening + closing).
3. Days of inventory holding = 365 days (or 12 months) ÷ the ratio.
4. Interpretation: high ratio, fast moving; low ratio, over-investment and locking up of working capital.
5. Use: compare materials (and with standards), reduce capital locked up and prevent over-stocking of slow-moving items.

### "How are shortages found on physical verification treated?"

1. Valuation: entered in the issue column at the rate of the issue method adopted (FIFO or any other).
2. Normal shortage (breaking of bulk, evaporation, unavoidable): absorbed by good units, or charged to factory overheads.
3. Abnormal shortage (mishandling, pilferage, avoidable): transferred to Costing Profit and Loss Account.
4. Control: explain the causes promptly, make corrective entries, remove the causes.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the ratio built on purchases** **Fix:** the numerator is materials consumed: opening + purchases − closing.
- **Closing stock in place of average stock** **Fix:** average stock = ½ (opening + closing), unless the question gives the average.
- **The whole difference written off** **Fix:** first correct the records (the 150 litres of requisition 318 belong to the snacks line); of the real shortage only the abnormal 30 litres go to Costing Profit and Loss, while the normal 40 stay in product cost, absorbed by the good units or through overheads.
- **The shortage at the latest purchase price** **Fix:** a shortage is an issue: use the rate of the issue method, here the weighted average ₹156.00.

### Recap

- Materials consumed = opening stock + purchases − closing stock.
- Inventory turnover ratio = cost of materials consumed ÷ cost of average stock; average stock = ½ (opening + closing).
- Days of inventory holding = 365 days (or 12 months, or the 360 a question fixes) ÷ ratio.
- High ratio: fast moving. Low ratio: over-investment, working capital locked up.
- Input-output ratio = material input ÷ standard material content of actual output: above 1 adverse, below 1 favourable.
- Shortage on verification: an issue at the issue-method rate; normal (bulk breaking, evaporation, unavoidable) stays in product cost, abnormal (pilferage, mishandling, avoidable) to Costing Profit and Loss.
- Cost ledger: normal, Factory Overhead Control Dr; abnormal, Costing P&L Dr; Stores Ledger Control Cr. (Count the table turns, not the full tables.)

## Lesson 3.8: Waste, scrap, spoilage, defectives and obsolescence

*The five forms of material loss in ICAI's words, how each is treated when normal and when abnormal, and the spoiled and defective work rules ICAI keeps asking.*

### Key idea

Normal losses are inherent and are borne by the good units, net of any realisable value; abnormal losses are avoidable and are charged to the Costing Profit and Loss Account after crediting any realisable value. Obsolete material is a total loss, abnormal by definition, and never part of the cost of manufacture.

### Formats and formulas

*Reproduce the cost-per-good-unit formula and the spoilage entry from memory.*

**[formula] Cost of a good unit when there is a normal loss**
$$\text{Cost per good unit} = \frac{T - R}{I - N}$$
T = total cost; R = realisable value of the normal loss (scrap value less cost to sell); I = input units; N = normal loss units. An abnormal loss unit is valued at the same cost as a good unit (SM 10.7–10.9).

**[formula] Splitting a spoilage between normal and abnormal**
$$\text{Abnormal} = \text{Actual} - \text{Normal spoilage}$$
Any realisable value of the spoiled work is apportioned between the two in the ratio of their quantities (SM 9.10).

**[trick] "Inherent goes in, avoidable goes out"**
Normal = inherent in the operation: cost stays in the good units, less what the loss sells for. Abnormal = avoidable or beyond the normal: cost goes to Costing Profit and Loss, after what it sells for. Conditions: two exceptions to remember, rigid-specification spoilage (absorbed by good production, disposal cost to production overhead) and obsolescence (always abnormal).

### Answer frame

### Distinguish between waste and scrap (SM 2.64, 2.66)

ICAI's suggested answer to May 2024 Q6(b) puts the two definitions first, then the Study Material's three points:

| Waste | Scrap |
|---|---|
| 1. The portion of raw material which is lost during storage or production and discarded | 1. The output which is discarded and disposed of without further treatment |
| 2. Connected with raw material or inputs to the production process | 2. The loss connected with the output |
| 3. May be visible or invisible | 3. Generally identifiable and has physical substance |
| 4. Generally has no recoverable value | 4. Termed as by-products and has small recoverable value |

### Distinguish between scrap and defectives (SM 2.67)

| Scrap | Defectives |
|---|---|
| 1. Loss connected with the output | 1. Connected with the output as well as the input |
| 2. Not intended, but cannot be eliminated due to the nature of material or process | 2. Not intended, but can be eliminated through a proper control system |
| 3. Generally not used or rectified | 3. Can be used after rectification |
| 4. Insignificant recoverable value | 4. Sold at a lower value than the good one |

**Spoilage against defectives** (SM 2.67): spoilage cannot be repaired or reconditioned, while defectives can be rectified and transferred back to standard production or to the seconds; the problem in accounting for defective work is the cost of rectification or rework.

### Three answers to "Discuss the treatment of normal and abnormal scrap"

**[cols] **Loses marks****

"Normal scrap is absorbed; abnormal scrap goes to P&L."

No realisable value, no scrap account, no credit to the job.
---
**Passes**

"Normal: cost borne by good units, realisable value deducted. Abnormal: written off to Costing P&L."

Normal is right; abnormal skips the scrap account at full cost.
---
**Full marks**

The definition first. Normal: cost of scrap borne by good units, income from its realisable value deducted from the cost. Abnormal: scrap account charged with full cost, job or process credited, profit or loss on realisation to Costing Profit and Loss Account.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: Kesar's miscellaneous drawer** **Fix:** split each loss into normal (stays in product cost) and abnormal (Costing Profit and Loss Account); an abnormal loss inside product cost overstates the cost of every good unit and hides the inefficiency.
- **Abnormal scrap valued at its sale price** **Fix:** the scrap account is charged with the **full cost** (300 kg × ₹50 = ₹15,000); production is credited with that, and only the ₹12,000 shortfall on realisation goes to Costing Profit and Loss.
- **Obsolete stock carried at cost "until it is used"** **Fix:** obsolete material is a total loss: dispose of it at the best available price and charge the loss to Costing Profit and Loss; it is never part of the cost of manufacture.
- **Every defect written off** **Fix:** rectification within the normal rate of defectives (or the proportion management provided for) is a normal cost charged to the batch; only the excess, or defects caused by the Inspection Department's error, are written off.

### Recap

- Five forms: waste (lost, discarded), scrap (discarded output, small value), spoilage (beyond economic repair), defectives (below standard: reworks or rejects), obsolescence (value lost to supersession).
- Normal loss: borne by good units, net of realisable value. Abnormal loss: Costing Profit and Loss Account, after realisable value.
- Scrap: normal, realisable value deducted from cost; abnormal, scrap account at full cost, job credited, loss on realisation to Costing P&L.
- Spoilage: normal, charged to the production order or production overhead; abnormal, Costing P&L; rigid specification, absorbed by good production with disposal cost to production overhead.
- Defectives: normal, cost less realisable value to material cost of good production; abnormal, Costing P&L after crediting realisable value.
- Defective work by circumstance: allowed percentage, to the batch (excess written off); bad workmanship, written off; Inspection Department's error, charged to it and written off.
- Obsolescence: always abnormal, total loss, dispose at the best price. (The tailor prices in the offcuts, never the ruined shirt.)
