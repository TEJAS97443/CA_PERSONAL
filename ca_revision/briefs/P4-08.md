## Lesson 8.1: Unit costing

*Where every unit of output is identical, cost is collected element-wise for a period and divided by the units produced; the statement of cost per unit then prices any output level.*

### Key idea

Unit costing applies where the output is identical and each unit requires identical cost: cost per unit = total cost of production ÷ number of units produced. To price a different level of output, keep variable cost per unit, keep fixed cost in total, and split semi-variable costs first.

**[exam] The traps ICAI set**
- Capacity given at one level ("1,80,000 units at 60% capacity"): find 100% first (3,00,000), then each period's units.
- Semi-variable "for every 20% increase in the capacity or part thereof": 50% to 75% is two steps, not one (MTP Series II December 2021 and November 2022).
- Fixed costs are annual: take the months' share, not the full year, for each period.

**[trick] Units in a period**
Units = annual capacity at 100% × months ÷ 12 × capacity %. Condition: the capacity is stated per annum; convert a monthly or a partial-capacity figure to 100% per annum first.

### Answer frame

**Describe unit costing and batch costing, with three examples of industries for each** (May 2024 Q6(c), 4 marks). ICAI's suggested answer:

1. **Unit costing**: the method of costing where the output produced is identical and each unit of output requires identical cost; costs are collected and analysed element-wise, and cost per unit = total cost ÷ number of units produced. Used in paper, cement, steel works, mining, breweries.
2. **Batch costing**: a type of specific order costing where articles are manufactured in predetermined lots, known as batches; cost per unit in a batch = total cost of the batch ÷ units produced in that batch. ICAI's answer named pharmaceutical or drug industries, readymade garments and electronic parts of TV and radio sets ([[cma.unit.batch|batch costing]]).

**For an average-price question**, the marks are in: a working note for units by period, a working note for each step of the semi-variable cost, a statement of cost by period with a total column, profit added, and the average price with its division shown; then the advice ("accept, as ₹130 is above the required ₹128.45").

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: one cost per unit for every output** **Fix:** at each level, take fixed costs in total; only variable costs stay the same per unit.
- **The semi-variable cost treated as fixed (or as variable)** **Fix:** split it: ₹5 a unit plus ₹25,000.
- **Annual fixed cost charged in full to each period** **Fix:** months ÷ 12 of the annual figure for each period; a cost given per month (the shift cost, the fixed part of power) is multiplied by the months instead.
- **Abnormal defectives left in the cost of good units** **Fix:** cost of abnormal defectives goes to the Costing Profit and Loss Account (SM 8.4).

### Recap

- Unit costing: output identical, each unit requires identical cost; also called single or output costing (SM 8.3).
- Cost per unit = total cost of production ÷ number of units produced.
- Industries: paper, cement, steel works, mining, breweries.
- Costs collected element-wise: materials from requisition notes, labour from time cards, overheads under standing order numbers, apportioned and absorbed (SM 8.3–8.4).
- Defectives: normal within limit to the entire output; excess and abnormal to Costing Profit and Loss Account (SM 8.4).
- New output level: variable per unit same, fixed in total same, semi-variable split by change in cost ÷ change in output.
- Production programme: cost each period at its own capacity (steps "or part thereof" round up), add profit, divide by total units.
- One juice cart, one product, one division.

## Lesson 8.2: Batch costing and the economic batch quantity

*Batch costing makes the batch the cost object and divides its cost by the units in it; the economic batch quantity is the batch size at which set-up and holding costs together are lowest.*

### Key idea

In batch costing the batch is the cost object: cost per unit = cost of the batch ÷ units in the batch. The economic batch quantity is the batch size at which set-up costs and inventory holding costs together are at a minimum: EBQ = √(2DS ÷ C).

### Formats and formulas

**[formula] Economic batch quantity**
$$\text{EBQ} = \sqrt{\frac{2DS}{C}}$$
D = annual demand for the product; S = setting up cost per batch; C = carrying cost per unit of production, per annum (SM 8.12). If C is given per month, multiply by 12; if as a percentage, apply it to the cost of making one unit (SM Illustrations 5 and 6).

**[formula] Number of runs, interval and the costs at any batch size Q**
$$\text{Runs} = \frac{D}{Q} \qquad \text{Interval (days)} = \frac{365}{\text{Runs}}$$

$$\text{Set-up cost} = \frac{D}{Q} \times S \qquad \text{Holding cost} = \frac{Q}{2} \times C$$
Average inventory is half the batch (SM 8.13). At the EBQ the two costs are equal.

**[definition] Economic batch quantity (EBQ)**
Economic batch quantity is the size of a batch where total cost of set-up and holding costs are at minimum. (SM 8.11)

**[exam] The traps ICAI set**
- Carrying cost per month (multiply by 12) or as a percentage of the unit's production cost.
- Monthly demand: annualise before the formula (500 a month × 12 in SM Illustration 5).
- "Extra cost of the present policy": total cost at the policy minus total cost at the EBQ, both set-up and holding.
- Rejections in a batch: cost is spread over the good units only; scrap value reduces cost (January 2025).

**[trick] The equal-cost check**
At the EBQ, annual set-up cost equals annual holding cost (D ÷ Q × S = Q ÷ 2 × C, which solves to the formula). If your two figures differ, recheck C's period. Condition: the simple EBQ model, with average inventory taken as half the batch.

### Answer frame

**In batch costing, state how the economic batch quantity is determined** (SM 8.18, Q3; MTP Series I May 2026 Q6(c)).

1. In batch production the lot size is critical to the least cost of operation; total production cost comprises two main costs: machine set-up costs and inventory holding costs.
2. If the batch size is higher, set-up costs decline because fewer set-ups are needed, but units in inventory rise and holding costs go up; if it is lower, holding costs fall but set-up costs rise.
3. EBQ is the size of a batch where the total of set-up and holding costs is at a minimum: on a graph, where the two cost lines intersect.
4. It is found by calculating total cost for a series of batch sizes and picking the lowest, or by the formula EBQ = √(2DS ÷ C), with D, S and C defined.

**Describe batch costing, with examples, and its costing procedure** (MTP Series II May 2026 Q6(b)): the SM 8.8 definition; cost per unit = cost of the batch ÷ units in it; examples (ICAI's May 2024 answer: pharmaceutical or drug industries, readymade garments, electronic parts of TV and radio sets; the SM's own: pens, bakery items); then the procedure (one number per batch, materials from requisitions, labour from time cards, overheads on machine or labour hours).

**Distinguish job costing and batch costing**: ICAI's three points in the table in How it works.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the monthly carrying cost used as annual** **Fix:** C is per unit per annum: ₹1 × 12 = ₹12.
- **Monthly demand in an annual formula** **Fix:** D = annual demand, 500 × 12 = 6,000 (SM Illustration 5). Keep D and C over the same period.
- **Set-up cost spread per unit** **Fix:** a cost per batch is counted by batches, and a part batch is a whole batch.
- **The extra cost of a policy measured on one cost only** **Fix:** compare total cost, set-up plus holding: holding rises from ₹24,000 to 15,000 × ₹4 = ₹60,000, so the policy costs ₹21,600 more.

### Recap

- Batch costing: a type of specific order costing; articles made in predetermined lots; the batch is the cost object (SM 8.8).
- Procedure: a number per batch; materials from requisitions; labour from time cards; overheads on machine or labour hours; cost per unit = batch cost ÷ units.
- Set-up cost is incurred per batch, whatever its size.
- EBQ = size of batch where total set-up and holding costs are minimum: √(2DS ÷ C), D annual, C per unit per annum.
- Runs = D ÷ EBQ (ICAI rounds up); interval = 365 ÷ runs; at EBQ, set-up cost = holding cost.
- Extra cost of a policy = total cost at the policy − total cost at the EBQ.
- Job vs batch (SM 8.15): unique jobs vs homogeneous lots; cost per job vs cost per batch then per unit; individuality vs none.
- Knead the atta for a day or two, not for every roti.
