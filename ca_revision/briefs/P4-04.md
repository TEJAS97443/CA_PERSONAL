## Lesson 4.1: Attendance, payroll and the elements of wages

*How a factory records who came and what they worked on, turns that into a payroll, and works out what one productive hour of an employee really costs.*

### Key idea

Time-keeping says how long an employee was at work; time booking says what that time was spent on. The cost of an employee hour for costing is the whole employee cost (monetary and non-monetary) divided by the effective hours, not the payslip divided by the hours paid.

### Formats and formulas

**[definition] Employee (labour) cost**
Benefits paid or payable to the employees of an entity, whether permanent or temporary, for the services rendered by them. Employee cost includes payments made in cash or kind. (SM 3.3)

**[definition] Direct and indirect employee cost**
Direct: benefits paid or payable to the employees which can be attributed to a cost object in an economically feasible manner. Indirect: benefits paid or payable to the employees which cannot be directly attributable to a particular cost object in an economically feasible manner. (SM 3.3)

**[definition] Time-keeping and time booking**
Time-keeping refers to recording and keeping of the employees' attendance time. Time booking is recording the details of work done and the time spent by an employee on each job or process. (SM 3.55)

**[formula] Rate per hour for costing (direct workers)**
$$\text{Rate per hour} = \frac{\text{All benefits}}{\text{Effective hours}}$$
ICAI: total estimated monetary benefits and cost of non-monetary benefits ÷ (budgeted direct employee hours − normal idle time) (SM 3.42). Leave on full pay also comes off the hours.

When the question asks for both the pay and the cost, ICAI keeps two statements (SM 3.19–3.20, Illustration 3):

|---|---|

The cost statement leaves overtime out because ICAI allocates the overtime wages separately, to the job on which the overtime was worked (Job Y in Illustration 3); the ordinary wages are shared over the jobs in the ratio of time booked.

**[exam] The traps ICAI set**
- Leave on full pay and normal idle time come off the hours, not the cost; abnormal idle time stays in the hours and its cost is charged separately (MTP September 2026 Series I Q1(a)).

**[trick] Cost on top, effective hours below**
Rate for costing = everything the employer bears ÷ hours that can be worked. What is withheld *from* the worker's wages (his PF, ESI, TDS) is already in the top line; paid time not worked (leave, normal idle time) comes off the bottom line. Condition: the inflated-rate method for direct workers; under the overheads method only basic and DA go on top.

1. Attendance is marked by recognising an employee on the basis of physical and behavioural traits.
2. Each employee's unique identity (fingerprint, face, retina image) is kept in a database and matched when attendance is marked.
3. It includes fingerprint recognition, face recognition and time and attendance tracking technology.
4. It reduces the risk of time manipulation and proxy attendance.
5. Its set-up and maintenance cost may make it unsuitable for small organisations (SM 3.8).
6. Apply it: a large factory with proxy attendance justifies the cost; payroll should also pay only the employees on roll listed by HR (SM 3.11).

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the PF counted twice** **Fix:** the employee's contribution is already inside his gross wages; add only the employer's contribution.
- **The paid holiday that worked a shift** **Fix:** leave on full pay and normal idle time come out of the hours; their cost stays in the numerator, spread over the hours actually worked.
- **The night shift that billed the night's job** **Fix:** it is not for any particular job, so it is overheads (SM 3.42).

### Recap

- Employee cost: benefits paid or payable to employees, permanent or temporary, in cash or kind; direct if traceable to a cost object economically, else indirect (overheads).
- Aim of control: low wages per unit of output, not a low wage bill. Eight factors, from manpower assessment to employee productivity.
- Time-keeping = attendance time (six objectives); time booking = time on each job (costing, efficiency, responsibility). Job time + idle time = attendance time.
- Payroll in five steps: time and attendance, employee details, wage sheet, payment after deductions, deposit with statutory bodies. HR's list stops bogus employees.
- Wages include non-monetary benefits: medical, education and training, recreation, housing and welfare, subsidised canteen.
- Rate for costing = (monetary + non-monetary benefits) ÷ (budgeted hours − normal idle time); leave days come off the hours too.
- Add the employer's PF and ESI; never the employee's. Night shift allowance: overheads. (Measure the iceberg, not the tip.)

## Lesson 4.2: Idle time and overtime

*Paid hours that produce nothing and extra hours that cost extra: what causes each, where ICAI charges it, and how to compute the charge to a job.*

### Key idea

Normal idle time and the normal-rate part of overtime are costs of production; abnormal idle time is a loss for Costing Profit and Loss. The overtime premium follows the reason the overtime was worked.

### Formats and formulas

**[definition] Idle time**
The time for which the employer pays but obtains no direct benefit or for no productive purpose. (SM 3.55)

**[definition] Overtime premium**
The rate for overtime work is higher than the normal time rate; the extra amount so paid over the normal rate is called overtime premium. (SM 3.17)

**[definition] Ordinary rate of wages (Factories Act, as quoted in the SM)**
The basic wages plus such allowances, including the cash equivalent of the advantage accruing through the concessional sale to workers of food grains and other articles, as the worker is for the time being entitled to, but does not include a bonus and wages for overtime work. (SM 3.17)

**[formula] Idle time and overtime**
$$\text{Idle time} = \text{Time paid} - \text{Time booked}$$
$$\text{OT pay} = \text{OT hours} \times \text{normal rate} \qquad + \text{premium}$$
Premium = overtime hours × (overtime rate − normal rate).

**[formula] Average inflated wage rate**
$$\text{Inflated rate} = \frac{\text{Total wages}}{\text{Total hours}}$$
Total wages = normal-time wages + overtime wages at the overtime rates (premium included). Used when overtime is a regular policy (SM 3.25).

ICAI's layout for the wages of a period (SM 3.16, Illustration 2). *Reproduce it from memory.*

| Allocation of wages in cost accounting | Hours × rate | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|---:|
| Allocated to Job X | hours booked × rate | xxx |

| **Total** (equals the wages paid) | | **xxx** |

Rate = wages of the period ÷ effective hours (paid hours less normal breaks). For overtime questions, ICAI shows each situation separately: employee cost chargeable to the job, and the premium as overhead, to the job, to another department or to Costing Profit and Loss (SM 3.25–3.26).

**[exam] The traps ICAI set**
- **Rate or premium?** Illustration 5 gives overtime "rates" of 175% and 225% of basic; MTP May 2025 Series I Q1(c) gives a "premium" of 75% and 125%: the same thing. Premium = rate − 100%.
- **Only the premium moves.** In the irregular, customer and flood cases, the job keeps all hours at the basic rate; only the premium goes elsewhere.
- **Which time is abnormal?** "Unaccounted time attributable to power failure" (January 2025 MCQ 13) is abnormal; the allowed 15 minutes a day for maintenance is normal.
- **Which rate?** The cost of idle time with a rate per hour given is hours × that rate (January 2025 MCQ 13); an allocation of the week's wages uses wages ÷ effective hours for the jobs and the abnormal hours alike (MTP September 2025 Series II MCQ 14: ₹2,640 ÷ 44 hours = ₹60).
- One MTP answer (September 2026 Series I Q1(a)(iv)) words the customer case differently; write the Study Material's rule: a customer's request is charged to the job.

**[trick] Follow the cause**
Ask "Who or what made this time necessary?" The normal course of business: cost of production. The customer: the job. Another department: that department. Nature or abnormal events: Costing Profit and Loss. Condition: overtime worked regularly as a policy is the exception; it is spread through the inflated rate.

### Answer frame

### "Describe idle time and its treatment in the following situations"

Define idle time first (time paid but no production; time paid − time booked; normal or abnormal), then a table, as ICAI's suggested answer to May 2024 Q6(c) does:

| Situation | Idle time | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Setting-up time for a direct worker's machine | Normal | Part of cost of production; allowed for in standard hours or rate |
| Lunch break of an indirect worker | Normal | Considered in computing the overhead rate |
| Time lost by a machine breakdown | Abnormal | Separate item in the Costing Profit and Loss Account |

### "Discuss the effect of overtime payment on productivity"

1. From the SM (3.17–3.18): occasional overtime is healthy (optimum capacity, fully used); persistent overtime is a bad sign (more capacity needed, or work habitually postponed to earn overtime).
2. ICAI's suggested answer (MTP May 2025 Series I Q2(b)) adds that overtime raises the cost of production: the premium is an extra payment; efficiency during overtime may fall; workers may slacken in normal hours to earn more; lower output and higher premium raise cost.

### "Discuss the accounting treatment of idle time and overtime wages"

The chapter's own Theoretical Question 1, set as MTP September 2025 Series I Q4(b) (4 marks). ICAI's suggested answer gives the normal idle time treatment and the four premium treatments; add abnormal idle time to make the answer complete. In this order:

1. **Normal idle time**: part of the cost of production; for direct workers an allowance in the standard hours or standard rate, for indirect workers considered in computing the overhead rate (SM 3.13).
2. **Abnormal idle time**: not part of production cost; a separate item in the Costing Profit and Loss Account, analysed into controllable and uncontrollable by cause (SM 3.14).
3. **Overtime premium** (SM 3.18): at the desire of the customer, charged to the job directly; for general production programmes or urgent orders, overhead cost of the department or cost centre that works overtime; due to the fault of another department, charged to that department; on account of abnormal conditions such as flood or earthquake, not charged to cost but to Costing Profit and Loss Account. The overtime hours at the normal rate are charged like ordinary wages.
4. **Overtime worked regularly as a policy**: the premium is part of employee cost, and jobs are charged at the average inflated wage rate (SM 3.25).

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the 75% premium paid at 75%** **Fix:** a premium is the extra; the overtime rate is 175% of basic.
- **The whole overtime wage sent to overheads** **Fix:** the overtime hours are still the jobs' hours at the normal rate; only the premium (240 × ₹48) goes to overheads.
- **The power cut spread over the jobs** **Fix:** divide by the effective hours (3,600), and charge the 200 abnormal hours separately to Costing Profit and Loss.
- **Normal idle time written off** **Fix:** normal idle time cannot be avoided in the normal course of business; it is part of the cost of production.

### Recap

- Idle time = time paid − time booked: paid, nothing produced.
- Normal idle time (gate to workplace, between jobs, set-ups, rest and lunch): cost of production; direct workers through the rate, indirect workers through the overhead rate.
- Abnormal idle time (lack of coordination, power failure, breakdown, no material, strikes, lockouts, fire, flood): Costing Profit and Loss, analysed as controllable and uncontrollable.
- Overtime payment = overtime hours at normal rate + premium; usually double the normal rate.
- Premium: customer's request → job; general production or urgent orders → department overheads; another department's fault → that department; abnormal conditions → Costing P&L. Regular policy → inflated rate for all jobs.
- The SM's Factories Act quote: more than nine hours a day or 48 a week, twice the ordinary rate.
- Occasional overtime healthy; persistent overtime a bad sign. (Whoever caused the wait pays for it.)

## Lesson 4.3: Employee turnover: measurement, causes, effects and cost

*How fast a workforce changes, measured three ways; why people leave; what it does to cost; and how to compute the profit forgone because of it.*

### Key idea

Turnover = movement out, in, or both, divided by the average number on roll. It costs the contribution on the productive hours lost plus the costs of leaving, hiring and training; preventive spending is worth it up to the optimum level of turnover.

### Formats and formulas

**[definition] Employee (labour) turnover**
The rate of change in the composition of employee force during a specified period measured against a suitable index. (SM 3.45)

**[formula] Replacement and separation methods**
$$\text{Replacement} = \frac{\text{Employees replaced}}{\text{Average on roll}} \times 100$$
$$\text{Separation} = \frac{\text{Employees separated}}{\text{Average on roll}} \times 100$$
(SM 3.45–3.46)

**[formula] Flux method**
$$\text{Flux} = \frac{\text{Separated} + \text{Replaced}}{\text{Average on roll}} \times 100$$
With accessions (all joining, including replacements): (separations + accessions) ÷ average on roll × 100, or (separations + replacements + new joinings) ÷ average on roll × 100 (SM 3.46).

**[formula] Average on roll and the equivalent annual rate**
$$\text{Average} = \frac{\text{At beginning} + \text{At end}}{2} \qquad \text{Annual rate} = \frac{\text{Rate for period} \times 365}{\text{Days in period}}$$
(SM 3.46–3.47)

**[exam] The traps ICAI set**
- **Accessions or replacements?** "Recruited and joined" is accessions: replacements plus new joinings (Illustration 14; May 2026 Q4(b)).
- **Productive hours, not all hours.** Only the unproductive part of training hours is lost (40% in May 2024 Q5(a)); sales per hour is sales ÷ actual productive hours.
- **Contribution, not sales.** The lost sales are multiplied by the P/V ratio before the turnover costs are added.
- **Annualise.** A quarter's rate × 4; any other period × 365 ÷ days.

**[trick] One count unlocks the rest**
The three methods share one denominator. Find the average on roll from whichever method's count is given (count ÷ rate), then every other count is rate × average. Condition: all the rates are for the same period and use the same average.

### Answer frame

### "Discuss the two types of cost associated with labour turnover"

ICAI's suggested answers (MTP May 2026 Series I Q6(b); MTP September 2025 Series II Q6(c)) reproduce SM 3.50: (a) **preventive costs**, defined, with the three items (medical benefits, welfare such as pension, other retention benefits); (b) **replacement costs**, defined, with the items (recruitment, training and induction, abnormal breakage and scrap, extra wages and overheads from the inefficiency of new workers); then the conclusion that high turnover means high replacement costs and only adequate preventive costs keep it low, so each firm works out its optimum level.

### "Discuss the remedial steps to minimise labour turnover"

The Study Material says proper and timely management action can appreciably reduce turnover from avoidable causes (SM 3.49) and lists the preventive costs (SM 3.50). ICAI's suggested answers to the MTP questions (January 2026 Series II Q6(b); May 2024 Series II Q6(c)) give these steps; write them with one line each:

1. **Exit interview** with each outgoing employee, to find out why he is leaving.
2. **Job analysis and evaluation**, to know the requirements of each job.
3. A **scientific system of recruitment, placement and promotion**.
4. A **healthy atmosphere**, with education, medical and housing facilities for workers.
5. A **committee for settling workers' grievances**.

Then tie them to the avoidable causes they remove (dissatisfaction, strained relations, no training or promotion, no recreational or medical facilities, low wages).

### "Causes" and "effects"

Write the three heads with ICAI's items in ICAI's order (the lists above), adding the remark on remedies under each head. For effects, the five ways high turnover raises the cost of production (SM 3.50).

### The numerical: three answers to the profit-forgone question

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the opening headcount as the denominator** **Fix:** every method divides by the average number on roll, (beginning + end) ÷ 2.
- **The new café's staff counted as replacements** **Fix:** employees appointed for expansion are not replacements (SM 3.45); they enter only the flux rate with accessions.
- **All training hours written off** **Fix:** only the unproductive half (1,600 hours) is lost; the rest produced output.
- **A month's rate reported as the year's** **Fix:** rate × 365 ÷ days in the period (24.33% here), or a quarterly rate × 4.

### Recap

- Employee turnover: the rate of change in the composition of the workforce in a period, against an index (industry, locality, or own past rate).
- Replacement = replaced ÷ average on roll; separation = separated ÷ average; flux = (separated + replaced) ÷ average; all × 100.
- Expansion hires are not replacements; accessions = all joinings including replacements; flux with accessions = (separations + accessions) ÷ average.
- Average on roll = (beginning + end) ÷ 2; annual rate = period rate × 365 ÷ days (a quarter × 4).
- Causes: personal, unavoidable, avoidable (management can act on the avoidable ones).
- Costs: preventive (medical, welfare, retention benefits) and replacement (recruitment, training, breakage, inefficiency); find the optimum level.
- Profit forgone = (productive hours lost × sales per productive hour × P/V ratio) + settlement, recruitment, selection, training and rectification costs. (Count who got off the coach, not the new coach.)

## Lesson 4.4: Time rate, piece rate and the Halsey and Rowan premium plans

*Paying for time, for output, or for time plus a share of the time saved: the Halsey and Rowan formulas, which pays more when, and what each does to labour cost per unit.*

### Key idea

Premium plans guarantee time wages and share the time saved. Halsey: 50% of the time saved, at the time rate. Rowan: (time saved ÷ time allowed) × time wages. Equal when time taken is 50% of time allowed; Rowan pays more for moderate efficiency, Halsey for high efficiency.

### Formats and formulas

SH = time allowed (standard hours), AH = time taken (actual hours), R = time rate per hour.

**[formula] Time rate and piece rate**
$$\text{Wages} = \text{Time worked} \times \text{Rate} \qquad \text{Wages} = \text{Units} \times \text{Rate per unit}$$
(SM 3.28)

**[formula] Halsey premium plan**
$$\text{Earnings} = AH \times R \qquad + 50\% \times (SH - AH) \times R$$
(SM 3.28)

**[formula] Rowan premium plan**
$$\text{Earnings} = AH \times R \qquad + \frac{SH - AH}{SH} \times AH \times R$$
(SM 3.30)

**[formula] Effective hourly rate and cost per unit**
$$\text{Effective rate} = \frac{\text{Total earnings}}{\text{Hours worked}} \qquad \text{Cost per unit} = \frac{\text{Total earnings}}{\text{Units}}$$

**[formula] Efficiency**
$$\text{Efficiency (\%)} = \frac{\text{Time allowed}}{\text{Time taken}} \times 100$$
Time allowed = time allowed as per standard for the work done (SM 3.43).

**[exam] The traps ICAI set**
- "Time saved" is SH − AH; Rowan multiplies time saved ÷ **time allowed** by **time wages** (AH × R), not by time saved × R.
- The promise test (May 2025 Q2(b)): compare total wages under each plan with present earnings × 1.15; only the plan that meets it is advisable.
- Efficiency is time allowed ÷ time taken (122.07% in May 2025 Q2(b)), not the other way round.
- Round only at the end: ICAI shows ₹1,38,854.40 and ₹35.424 an hour in full.

**[trick] The 50% line**
Work out AH ÷ SH first. Above 50%: Rowan pays more. Exactly 50%: equal, and Rowan's bonus is at its maximum. Below 50%: Halsey pays more. Conditions: same rate and same standard for both plans, and Halsey at 50%.

### Answer frame

### "Describe how wages are calculated under the Rowan and Halsey systems"

For each plan: the standard time and the guarantee; the bonus rule in ICAI's words; the formula; a two-line example; and one advantage and one disadvantage.

### "Distinguish between the Halsey and Rowan plans"

| Point | Halsey | Rowan |
|---|---|---|
| Bonus | 50% of time saved × rate | Time saved ÷ time allowed × time wages |
| Worker's share of the saving | Fixed at 50% | Equals AH ÷ SH: falls as the saving grows |
| Highest bonus | Keeps rising with time saved | At time taken = 50% of time allowed |
| Earnings can double? | Yes: hourly earnings reach twice the rate once time taken is a third of time allowed or less (loose standards) | Never, even with bad rate setting (SM 3.30) |
| Better for | Highly efficient workers (saving above 50%) | Moderately efficient workers (saving below 50%) |
| Simplicity | Simple | A bit complicated |

### "When is the time rate system preferred?"

ICAI's three cases (SM 3.27): services that cannot be directly or tangibly measured (helpers, supervisory and clerical staff); highly skilled jobs; output whose pace is independent of the operator (automatic chemical plants).

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: Rowan's bonus built on the time saved** **Fix:** the fraction multiplies the **time wages**, time taken × rate.
- **The negative bonus** **Fix:** premium plans guarantee day wages; with no time saved the bonus is nil (SM 3.28).
- **The effective rate on the wrong hours** **Fix:** the effective hourly rate is earnings ÷ hours actually worked (Joseph under Halsey: ₹2,100 ÷ 12 = ₹175, not ÷ 30 = ₹70).
- **Calendar hours for a clock-time question** **Fix:** count only working hours in each day.

### Recap

- Time rate: time worked × rate; for unmeasurable services, highly skilled jobs, operator-independent output.
- Piece rate: units × rate per unit, independent of time.
- Premium plans: standard time set, time wages guaranteed, time saved shared.
- Halsey: AH × R + 50% × (SH − AH) × R.
- Rowan: AH × R + (SH − AH) ÷ SH × AH × R; the worker can never double his earnings.
- Equal when AH = 50% of SH; Rowan more above it (moderate efficiency), Halsey more below it. Rowan's bonus is greatest at 50%.
- Efficiency % = time allowed ÷ time taken × 100. (Halsey halves the cake; Rowan slices it by time worked.)

## Lesson 4.5: Direct expenses

*Costs other than direct material and direct employee cost that can be traced to one product, job or service: what they are, how they are measured, and where they sit in prime cost.*

### Key idea

Direct expenses are the third part of prime cost: costs other than direct material and direct employee cost that are directly traceable, in an economically feasible way, to a product, job or service. Measure them net of rebates, with non-creditable taxes; spread lump sums over the output that benefits; treat trivial amounts as overheads.

### Formats and formulas

**[definition] Direct expenses**
Expenses other than direct material cost and direct employee cost, which are incurred to manufacture a product or for provision of service and can be directly traced in an economically feasible manner to a cost object. (SM 3.52)

**[formula] Amortising a one-time payment**
$$\text{Per unit} = \frac{\text{One-time payment}}{\text{Estimated output}}$$
Charge for the period = charge per unit × units produced in the period. Use the estimated benefit instead of units when the benefit is not measured in output (SM 3.53).

**[formula] Measuring a direct expense**
$$\text{Cost} = \text{Price} - \text{Rebate} \qquad + \text{Non-creditable taxes} + \text{Commission}$$
Price = invoice or agreed price; add any other directly attributable cost (SM 3.53).

**[exam] The traps ICAI set**
- "Specific equipment" or a job's own machine is direct; a plant used by all is overheads.
- GST or duty **with** input credit stays out; **without** credit it is part of the cost.
- Sales commission, marketing manager's salary and office attendants' salary are never direct expenses (SM 3.54, 3.67).
- A rate per hour or per unit (hire per labour hour, royalty per unit) is multiplied by the hours or units of the period.

### Answer frame

### "What are direct expenses? How are they measured and treated in cost accounts?"

1. **Definition** in ICAI's words (SM 3.52).
2. **Examples**: royalty; hire of specific equipment; product or service specific design or drawing; product or service specific software; other directly related expenses. The list is not exhaustive.
3. **Measurement**: invoice or agreed price net of rebate or discount, including duties and taxes without input credit, commission and other directly attributable costs; job work at agreed price plus materials supplied and incidental expenses.
4. **Treatment**: part of prime cost; lump sums amortised over estimated production or benefit; insignificant amounts as overheads.

### A classification question

Answer in ICAI's layout: a table of the direct expenses by job or product, with the working of each figure (hours × rate, packs × royalty, job work plus materials), then numbered notes giving the treatment of every item left out ("administrative overhead is indirect expense"; "commission paid to sales staff comes under selling expenses"), as the Suggested Answers do. The notes carry marks: a bare total of direct expenses earns little.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the design charged to the launch month** **Fix:** a one-time payment is amortised over the estimated production that benefits: ₹0.60 a bottle.
- **GST that will come back** **Fix:** only duties and taxes for which input credit is **not** available are part of the cost (SM 3.53).
- **Factory wages under direct expenses** **Fix:** they are direct employee cost, the second element of prime cost, together with the employer's contributions on them (SM 3.67; RTP September 2026 Q8): see [[cma.emp.payroll|attendance, payroll and the elements of wages]].
- **The sales commission that became prime cost** **Fix:** it is a selling overhead (SM 3.67); the commission that *is* part of a direct expense is one paid to acquire that expense, like the agent's fee for hiring the bottling line.

### Recap

- Direct expenses: costs other than direct material and direct employee cost, incurred for a product or service and traceable to it in an economically feasible manner.
- ICAI's examples: royalty; hire of specific equipment; product- or service-specific design or drawing; specific software; other directly related expenses (not exhaustive). The Cost Sheet chapter adds utilities such as power and fuel, technical know-how fees and amortised moulds and patterns (SM 6.4).
- Measured at invoice or agreed price net of rebate or discount, plus taxes without input credit, commission and other directly attributable costs.
- Job work: agreed price, plus materials supplied by the principal and incidental expenses.
- Part of prime cost; one-time payments amortised over estimated production or benefit; insignificant amounts may be overheads.
- Not direct expenses: factory wages (employee cost), administration, sales commission, marketing salaries, general plant hire. (If only this product ran up the bill, it is a direct expense.)
