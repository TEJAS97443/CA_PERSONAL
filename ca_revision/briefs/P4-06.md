## Lesson 6.1: Activities, cost pools, cost drivers and the cost hierarchy

*Why one labour-hour rate miscosts products, and how ABC traces overheads to activities, collects them in cost pools and charges cost objects through cost drivers at four levels of the cost hierarchy.*

### Key idea

Overheads are caused by activities, not by labour hours. ABC traces overheads to activities first, then charges each cost object for the activities it actually uses, through cost drivers.

### Formats and formulas

*Reproduce the formula and both driver tables from memory: ICAI asks for examples of cost drivers as 5-mark parts.*

**[formula] Activity cost driver rate (SM 5.10)**
$$\text{Activity cost driver rate} \qquad = \frac{\text{Total cost of activity}}{\text{Activity driver}}$$
The activity driver is the total quantity of the driver for the period (all set-ups, all orders). Overhead charged to a cost object = the sum, over the activities it uses, of driver rate × driver quantity it consumed.

**Cost drivers by business function** (SM 5.4–5.5):

| Business function | Cost drivers |
|---|---|
| Research and development | Number of research projects; personnel hours on a project |
| Design of products, services and procedures | Number of products in design; number of parts per product; number of engineering hours |
| Customer service | Number of service calls; number of products serviced; hours spent on servicing products |
| Marketing | Number of advertisements; number of sales personnel; sales revenue |
| Distribution | Number of units distributed; number of customers |

**Cost drivers by activity cost pool** in a production department (SM 5.13):

| Activity cost pool | Related cost driver |
|---|---|
| Ordering and receiving materials | Number of purchase orders |
| Setting up machines | Number of set-ups |
| Machining | Machine hours |
| Assembling | Number of parts |
| Inspecting and testing | Number of tests |
| Painting | Number of parts |
| Supervising | Direct labour hours |

**[trick] One more what?**
To place an activity in the hierarchy, ask: its cost rises if we make one more what? One more unit: unit level. One more batch: batch level. One more product in the range: product level. None of these: facility level. Condition: judge the activity as the question describes it, not by its name.

### Answer frame

### "Distinguish between ABC and traditional absorption costing" (SM 5.7)

**[compare] | Point | Activity based costing | Traditional absorption costing |**
|---|---|---|
| 1. Grouping | Overheads related to activities and grouped into activity cost pools | Overheads related to cost centres or departments |
| 2. Realism | Costs related to activities, hence more realistic | Costs related to cost centres, hence not realistic of cost behaviour |
| 3. Drivers | Activity-wise cost drivers determined | Time (hours) assumed to be the only cost driver in all departments |
| 4. Rates | Activity-wise recovery rates; no single overhead recovery rate | Multiple rates (one per department) or a single overhead recovery rate |
| 5. Cost objects | Customers, products, services, departments, etc. | Cost units: products, jobs or hours |
| 6. Control | Essential activities simplified, unnecessary ones eliminated: aids cost control | Cost centres cannot be eliminated: not suitable for cost control |

### Other questions, in ICAI's order

- **Usefulness or suitability** (4 marks): the four headed situations of SM 5.3, each with one line of explanation.
- **Cost driver**: definition; the two categories with their uses; then two drivers for each function asked, from the SM 5.4–5.5 table.
- **How product costs are determined in ABC, or how an ABC system is developed**: the five stages in order (identify activities; relate overheads to activities to create cost pools using resource cost drivers; spread support activities over primary activities; determine activity cost drivers; calculate driver rates and charge cost objects), then the numbers.
- **Levels of activities**: the four levels, each with ICAI's meaning and one example.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the inspection that was batch level because it said "inspection"** **Fix:** classify by what the cost varies with. Testing every unit is unit level; testing the first item of each batch is batch level (SM 5.8).
- **Charging products with a resource cost driver** **Fix:** a resource cost driver (supervisors' time on set-ups, receiving and testing) takes a resource to the activities: stage one. Products are then charged through activity cost drivers (set-ups, tests, deliveries) at each activity's rate.
- **Dividing a pool by one product's driver** **Fix:** the rate is the pool ÷ the total driver for the period (60 set-ups), then multiplied by each product's own quantity. Check: the products' charges add back to the pool.
- **"ABC increased the overheads"** **Fix:** ABC redistributes the same total; under-costed and over-costed amounts are equal and opposite. If your totals differ from the ledger, a pool or a rate is wrong.

### Recap

- ABC assigns overheads to activities, then to cost objects according to their use of activities (SM 5.3).
- Two stages: resources to activities by **resource cost drivers**; activities to cost objects by **activity cost drivers**.
- Activity: an event that incurs cost. Cost pool: costs with the same cause and effect relationship. Cost driver: a factor that causes a change in the cost of an activity.
- Activity cost driver rate = total cost of activity ÷ activity driver.
- Hierarchy: unit (varies with units), batch (with batches), product (supports each product), facility (sustains the plant).
- Useful when overheads are high, products many, non-volume activities significant, competition stiff.
- Same total overhead, spread differently: small-batch products often turn out under-costed by a labour-hour rate. (Split the dinner bill by the orders.)

## Lesson 6.2: Product costs under ABC versus traditional absorption

*Building ICAI's two cost statements side by side (one overhead rate against activity driver rates), reading the cost distortion, costing unused capacity and measuring customer-level operating income.*

### Key idea

Prime cost does not change; only overheads are re-spread. Cost distortion = traditional cost − ABC cost: positive means over-costed, negative means under-costed, and across all products the distortion adds up to zero.

### Formats and formulas

*Reproduce both statements from memory. ICAI's suggested answers show the driver rate and the quantity in brackets on every overhead line.*

**Statement of cost per unit: traditional absorption** (SM 5.16, 5.44)

**Statement of cost: activity based costing** (SM 5.17, 5.44–5.45)

| Direct materials | xxx | xxx |

| Machine costs @ ₹… per machine hour (hours) | xxx | xxx |

| **Total cost** | **xxx** | **xxx** |
| **Cost per unit** (total cost ÷ units) | **xxx** | **xxx** |

Working notes before the statement: driver totals (batches, orders, inspections, hours) and the driver rate table (cost pool, driver, quantity, rate).

**[formula] Activity cost driver rate and the product's overhead**
$$\text{Rate} = \frac{\text{Cost of activity pool}}{\text{Total quantity of the driver}} \qquad \text{Overhead}_{\text{product}} \qquad = \sum \text{Rate} \times \text{Driver quantity used}$$
On a capacity basis the denominator is the driver's capacity, not its actual use.

**[formula] Cost distortion**
$$\text{Cost distortion per unit} \qquad = \text{Traditional cost} - \text{ABC cost}$$
Both costs are per unit. Positive: over-costed by the traditional system; negative: under-costed. ICAI prints the sign: "(−) 40.42" for the under-costed product (SM 5.26).

**[formula] Cost of unused capacity**
$$\text{Cost of unused capacity} \qquad = \text{Cost of activity} - \text{Cost allocated} \qquad = \text{Unused driver units} \times \text{Rate}$$
Cost allocated = the driver units products actually used × the capacity rate.

**[exam] The traps**
- Driver data per batch or per unit: multiply up to totals first (the SM's Practical Problem 1).
- Overheads given as ledger items with sharing ratios: distribute them to the activities before computing rates (September 2024 Q3(a)).
- "Calculates activity cost rates based on cost driver capacity": divide by capacity, not by use (May 2025).
- Percentages of revenue: on revenue at actual price, net of discount (May 2024; September 2026 MCQ 3 says "net of discount").
- State the comment ICAI expects: which product is under- or over-costed, and why; ICAI's own line is "The Activity Based Costs appear to be more precise" (SM 5.17).

**[trick] Check the zero**
Total overhead under ABC must equal the traditional total, so the distortions × units must add up to zero (and with capacity rates, allocated cost + unused capacity cost = total activity cost). If they do not, a driver total or a rate is wrong. Condition: both systems absorb the same overheads; a question that gives a different total for ABC must be read as given.

### Answer frame

### A full-marks numerical answer, in ICAI's order

1. **Working notes**: driver totals (batches, orders, inspections, hours); the split of overheads into pools if percentages or ratios are given; the driver rate table (pool, driver basis, quantity, rate).
2. **Statement 1**: cost per unit under the traditional method (rate shown).
3. **Statement 2**: cost under ABC with "@ ₹ rate (quantities)" on each overhead line; total cost and cost per unit.
4. **Comparison**: cost distortion with its sign, under- or over-costed.
5. **Comment**: reason for the difference (overheads borne by each product differ; batch-level and product-level activities) and the decision asked (price, mix, customer action).

"Masala Gold ABC cost ₹1,200, so it is under-costed."

**Passes**

**Full marks**

Working notes for driver totals and rates, both statements, the distortion with "(−)" for under-costed products and the check that it nets to zero, and a two-line comment on pricing.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: per-run data used as if it were the total** **Fix:** tests = 40 runs × 4 = 160; charge = ₹2,56,000. Where a question gives a batch size instead of the number of runs, batches = units ÷ batch size first.
- **The rate on actual use when the question says capacity** **Fix:** divide by capacity (12,000 hours: ₹80) and report the unused capacity separately.
- **Dividing ABC overhead by units sold** Cost per unit is total cost ÷ units **produced**. Units sold matter only for sales, cost of goods sold and closing stock.
- **Percentages on list-price revenue** **Fix:** supermarkets ₹6,99,000 ÷ ₹66,00,000 = 10.59%, not ÷ ₹75,00,000 = 9.32%.

### Recap

- Prime cost is the same under both systems; only production overheads move.
- Traditional: overhead per unit = absorption rate × base per unit. ABC: driver rate × driver quantity per product ÷ units produced.
- Turn per-batch and per-unit data into totals first; batches = units ÷ batch size.
- Cost distortion = traditional − ABC: + over-costed, (−) under-costed; the total distortion is nil.
- Capacity-based rates: allocated + cost of unused capacity = activity cost; unused capacity = unused units × rate.
- Customer-level operating income = revenue at actual price − cost of goods sold − customer-level activity costs; % on revenue at actual price.
- Small-batch, low-volume products tend to be under-costed by volume rates (SM 5.26). (The ground floor was paying the fourteenth floor's bills.)

## Lesson 6.3: Activity based management and budgeting; benefits and limits

*What ABC is good for and where it struggles; how ABM uses activity costs to cut non-value-added work, re-engineer, benchmark and measure; and how ABB runs ABC backwards to budget resources.*

### Key idea

ABC measures what activities cost; ABM manages the activities (keep value-added, remove or reduce non-value-added); ABB budgets by working back from the planned quantity of work to the resources it needs.

### Formats and formulas

*Reproduce ICAI's budget statement layout (Illustration 4, SM 5.28–5.30): working note of budgeted activity costs first, then rates, then the cost per unit of each product.*

**[formula] Budgeted activity cost**
$$\text{Budgeted activity cost} \qquad = \text{Fixed part} \qquad + \text{Present variable part} \times \text{Growth factor}$$
The growth factor is given ("to double": 2; "to increase by 80%": 1.8), or is budgeted level ÷ present level when the variable part moves with the driver. Stepped costs are added step by step ("₹1 lakh more for every one lakh more statements").

**[formula] Resources required for the budgeted work**
$$\text{Resource units required} \qquad = \frac{\text{Budgeted driver quantity}}{\text{Capacity of one resource unit}} \qquad \text{(rounded up to whole units)}$$
People, vehicles and machines come in whole units, so the budget carries some spare capacity: capacity provided minus capacity used, valued at the capacity rate, is the cost of unused capacity (see [[cma.abc.compute|capacity-based rates]]).

**[formula] Budgeted activity rate and product cost**
$$\text{Budgeted activity rate} \qquad = \frac{\text{Budgeted activity cost}}{\text{Budgeted driver quantity}} \qquad \text{Budgeted cost per unit} \qquad = \frac{\sum \text{Rate} \times \text{Driver quantity}}{\text{Budgeted units of product}}$$

### Answer frame

### "What is ABM? How can it be used in business?" (5 marks)

1. **Meaning**: the cost management application of ABC; the use of ABC to manage costs at activity level; a discipline focusing on the efficient and effective management of activities as the route to continuously improving the value received by customers; it utilises ABC information.
2. **Cost reduction**: identify costs against activities; streamline, reduce or eliminate, especially where no value is added.
3. **Business process re-engineering**: examine processes and make substantial changes; measure performance and cost of output; find efficiency improvements.
4. **Benchmarking**: compare ABC-derived activity costs across segments; needs uniform definition and measurement.
5. **Performance measurement**: monitor the efficiency and effectiveness of activities to face competitors and manage costs.

If the question asks for the analyses instead: cost driver analysis; activity analysis (VA with ICAI's example, NVA with ICAI's examples); performance analysis.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the accounts department declared non-value-added** **Fix:** classify with ICAI's definition and examples. NVA is work "not valued by the external or internal customer" that does not improve the quality or function of the product but adds cost, such as moving materials or set-ups; the answer then says reduce, streamline or eliminate, not "delete everything the customer does not see". The accounts department serves internal users (management, lenders, the board) and the law requires it, so it is not NVA merely because no chai drinker sees it; ABM can still make it cheaper.
- **Mixing up the three lists** **Fix:** implementation has five stages (5.18); ABM in business has four uses (5.20–5.21); ABM analysis has three types (5.20).
- **Budgeting the variable part on the whole present cost** **Fix:** fixed ₹2,50,000 + variable ₹2,50,000 × 3 = ₹10,00,000 (SM 5.29).
- **Hall of Shame: the budget that hired 12.33 riders** **Fix:** ABB estimates the resources the planned work requires, and riders, analysts and vans come in whole units: round up, budget the cost of the whole resource, and show the spare capacity (here 4,800 deliveries, ₹1,44,000) separately instead of hiding it in the services' rates.

### Recap

- ABC advantages: more accurate costing; logical overhead allocation; better pricing; unit cost; identifies NVA activities; helps multi-product firms; highlights problem areas.
- ABC limitations: more expensive; not helpful to small organisations; not for limited products; driver selection difficult.
- Implementing ABC: staff training, process specification, activity definition, activity driver selection, assigning cost.
- ABM: the cost management application of ABC; analyses: cost driver, activity (VA/NVA), performance.
- ABM in business: cost reduction, business process re-engineering, benchmarking, performance measurement.
- VA example: polishing furniture. NVA examples: moving materials, machine set-up for a production run.
- ABB: the reversing of the ABC process; key elements: type, quantity and cost of work; benefits: accurate forecasts, rapid plans at varying volumes, less needless rework. (The accounts department stays.)
