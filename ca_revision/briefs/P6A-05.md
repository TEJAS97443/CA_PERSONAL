## Lesson 5.1: Capital structure theories: NI, NOI, traditional and Modigliani-Miller

*Does borrowing change what a company is worth? NI says yes, NOI and MM (no tax) say no, the traditional view says up to a point, and MM with tax says debt adds debt × tax rate.*

### Key idea

A firm is worth its operating profit capitalised at its overall cost of capital, so the debt-equity mix matters only if it changes that overall cost. NI: it falls as debt rises. NOI and MM without tax: it stays constant, because the cost of equity rises just enough to cancel the cheap debt. Traditional: it first falls, then rises, so there is an optimum. MM with tax: borrowing adds value equal to the tax the interest saves, the debt times the tax rate.

**[formula] Overall cost of capital**
$$K_o = K_d \times \frac{D}{D+S} + K_e \times \frac{S}{D+S} \qquad = \frac{\text{EBIT}}{V}$$
Ko is the WACC; D and S are market values of debt and equity (SM 5.3). With tax, use the post-tax Kd in the weighted average.

**[formula] MM without tax: cost of equity of a levered firm**
$$K_e = K_o + (K_o - K_d) \times \frac{\text{Debt}}{\text{Equity}}$$
Ko equals the Ke of the identical unlevered firm (SM 5.16).

**[formula] MM with tax: value of a levered firm**
$$V_g = V_u + \text{TB} \qquad V_u = \frac{\text{EBIT}\,(1-t)}{K_{eu}}$$
TB = present value of tax shields; for permanent debt, debt × t (SM 5.18, 5.69).

**[formula] MM with tax: cost of equity and WACC of a levered firm**
$$K_{eg} = K_{eu} + (K_{eu} - K_d) \qquad \times \frac{\text{Debt}\,(1-t)}{\text{Equity}} \qquad K_{og} = K_{eu}\,(1 - tL)$$
Kd here is the pre-tax rate of interest; L = debt ÷ (debt + equity) at market value (SM 5.26–5.27).

**Arbitrage in three steps** (the layout of ICAI's Suggested Answers): 1. Valuation of firms (EBIT, interest, earnings for equity, Ke, S, D, V); 2. Investment and borrowings (sell, borrow or lend, buy); 3. Change in return (new income, less interest, less old income = incremental income due to arbitrage).

**[definition] Arbitrage**
Arbitrage refers to buying asset or security at lower price in one market and selling it at a higher price in another market. As a result, equilibrium is attained in different markets. (SM 5.16–5.17)

**[exam] The traps in those answers**
- MM with tax: equity after the change = Vg − debt. The Suggested Answers subtract the debt that replaced the equity.
- An all-equity value and Ke given, with tax: back out EBIT = (value × Ke) ÷ (1 − t) first, as in May 2025 Q2(b).
- Arbitrage: borrow (or lend) your percentage of the levered firm's debt at the same rate; the answers say "to maintain the level of risk".
- The Suggested Answers round weights to two decimals, so the WACC can differ in the second decimal from the formula Keu (1 − tL): SM Illustration 10 shows 19.11% and 19.10%, and the May 2025 answer prints 16.93% with a note that unrounded weights give 16.91%.

**[trick] Which way does the arbitrage run?**
Always sell the firm with the **higher** total value V and buy the lower. If the levered firm is dearer, borrow on personal account; if the unlevered firm is dearer, lend (buy the levered firm's debt) in the levered firm's debt-equity proportion. Valid in MM's no-tax world, where the two firms differ only in leverage.

### Answer frame

### Theory questions and the points that earn marks

**"Explain the assumptions of the Modigliani-Miller theory"** (SM 5.15): perfect capital markets (information free, no transaction costs); rational investors; equivalent risk classes based on business risk; no corporate taxes; plus the general assumptions (two sources, constant total financing, 100% payout, constant business risk, perpetual life).

**"Describe the NOI approach and its assumptions"** (SM 5.5, 5.11): NOI = EBIT; capital structure irrelevant; Ko independent of leverage; cheaper debt offset by a rising Ke because equity investors seek compensation for greater risk; Ko and Kd constant, Ke rises; value = EBIT ÷ Ko; the seven general assumptions.

**"What is the hierarchy of financing under the pecking order theory, and why does it exist?"** (SM 5.29; May 2025): internal finance, then debt (secured, unsecured, hybrid), then new equity last; it exists because of asymmetric information, as managers prefer the source that reveals the least information. ICAI's 2025 answer adds: to minimise the cost of capital, keep financial control and avoid negative market signals.

**"Explain the trade-off theory"** (SM 5.27–5.28): balance benefit of debt (tax) against costs (financial distress: bankruptcy and non-bankruptcy costs; agency costs); marginal benefit falls and marginal cost rises with debt; optimum capital structure; explains mixed financing.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the arbitrageur who forgot to borrow** **Fix:** borrow your share of the levered firm's debt (here ₹50,000) and invest it too; the interest on it comes off the new income.
- **The value of the firm passed off as the value of equity** **Fix:** equity = Vg − debt = ₹1,560 lakh, and Ke = 21.38%.
- **NI and NOI swapped** **Fix:** read which rate is given: Ke given, start from earnings for equity (NI); Ko given, start from EBIT ÷ Ko (NOI, MM).
- **Post-tax Kd in the wrong formula** **Fix:** pre-tax Kd inside (Keu − Kd); post-tax Kd in the weighted average.

### Recap

- Value of the firm = EBIT ÷ Ko; Ko = Kd × D/V + Ke × S/V. Capital structure matters only if it moves Ko.
- NI: Ke, Kd constant; S = NI ÷ Ke; V = S + D; Ko falls; use maximum debt.
- NOI: Ko constant; V = EBIT ÷ Ko; S = V − D; Ke = (EBIT − I) ÷ S rises; mix irrelevant.
- Traditional: Kd flat then up, Ke gradual then speedy; Ko falls then rises; optimum at the lowest Ko.
- MM 1958: perfect markets, rational investors, equivalent risk classes, no tax; Ke = Ko + (Ko − Kd) D/E; arbitrage with personal leverage equalises values.
- MM 1963: Vg = Vu + D × t; Ke = Keu + (Keu − Kd) D (1 − t)/E; Ko = Keu (1 − tL).
- Trade-off: tax benefit against distress and agency costs gives an optimum. Pecking order: internal funds, debt, equity last.
- One samosa, however you slice it, unless the taxman only eats from one plate.

## Lesson 5.2: EBIT-EPS analysis, indifference point and financial break-even

*How the same EBIT gives different EPS under equity, debt and preference financing; the financial break-even point where EPS is zero; and the indifference point where two plans tie.*

### Key idea

Hold EBIT fixed, deduct each plan's interest, tax and preference dividend, and divide by its number of shares: that is EBIT-EPS analysis. The financial break-even point is the EBIT where EPS is zero; the indifference point is the EBIT where two plans' EPS are equal. Expect EBIT above the indifference point: prefer the plan with more debt. Below it: prefer equity.

### Formats and formulas

*Reproduce the EPS statement from memory; every EBIT-EPS answer starts with it.*

| Statement of EPS under alternative financing plans | Plan I (₹) | Plan II (₹) |
|---|---:|---:|
| EBIT | xxx | xxx |
| Less: Interest (existing + new) | (xxx) | (xxx) |
| EBT (PBT) | xxx | xxx |
| Less: Tax | (xxx) | (xxx) |
| PAT | xxx | xxx |
| Less: Preference dividend | (xxx) | (xxx) |
| Earnings available to equity shareholders | xxx | xxx |
| Number of equity shares (existing + new) | xxx | xxx |
| **EPS** | **xxx** | **xxx** |
| PE ratio | x | x |
| **MPS = EPS × PE ratio** | **xxx** | **xxx** |

**[formula] Earnings per share**
$$\text{EPS} = \frac{(\text{EBIT} - I)(1-t) - \text{PD}}{N}$$
I = interest, t = tax rate, PD = preference dividend, N = number of equity shares (existing + amount raised ÷ issue price).

**[formula] Financial break-even point**
$$\text{Financial BEP} = I + \frac{\text{PD}}{1-t}$$
If the company has no preference shares, the financial BEP is simply the interest (SM 6.19).

**[formula] Indifference point between two plans**
$$\frac{(\text{EBIT} - I_1)(1-t)}{E_1} = \qquad \frac{(\text{EBIT} - I_2)(1-t)}{E_2}$$
E₁, E₂ = number of equity shares and I₁, I₂ = interest under plans 1 and 2 (SM 5.37). With preference shares, deduct PD after tax: (EBIT − I₂)(1 − t) − PD₂ in the numerator.

**[formula] Market price per share**
$$\text{MPS} = \text{EPS} \times \text{PE ratio} \qquad = \frac{\text{EPS}}{K_e}$$
(SM 5.32)

**[definition] Financial break-even point**
Financial break-even point is the minimum level of EBIT needed to satisfy all the fixed financial charges i.e. interests and preference dividends. It denotes the level of EBIT for which the company's EPS equals zero. (SM 5.36)

**[definition] Indifference point**
The EBIT at which the EPS lines of two alternatives of financing intersect: between the two given alternatives (regardless of leverage in the financial plans), EPS would be the same at that level of EBIT. Also called the EPS equivalency point. (SM 5.37)

**[trick] The one-line indifference formula**
Write F for each plan's financial BEP (interest + PD ÷ (1 − t)) and E for its number of shares. Then EBIT at indifference = (E₁ × F₂ − E₂ × F₁) ÷ (E₁ − E₂). Tapri, A against C: (2 × 2.9333 − 1.5 × 0) ÷ 0.5 = ₹11.73 crore. Conditions: the same tax rate in both plans and E₁ ≠ E₂ (equal shares means no point or every point).

### Answer frame

### The theory questions

**"Discuss financial break-even and EBIT-EPS indifference analysis"** (MTP Series I for January 2026; SM 5.36–5.37):

1. Financial BEP: the minimum EBIT needed to satisfy all fixed financial charges (interest and preference dividend); EBIT at which EPS = 0; formula interest + preference dividend ÷ (1 − t).
2. Below it EPS is negative; if expected EBIT is above it, more fixed-cost financing can be taken, otherwise equity is preferred.
3. EBIT-EPS indifference: the EBIT at which EPS is the same under two financing plans; formula (EBIT − I₁)(1 − t) ÷ E₁ = (EBIT − I₂)(1 − t) ÷ E₂, symbols defined.
4. It is a vital tool for designing the optimal capital structure.

**"Explain the relationship between EBIT-EPS-MPS"** (MTP Series II for September 2024; SM 5.33): the objective is the highest MPS, which depends on EPS; at a given EBIT, EPS differs with the financing mix because of fixed financial charges; if the return on assets exceeds the cost of fixed-charge funds, EPS rises (favourable leverage, trading on equity), otherwise it falls; debt is preferred to preference shares because of lower cost and tax-deductible interest.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: new shares at face value** **Fix:** new shares = amount raised ÷ issue price (face value + premium).
- **Preference dividend treated like interest** **Fix:** it comes out of PAT; in the financial BEP, divide it by (1 − t).
- **The forgotten old loan** **Fix:** every plan carries the existing interest and the existing shares; only the new money differs.
- **Highest EPS crowned the winner** When the question gives a PE ratio that changes with leverage, the winner is the highest MPS. In Example 2 the all-debt plan has the best EPS and the worst MPS.

### Recap

- EPS = [(EBIT − I)(1 − t) − PD] ÷ N; new shares = amount ÷ issue price.
- Same EBIT for every plan; existing interest and shares go into every plan.
- Financial BEP = I + PD ÷ (1 − t): the EBIT at which EPS = 0.
- Indifference point: (EBIT − I₁)(1 − t) ÷ E₁ = (EBIT − I₂)(1 − t) ÷ E₂; deduct PD after tax where there are preference shares.
- Above the indifference point: more debt gives more EPS. Below: more equity.
- Same number of shares: no indifference point (different after-tax costs) or every EBIT is one (same costs).
- Debt beats preference shares: its rate is usually lower and its interest is tax-deductible, so it wins even at the same rate.
- Different PE ratios: choose the highest MPS, not the highest EPS. (The pass beats the card only if you ride enough.)

## Lesson 5.3: Capital structure planning, determinants and over- and under-capitalisation

*What capital structure is, the control-risk-cost balance and seven principles that shape it, how an optimal structure is found, and the causes, consequences and remedies of over- and under-capitalisation.*

### Key idea

Capital structure is planned to balance control, risk and cost so as to maximise the value of the firm, guided by seven principles (trading on equity, growth and stability of sales, cost, risk, control, flexibility, other considerations). Over-capitalisation (capital more than earnings justify) and under-capitalisation (capital less than earnings warrant) are both bad; over-capitalisation is the more dangerous.

### Exam angle

### What ICAI asks

- **Planning in a case**: a debt-free company raising ₹10 crore: what practical factors would you evaluate before choosing debt, equity or a mix? (MTP Series II for September 2025, 2 marks; ICAI's answer: cost of capital, control, current interest rate and market conditions, cash flow stability).

The old-scheme Paper 8 papers read for practice ask the same things: "The firm has more capital than its requirements. What is this situation called? Give two consequences" or "What are the important factors considered for deciding the source and quantum of capital?" (November 2022, 2 marks); "What are the causes of over-capitalisation?" (November 2023, 2 marks; reused word for word in the RTP for January 2026).

**[exam] What the Suggested Answers give marks for**
- ICAI's lists, in ICAI's order and nearly in ICAI's words: five causes, four consequences, four remedies of over-capitalisation; three remedies of under-capitalisation with bonus shares "the most appropriate".
- For a scenario ("more capital than its requirements"), the name of the situation first, then the points.
- For "factors for source and quantum", the three: control, risk, cost.

**[trick] Over is cured by shrinking, under by spreading**
Over-capitalisation is cured by reducing capital or claims (reorganise, buy back, reduce debenture and creditor claims, reduce share value). Under-capitalisation is cured by spreading the same earnings over more shares or more face value (split, bonus, higher par value). Use it to sort the remedies; the lists themselves must be written in ICAI's words.

### Answer frame

### "Explain over-capitalisation. What are its causes and consequences?" (4 marks)

Meaning, with the chief sign (falling dividend, interest and share value) → all five causes → all four consequences, each in ICAI's words as listed in section 6 above. In a scenario ("a very high capital base but low EPS"), name the situation in the first line.

### "Write a note on under-capitalisation"

Meaning (with the secret reserves case) → three consequences → five effects → three remedies → one line: less dangerous than over-capitalisation, a problem of adjusting capital structure.

---

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the bonus issue for an over-capitalised company** **Fix:** over-capitalisation needs reorganisation, buyback, reduction of debenture and creditor claims, or reduction in the value of shares.
- **The two definitions swapped** **Fix:** over = more capital than needed, earnings insufficient; under = capitalisation lower than the earning capacity warrants.
- **Under-capitalisation read as "short of cash"** **Fix:** in ICAI's sense it is a company whose earnings are high for its small book capital, often because of secret reserves; its symptoms are a high dividend rate and a high market value.
- **Only "cost" in a capital structure answer** **Fix:** a full answer balances cost against risk and control (the three factors) and, for "factors affecting", walks through all seven principles, ending in a compromise.

### Recap

- Capital structure: the combination of equity shareholders' fund, preference share capital and long-term external debts; balance control, risk and cost to maximise value.
- The decision: forms of financing, amounts and proportions in total capitalisation.
- Seven principles in order: trading on equity, growth and stability of sales, cost, risk, control, flexibility, other considerations.
- Optimal structure: highest value, lowest cost of capital; found by EBIT-EPS-MPS, indifference point and financial BEP analysis.
- Over-capitalisation: more capital than needed, earnings insufficient; 5 causes, 4 consequences, 4 remedies; chief sign: falling dividend and interest.
- Under-capitalisation: capitalisation below what earnings warrant (secret reserves); high dividend rate and share value; remedies: split, bonus (most appropriate), higher par value.
- Over-capitalisation is the more dangerous. (The empty wedding hall is the real worry; the busy chai stall only needs bigger books.)
