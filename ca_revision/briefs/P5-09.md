## Lesson 9.1: Government audit and the C&AG

*How the C&AG audits public money for Parliament: the Constitution's safeguards, the C&AG's duties and powers, the five standards of expenditure audit, receipts, stores and government companies.*

### Key idea

The C&AG audits public money on behalf of Parliament and the State Legislatures. Expenditure audit applies five standards: rules and orders, sanctions, provision of funds, propriety and performance. The first three check that spending was allowed, approved and provided for; propriety and performance catch spending that is perfectly regular and still wasteful or useless.

### Formats and formulas

### When is a body "substantially financed"?

**[formula] Substantially financed (C&AG's duty (iii))**
A body or authority is deemed substantially financed by grants or loans from the Consolidated Fund of India, of a State or of a Union Territory having a Legislative Assembly when, **in a financial year**, both hold (SM 9.10):

- the grant or loan is **not less than ₹25 lakh**; and
- the grant or loan is **not less than 75% of the total expenditure** of that body or authority.

Both tests must be met; "not less than" includes exactly ₹25 lakh or 75%.

### The five standards, in ICAI's words

*Reproduce this table from memory: answers quote the "that ..." clause, then name the audit.*

| Standard: the auditor ensures | Name of the audit |
|---|---|
| that the expenditure incurred conforms to the relevant provisions of the statutory enactment and is in accordance with the financial rules and regulations framed by the competent authority | Audit against rules and orders |
| that there is sanction, either special or general, accorded by competent authority authorising the expenditure | Audit of sanctions |
| that there is a provision of funds out of which expenditure can be incurred and the same has been authorised by competent authority | Audit against provision of funds |
| that the expenditure is incurred with due regard to broad and general principles of financial propriety | Propriety audit |
| that the programmes, schemes and projects where large financial expenditure has been incurred are being run economically and are yielding results expected of them | Performance audit |

### Answer frame

### "Identify the type of audit" case

1. **Relevant provision**: the basic standards of expenditure audit, in one sentence.
2. For each observation: **name the audit**, quote its "that ..." clause, apply the fact ("the Deputy Director's limit is ₹10 lakh").
3. **Conclusion**: one line per observation.

### "Explain the duties (or powers) of the C&AG"

Start with Article 149 and the 1971 Act; then duties (i) to (vii) as headings with one sentence each (the ₹25 lakh and 75% test under (iii)); then, if asked, powers (a) to (d).

### "Distinguish propriety audit and performance audit"

| Point | Propriety audit | Performance audit |
|---|---|---|
| Question asked | Was the expenditure wise, faithful and economical? | Is the programme run economically and yielding the results expected? |
| Typical subject | A transaction or item of expenditure | Programmes, schemes, projects with large financial expenditure |
| What it brings out | Improper, avoidable or ineffective expenditure, even when regular | Efficiency, economy and effectiveness |
| Study Material example | Building for a telephone exchange not used for it | Metro station sited where ridership is low (case study) |

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the report that went to the Prime Minister** **Fix:** Article 151: reports go to the **President** (Union) or **Governor** (State), who has them laid before the legislature. Article 150: the **President** prescribes the form, on the C&AG's advice.
- **Sanction or funds? Mixing up the two** **Fix:** no sanction by a competent authority, or a sanction beyond the officer's delegated powers: **audit of sanctions**. Spent on a purpose other than the grant's, or beyond the appropriation: **audit against provision of funds**.
- **Calling every waste "performance audit"** **Fix:** an avoidable or unproductive item of expenditure, regular but unwise (the locked museum, the unused building), is **propriety audit**. Whether a programme, scheme or project with large expenditure is economical and achieving its expected results is **performance audit**.
- **"The C&AG audits government companies himself, and the board must answer him"** **Fix:** the auditor the C&AG appoints and directs does the statutory audit; the C&AG may conduct a supplementary audit (within 60 days of receiving the report) or a test audit, and comment. The board explains the **auditor's** reservations under section 134(3), not the C&AG's comments.

### Recap

- The C&AG, appointed by the President and removable only like a Supreme Court Judge (two-thirds of members present and voting in each House), audits on behalf of Parliament and the State Legislatures.
- Article 149: duties and powers by law of Parliament (the 1971 Act). Article 150: form of accounts by the President on the C&AG's advice. Article 151: reports to the President or Governor, laid before the legislature.
- Duties, in order: compile accounts, general audit (Consolidated Fund expenditure, Contingency Funds, Public Accounts, departmental trading accounts), bodies substantially financed (grant ≥ ₹25 lakh **and** ≥ 75% of expenditure), specific-purpose grants, receipts, stores, government companies.
- Expenditure audit, in order: rules and orders, sanctions, provision of funds, propriety, performance.
- Propriety: improper, avoidable or ineffective expenditure even when regular; performance: efficiency, economy and effectiveness of large programmes.
- Receipts audit looks at the general more than the particular; its extent is fixed by the C&AG and is not negotiable.
- Government companies: the C&AG appoints and directs the auditor, may do a supplementary audit and comment within 60 days, and may order a test audit; comments go to members and the AGM; the board need not reply to them.
- Reports are factual; conclusions are left to the reader, recommendations to the PAC. (Nani reads the bills out; the council decides.)

## Lesson 9.2: Audit of local bodies

*What a municipality is, how it raises and spends money, why State external audit is the only control over its spending, the five audit objectives and the audit programme.*

### Key idea

A municipal council combines legislative and executive powers, so external audit by the State government is the only instrument of controlling municipal expenditure. The audit reports on fair accounts, financial controls, compliance, value for money, and detects and prevents error, fraud and misuse of resources.

### Exam angle

**[exam] Where this topic appeared**
In the one new-scheme document read for this lesson that asks it, **MTP September 2026 Series II Q5(a)** (5 marks), a municipal corporation with weak financial controls, non-compliance, projects without benefits and possible fraud: "Explain the important objectives of audit of municipal accounts and apply them". The Suggested Answer quotes the State-government control sentence, lists objectives (a) to (e) and then applies them in one paragraph.

Old-scheme papers asked it often: reporting areas when the Local Fund Audit Wing appoints you (PYQ December 2021 Q6(a)(ii), 4 marks, answered with the five objectives); the three revenue grants (PYQ November 2020 Q5(c), 3 marks; MTP December 2021 Series II Q6(c)(i), 2 marks); "define municipality" with the six heads of expenditure (PYQ November 2022 Q4(c), 3 marks); the audit programme (MTP November 2023 Series I Q6(b), 4 marks); the objectives (RTP December 2021 Q28(a), RTP May 2023 Q28(b)).

**[exam] Traps ICAI sets**
The Study Material's own MCQ offers "to provide better civic amenities to residents" as an objective of the audit: it is not; the audit reports, it does not run the town. Its correct/incorrect statement says the Central Government exercises external control of municipal expenditure: incorrect, it is the State governments (SM 9.84–9.89).

**[trick] Five objectives: "F-S-A-V-E"**
**F**airness of financial statements, **S**ystems of financial control, **A**dherence to legal and administrative requirements, **V**alue for money, **E**rror, fraud and misuse detected and prevented. Condition: this is ICAI's order (a) to (e), and the audit programme's "auditor's concerns" repeat the first four and add the fifth as the objective.

### Answer frame

### "State the objectives (or reporting areas) of the audit of a local body"

1. Opening sentence: external control of municipal expenditure is exercised by the State governments through the appointment of auditors; Delhi, Mumbai and a few others appoint their own.
2. Objectives (a) to (e), in ICAI's words and order.
3. For a case: one line applying each objective to a fact.

### "Draft the audit programme for a local body"

The six steps in order: appointment, auditor's concerns, rules and regulations, authorisations, provisioning, performance; one sentence each, in the Study Material's words.

### "Explain the types of revenue grants"

The opening line ("local bodies may receive different types of grants from the state administration"), then general purpose, specific purpose, statutory and compensatory, each with its one-line purpose.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the Centre audits the town** **Fix:** external control of municipal expenditure is exercised by the **State governments**, generally through the Local Fund Audit Wing; Delhi, Mumbai and a few other corporations appoint their own auditors.
- **Four objectives instead of five** **Fix:** the fifth, **detection and prevention of error, fraud and misuse of resources**, is the one the audit programme calls "his objective"; write all five.
- **Compensatory grant or general grant?** **Fix:** a grant that makes good the revenue lost because the State took over a tax is a **statutory and compensatory grant**; a general purpose grant bridges the gap between needs and resources.
- **Ignoring the missing internal audit** **Fix:** where a small or poorly staffed municipality has no internal audit, the external auditor cannot rely on one and has to do **detailed checking** himself (SM 9.26).

### Recap

- A municipality is a unit of local self-government in an urban area: representing local inhabitants, fairly autonomous, raising part of its revenue by local taxation, spending on local services.
- Five urban local authorities: municipal corporations, municipal councils, notified area committees, town area committees, cantonment committees. Functions: regulatory, maintenance, development.
- Expenditure heads: general administration and revenue collection, public health, public safety, education, public works, others (interest).
- Property taxes and octroi are the major revenues. Revenue grants: general purpose, specific purpose, statutory and compensatory.
- No strict revenue-capital separation ("extraordinary items"); a council combines legislative and executive powers, so external audit by the State is the only instrument of controlling expenditure.
- Auditors are appointed by the State (Local Fund Audit Wing); Delhi, Mumbai and a few others appoint their own.
- Objectives: fairness of financial statements, financial control systems, adherence to legal and administrative requirements, value for money, error, fraud and misuse. Ultra vires acts can lead to surcharge; no internal audit means detailed checking.
- Programme: appointment, concerns, rules and regulations, authorisations, provisioning, performance. (The fountain still does not dance; now it is in the report.)

## Lesson 9.3: Not-for-profit organisations, trusts and societies

*How NGOs, charitable institutions, trusts and societies are formed and funded, and what their auditor plans, vouches and reports, from corpus funds and donation boxes to indebted trustees.*

### Key idea

In an NGO, trust or society, the auditor's anchor is the constitution and the donor's terms: verify that all receipts (including box collections and foreign contributions) are recorded and banked, that restricted money (corpus, revolving and earmarked funds, specific grants) is used only as directed, that no trustee or committee member benefits, and that the governing laws are complied with.

- **Corpus contribution and revolving fund**: PYQ May 2024 Q6(a) (4 marks).
- **Donation drive programme**: RTP January 2025 Q18 and MTP September 2025 Series I Q5(b) (5 marks, three points each), answered from the six receipt and six remittance points.
- **Charitable institution**: MTP September 2026 Series I Q3(a) (5 marks): constitution, receipt books, box collections, deposits, published figures.
- **Trusts**: RTP May 2026 MCQ 8 (the auditor's responsibility: test checks based on internal control, materiality, working papers). **Societies**: RTP September 2026 Q18 (the ten considerations, with reimbursed expenses and a Registrar's inquiry in the facts).

Condition: these are SM 9.30's ten points in ICAI's order.

### Answer frame

### A list question ("points while planning", "receipts of income", "audit programme")

1. One opening line from the Study Material ("While planning the audit, the auditor may concentrate on the following").
2. ICAI's points, in ICAI's order, each with its heading in bold.
3. In a case: a closing line applying the points to the facts (the weekly banking, the demand draft).

### A trust or society case

**Relevant provision** (the matters the auditor ascertains) → **facts** → **analysis** (each fact to its point, by letter) → **conclusion** (report, and for a public trust any special matter to the Deputy or Assistant Charity Commissioner).

### "Distinguish corpus contribution and revolving fund"

| Point | Corpus contribution | Revolving fund |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Capital or corpus of the NGO | Temporary loans to other NGOs or beneficiaries, recovered and lent again |
| Donor's role | Specifies that it forms part of the corpus | Lays down restrictions, including on interest |
| Presentation | Capital receipt, liability in the balance sheet | Capital receipt, liability in the balance sheet |

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the corpus that became income** **Fix:** promoter's contributions towards corpus or a revolving fund are **capital receipts, shown as liabilities**; vouch them with the donors' letters, which carry the corpus direction.
- **Ticking the total and forgetting the trustees** **Fix:** the list of matters includes the people: the minimum and maximum number of trustees, a trustee's interest in investments, a trustee as debtor or creditor, and any benefit to a trustee or committee member from grants paid.
- **"Every NGO must use the accrual basis"** **Fix:** only NGOs registered under the Companies Act, 2013 must (section 128); others may use the accrual or the cash basis.

### Recap

- NGO: a non-profit organisation raising funds from members, donors or contributors, plus donated time and skills, for social objectives. Forms: trust (State Public Trust Act, else Indian Trusts Act, 1882), society (Societies Registration Act, 1860), section 8 company; plus the Income Tax Act and, for foreign money, the FCRA, 2010.
- Only NGOs registered under the Companies Act must use the accrual basis (others may use accrual or cash).
- Promoter's contributions (corpus, revolving fund) are capital receipts shown as liabilities; corpus needs the donor's direction; revolving-fund interest follows the contributor's restrictions or the NGO's rules.
- Auditors: appointed by management (trust, society) or members (section 8 company); FCRA report to the Ministry of Home Affairs within 60 days of year-end.
- Planning: ten points (knowledge, statutes, legal form, organisation and manuals, minutes, systems, materiality, reports, experts, previous report). Programme: every asset, liability, income and expenditure in sequence.
- Receipts: grant agreements; foreign contributions in the FCRA account; fund-raising collections counted and banked daily; fees and subscriptions to registers.
- Charitable institutions: constitution, receipt books, box collections, legacies, grants, investment income, rent, special functions, tax refunds, and grants paid only for the objects, with no benefit to trustees.
- Trusts: proper books; matters (a) to (r), including trustees' numbers, interests and debts, anonymous and cash donations, and special matters for the Charity Commissioner. Societies: governing law, objects, FCRA and tax registration, Registrar's inquiry, misconduct of the governing body. (Every envelope in the uncle's register.)

## Lesson 9.4: Educational institutions, hotels, clubs and hospitals

*The special audit steps ICAI lists for schools, colleges and universities, hospitals, clubs and hotels: read the constitution, match who should pay with who paid, test every concession and control the stocks.*

### Key idea

In educational institutions, hospitals, clubs and hotels the auditor first reads the constitution and the minutes, then matches the record of who should pay (class register, patients' attendance record, register of members, guest register) with the record of who paid, checks every concession and every large expense against the authority allowed to sanction it, and controls inventories that are easy to carry off.

### Formats and formulas

Working layouts for the Study Material's steps, not ICAI pro-formas: in the exam show the working and name the step it serves.

### Reconciling subscriptions (clubs, hospitals)

*Reproduce the order: due, collected, outstanding, compared with the register.*

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---:|
| Subscriptions due for the year (members on roll × rate, by category) | xxx |
| Less: collected for the year (cash received less arrears of earlier years and advances for next year) | (xxx) |
| **Outstanding per the books** | **xxx** |
| Outstanding per the Register of Members | xxx |
| **Difference to be explained** | **xxx** |

### Testing gross margin (hotels, clubs)

**[formula] Expected sales at the normal rate of gross profit**
$$\text{Expected sales} = \frac{\text{Cost of goods consumed}}{1 - \text{normal GP ratio}}$$

Cost of goods consumed = opening stock + purchases − closing stock of the sales point; normal GP ratio = the gross profit ÷ sales the entity normally earns at that point. Shortfall = expected sales − recorded sales.

### Occupancy-in-progress (hotels)

**[formula] Revenue earned but not yet billed at the balance sheet date**
$$\text{Occupancy-in-progress} = \qquad \sum \text{rooms} \times \text{unbilled nights} \times \text{tariff}$$

Unbilled nights = nights stayed up to and including the night of the balance sheet date and not yet billed; guests billed weekly or at checkout leave such nights at the year end. It is included in the accounts on the accrual basis (SM 9.57, 9.90).

### Answer frame

### "State the special steps in the audit of a hospital (club, school, hotel)"

2. The Study Material's points **under its headings, in its order** (for a school, group them under (A) General to (F) Compliances).

### A case on one of these entities

1. **Relevant provision**: the special step that fits each fact, in the Study Material's words.
2. **Facts of the case**: the facts and figures that decide it.
3. **Analysis**: is the treatment in line with the step, the constitution or the Managing Committee's decision? Quantify the effect if the question gives numbers.

### Three answers to "verify fees from students" (5 marks)

**[cols] **Loses marks****

"Check the fee register and the cash book and see that all fees are received."

One vague point; no class registers, no concessions, no admission fees, no caution money.
---
**Passes**

Class Registers vs Fee Register; counterfoils vs Cash Book; concessions authorised.

Three correct points, but four of ICAI's seven are missing.
---
**Full marks**

All seven points of (B) in order: demands vs Class Registers, counterfoils vs Cash Book, register totals (advance, arrears), admission fees to Capital fund, concessions, fines, hostel dues before caution money refund.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the caution money that became income** **Fix:** caution money and deposits paid on admission are a liability, not revenue, **unless they are not refundable** (SM 9.45). Check the rules before you transfer a rupee.
- **Admission fees always go to Capital fund** **Fix:** the auditor confirms the credit to a Capital fund **unless the Managing Committee has taken a decision to the contrary** (SM 9.44). A case that mentions a Committee resolution is testing exactly that exception.
- **Trusting the margin explanation** **Fix:** where controls are weak the gross margin is the auditor's main evidence; increase the scope of tests and, if a material discrepancy stays unexplained, consider qualifying (SM 9.56).
- **Mixing up the lists** **Fix:** learn each list with its own headings: the club has the secretary's powers and members' accounts; the hospital has the patients' register, budgets and the management certificate; the school has fees, caution money and separate funds; the hotel has K.O.T.s, room sales and occupancy-in-progress.

### Recap

- Read the constitution first: the Trust Deed or Regulations (an Act of Legislature for a university), bye-laws and the Managing Committee's minutes decide what is allowed.
- School: Fee Register vs Class Registers, counterfoils vs Cash Book, concessions and fines under authority, admission fees to Capital fund unless the Committee decides otherwise.
- School: caution money is a liability unless not refundable; prize endowment income above the prizes is added to the corpus; claim tax refunds; separate statements for each fund.
- Hospital: patients' register vs bills and attendance record, specific-purpose donations applied as agreed, capital expenditure only with prior sanction of the Trustees, budget variations reported, management certificate.
- Club: reconcile subscriptions due, collected and outstanding; adjust arrears and advances; every member's account debited; secretary's excess powers reported; irrecoverable dues mentioned in the report.
- Hotel: pilfering is the big risk; weekly trading accounts per sales point; K.O.T.s; room rates vs standard rate; housekeeper's reports kept; occupancy-in-progress accrued.
- Hotel: an unexplained material margin discrepancy means wider tests and possibly a qualified report. (The bar never drinks its own margin, but somebody does.)

## Lesson 9.5: Sole traders, partnership firms, LLPs and co-operative societies

*Who appoints the auditor and decides the scope, and what is specially examined, in the audit of a sole trader, a partnership firm, an LLP and a co-operative or multi-state co-operative society.*

### Key idea

The further from a sole trader, the less the client decides: a proprietor sets the scope (so write it down), a firm's deed governs, the LLP Act and Rules fix an LLP's audit and dates, and the Registrar appoints a co-operative's auditor, who reports to him.

### Exam angle

**[exam] Where this topic appeared**
In the ICAI documents read for this lesson, these entities were examined in 16 new-scheme documents (May 2024 to September 2026):

**[exam] How the marks are earned**
Provisions earn marks with their section number and figures ("section 29", "more than one thousand rupees"). Case answers state the provision, apply it and conclude ("investment in blue-chip mutual funds is not permissible"). MCQs pick on numbers: 60 days vs 30 days.

**[trick] Numbers that come in pairs**
LLP: **25 lakh contribution, 40 lakh turnover**; Form **11 in 60 days**, Form **8 in 30 days after six months**; appoint **30 days before** year end. Co-op: **20% or ₹1,000** of shares; **10%** to charity after reserve. Multi-State: first auditor in **one month**; debt over **₹1,000** disqualifies; **51%** government capital for a special audit; **15 days'** notice; **one-third** of the board or **one-fifth** of members. Condition: the Study Material's figures for January 2027.

### Answer frame

### A case on a co-operative society or LLP

1. **Relevant provision**: the section and its words ("As per section 29 of the Co-operative Societies Act, 1912, …").
2. **Facts of the case**: the figures or dates that decide it.
3. **Analysis**: compute where needed (the 10% cap, the deadline date); compare.
4. **Conclusion**: permissible or not; what the auditor does (report, special report to the Registrar, ask for correction, qualify).

### "Auditor's duty regarding audit of an LLP" (4 marks)

The four heads in order, each with its content: engagement letter; minutes book; LLP agreement (a)–(k); reporting (records correct and reliable, information obtained, restrictions). ICAI's September 2025 answer is exactly these; an answer that stops at the agreement loses the reporting mark.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the sole trader must be audited** **Fix:** a sole trader is under no legal obligation; the audit serves regulators or banks, and the proprietor sets the scope, so put it in writing (SM 9.32–9.33).
- **Charity before the reserve** **Fix:** 10% of the net profits **remaining after the compulsory transfer to the reserve fund**, and only with the Registrar's sanction (SM 9.63).
- **AGM appoints the first multi-state auditor** **Fix:** the **board** appoints the first auditor within one month of registration; the general meeting does so only if the board fails, and the AGM appoints the subsequent auditors (SM 9.69, 9.90).

### Recap

- Sole trader: no legal obligation to audit; the proprietor appoints and sets the scope, so the scope and limitations go in writing (or a letter to the client for confirmation).
- Partnership: partners appoint (decision or deed); study the deed's 11 matters; seven special matters; report if any partner's interest is prejudiced.
- LLP: audit under Rule 24 unless turnover ≤ ₹40 lakh or contribution ≤ ₹25 lakh (SM wording, though ICAI's MTP key reads it as audit if either is exceeded); designated partners appoint (first year before its end, later years 30 days before year end), partners if they fail.
- LLP returns: Form 11 within 60 days of year end; Form 8 within 30 days after six months; auditor's duty: engagement letter, minutes, agreement (a)–(k), reporting.
- Co-operative: Registrar audits or appoints (section 17); shares 20% or ₹1,000; loans only to members; section 32 investments only; reserve before dividend; charity ≤ 10% after reserve; Education Fund a charge.
- Co-operative audit: overdue debts (6 months–5 years, over 5 years), overdue interest out of profit, bad debts certified, special report to the Registrar, audit class, draft discussed with the managing committee.
- Multi-State: CA only; board appoints first auditor within a month; over ₹1,000 indebtedness disqualifies; section 77 special audit needs 51% government capital; inquiry and inspection on 15 days' notice. (The umpire is sent by the association, not chosen by the captain.)

## Lesson 9.6: Cinemas and hire purchase and leasing companies

*The special audit steps for a cinema (tickets, free passes, tax, film hire, advances to distributors) and for hire purchase and leasing companies (the agreements, the documents, finance versus operating lease).*

### Key idea

In a cinema, revenue is proved through the tickets: serially numbered, bound, a separate series for each show, class and advance booking, kept in custody, agreed with cash at the end of each show, with free passes authorised and tax reconciled to tickets. In a hire purchase or leasing company, the agreement is the evidence: its terms, the regular receipt of instalments or rentals, the lessee's credit and the documents, and whether the lease is a finance lease or an operating lease.

### Formats and formulas

**[formula] Hire-purchase charges**
$$\text{Total charges (interest)} = \qquad \text{Hire-purchase price} - \text{Cash price}$$

Hire-purchase price = deposit or initial payment + all instalments. Instalments overdue = instalments due to date under the agreement − instalments received.

**[formula] Present value test for a finance lease**
$$PV = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{L_t}{(1+r)^t}$$

$L_t$ = minimum lease payment of period $t$; $r$ = discount rate; $n$ = number of periods. If $PV$ is at least substantially all of the fair value of the asset, the lease is a finance lease (SM 9.54).

### Exam angle

**[exam] Where this topic appeared**

- **Cinemas.** Areas covered in verifying the internal control mechanism of a multiplex: May 2025 Q6(b) (5 marks; the answer is point 1(a)–(e)). Receipts from sale of tickets: MTP May 2024 Series I Q6(c) (4 marks; points 1–5 plus the online booking system).
- **Leases.** An 11-year lease over about the asset's economic life with the lessee bearing insurance and maintenance (type, ownership, accounting, tax): May 2024 Q6(d) (3 marks). Two equipment leases to classify, with accounting and tax treatment: May 2026 Q4(b) (1 + 4 marks). Points to note in a finance lease agreement: September 2024 Q5(d) (3 marks). **[exam] How the marks are earned**
Lists are marked point by point in the Study Material's words. Lease questions are marked for the classification **with reasons** (term vs economic life, who bears insurance and maintenance, purchase option), then the accounting treatment and the tax benefit from the comparison table.

**[trick] Five ticket controls: "Print, Number, Separate, Advance, Custody"**
**P**rinted tickets only; serially **N**umbered and bound; **S**eparate numbers for each show and class; a separate series for **A**dvance booking; **C**ustody of the ticket inventory with a responsible official. Condition: this is point 1(a)–(e) only; the other ten steps (end-of-show statement, free passes, tax, film hire and so on) are separate points.

### Answer frame

### "Areas to verify in the internal control mechanism of a cinema" (5 marks)

Write point 1(a)–(e) in order, one mark each; if more is asked ("receipts from sale of tickets"), add the end-of-show statement, free passes, tax reconciliation and Daily Statements to Cash Book, and the online booking system.

### "Which kind of lease, and its treatment?"

1. **Provision**: the attributes of a finance lease (or "other than a finance lease") as the Study Material lists them.
2. **Facts**: lease term against economic life; present value of the minimum lease payments against fair value; ownership transfer or purchase option; specialised asset; who bears insurance and maintenance.
3. **Conclusion**: finance or operating lease, then ownership, accounting treatment and tax benefit from the comparison table.

### Three answers to "points to note in a finance lease agreement" (3 marks)

**[cols] **Loses marks****

"See that the agreement is signed and the rent is received."

Neither is one of the four points.
---
**Passes**

Description of lessor, lessee, equipment and location; tenure, payment dates and late charges.

Two of four points.
---
**Full marks**

All four: description and location (no removal except for repairs; plates); amount, tenure, dates, late charges, deposits; return on termination at the lessee's cost; no assigning or subletting by the lessee.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the operating lease that became a loan** **Fix:** use alone does not make a finance lease; an operating lease is like renting, the asset stays off the lessee's balance sheet and the payments are expenses (SM 9.55).
- **Capitalising the cinema's repairs** **Fix:** for a cinema, the Study Material is explicit: vouch advertisement, repairs and maintenance, and **no part of such expenditure should be capitalised** (SM 9.50).
- **Writing a purchase option into the lease** **Fix:** in the normal finance lease modality, **no purchase option is given to the lessee in the lease agreement itself**; at the end the lessee may get a renewal right or join in a purchase when the lessor sells (SM 9.53).

### Recap

- Cinema revenue is proved through tickets: printed, serially numbered and bound, separate numbers for each show and class, a separate series for advance booking, ticket inventory in custody.
- After each show, tickets sold agree with cash; free passes recorded and authorised; tax reconciled with tickets per class; Daily Statements vouched into the Cash Book.
- Cinema expenses: advertisement, repairs and maintenance never capitalised; film hire vouched with distributors' bills and agreements; unadjusted advances for films already run enquired into and provided for; restaurant share followed up.
- Hire purchase: possession now, property with the last instalment, hirer may terminate before; agreement in writing, signed, stating hire-purchase price, cash price, commencement, instalments and goods; instalments received regularly.
- Leasing company: object clause, credit analysis, agreement terms (location, tenure, return, no subletting), proposal form, invoice, acceptance letter, lessee's Board resolution, insurance copies.
- Finance lease transfers substantially all risks and rewards (treated like a loan: asset on the lessee's balance sheet, interest and depreciation); an operating lease is any other lease (rent: expense, no depreciation). (Like the bus conductor, the cinema must account for every ticket on the roll.)
