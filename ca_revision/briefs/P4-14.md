## Lesson 14.1: Marginal cost, contribution and the P/V ratio

*What one more unit really costs, why fixed costs are charged to the period, how contribution pays fixed cost and profit, and the P/V ratio that links contribution to sales.*

### Key idea

Marginal cost is what one more unit adds to total cost: its variable cost. Price minus marginal cost is contribution, which pays the period's fixed costs first and becomes profit only after they are covered. The P/V ratio is contribution as a share of sales.

### Formats and formulas

ICAI's **Cost and Profit Statement under Marginal Costing** (SM 14.7). *Reproduce the headings and their order from memory.*

| Particulars | Amount (₹) | Amount (₹) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Revenue (A) | | xxx |
| Product cost: | | |
| − Direct materials | xxx | |
| − Direct employee (labour) | xxx | |
| − Direct expenses | xxx | |
| − Variable manufacturing overheads | xxx | |
| **Product (inventoriable) costs (B)** | **xxx** | **xxx** |
| **Product contribution margin {A − B}** | | **xxx** |
| − Variable administration overheads | xxx | |
| − Variable selling and distribution overheads | xxx | xxx |
| **Contribution margin (C)** | | **xxx** |
| Period cost (D): | | |
| Fixed manufacturing expenses | xxx | |
| Fixed non-manufacturing expenses | xxx | xxx |
| **Profit / (loss) {C − D}** | | **xxx** |

The short form is the **marginal cost statement**: Sales, less variable cost, = contribution, less fixed cost, = profit (SM 14.13).

**[formula] The marginal cost equation**
$$S - V = C = F \pm P$$
S = sales value, V = variable cost, C = contribution, F = fixed cost, P = profit (+) or loss (−) (SM 14.13). If C = F there is no profit or loss; at zero output the loss equals F.

**[formula] Contribution**
$$\text{Contribution} = \text{Sales revenue} - \text{Total variable cost} \qquad = \text{Fixed cost} + \text{Profit}$$
Variable cost here means all of it, manufacturing and non-manufacturing (variable selling commission included).

**[formula] P/V ratio (contribution to sales ratio)**
$$\text{P/V ratio} = \frac{\text{Contribution}}{\text{Sales}} \times 100 \qquad = \frac{\text{Change in contribution or profit}}{\text{Change in sales}} \times 100$$
The change form is valid when price, variable cost per unit and fixed cost are the same in both periods.

**[formula] Transpositions**
$$C = S \times \text{P/V ratio} \qquad S = \frac{C}{\text{P/V ratio}} \qquad \text{Variable cost ratio} = 100\% - \text{P/V ratio}$$
The first two are ICAI's (SM 14.14). The third follows because sales are variable cost plus contribution: a P/V ratio of 40% means variable cost is 60% of sales.

**[formula] Fixed cost from any period**
$$F = C - P \qquad = (S \times \text{P/V ratio}) - P$$

**[definition] Marginal cost**
The amount at any given volume of output by which aggregate costs increases if the volume of output is increased or decreased by one unit. (SM 1.34) In Chapter 14: the incremental cost of production for producing one additional unit, measured by the total variable cost attributable to one additional unit. (SM 14.2–14.3)

**[definition] Marginal costing**
A costing system where products or services and inventories are ==valued at variable costs only==. It does not take consideration of fixed costs. Also known as direct costing. (SM 14.3) As a technique: the ascertainment of marginal cost by differentiating between fixed and variable costs; it is used to ascertain the effect of changes in volume or type of output on profit. (SM 1.39)

**[definition] Contribution**
Contribution or contribution margin is the difference between sales revenue and total variable costs ==irrespective of manufacturing or non-manufacturing==. (SM 14.8)

**[definition] Period cost**
Costs which are not assigned to the products but are charged as expenses against the revenue of the period in which they are incurred. All fixed costs, either manufacturing or non-manufacturing, are recognised as period costs in marginal costing. (SM 14.9)

**[definition] P/V ratio**
This ratio shows the proportion of sales available to cover fixed costs and profit. (SM 14.13)

**[exam] The traps**
- Contribution deducts variable administration and selling costs too (September 2025 MCQ 7: total variable cost included the variable general and administration and the variable selling overheads).

**[trick] P/V ratio does the counting for you**
Profit at any sales = sales × P/V ratio − fixed cost: no units needed. A target profit of p% of sales needs sales = fixed cost ÷ (P/V ratio − p%). Conditions: one product (or a constant mix); price, variable cost per unit and fixed cost unchanged.

**[trick] The same percentage up, the same P/V ratio**
Price and variable cost per unit moved by the same percentage leave the P/V ratio unchanged (SM Illustration 9, item vii). So the price that keeps a P/V ratio is new variable cost ÷ (1 − P/V ratio).

### Answer frame

### "Discuss the advantages of marginal costing" (ICAI's seven, in ICAI's order)

1. **Simplified pricing policy:** marginal cost per unit is constant in the short run, so firm pricing decisions can be taken.
2. **Proper recovery of overheads:** no under- or over-recovery of fixed overheads absorbed at predetermined rates.
3. **Shows realistic profit:** stock and WIP at marginal cost, fixed expenses written off as period cost.
4. **How much to produce:** break-even analysis shows the effect of more or less production on profit.
5. **More control over expenditure:** actual variable expenses compared with budget through variances.
6. **Helps in decision making:** make or buy, discontinuance of a product, replacement of machines.
7. **Short-term profit planning:** through break-even charts.

### "Discuss the limitations of marginal costing" (ICAI's seven)

1. **Difficulty in classifying fixed and variable elements:** most expenses are neither wholly fixed nor wholly variable.
2. **Dependence on key factors:** contribution is no guide to optimum profitability unless linked with the key factor.
3. **Scope for low profitability:** sales staff may mistake marginal cost for total cost and sell at a loss.
4. **Faulty valuation:** fixed overheads cannot be wholly excluded from WIP, particularly in large contracts.
5. **Unpredictable nature of cost:** fixed cost and variable cost per unit do change in reality.
6. **Ignores time factor and investment:** two jobs with the same marginal cost may take different time and machines.
7. **Understating of WIP:** stocks and work-in-progress are understated.

### "Elaborate the practical application of marginal costing"

ICAI's suggested answer (MTP Series II for September 2026, 4 marks) has four points: **pricing policy** (constant marginal cost permits firm short-term pricing decisions), **decision making** (make or buy, discontinuance, replacement), **ascertaining realistic profit** (stock at marginal cost, fixed cost as period cost) and **determination of production level** (break-even analysis). The Study Material's own answer key to "Discuss the practical application of marginal costing" points instead to its "facts about marginal costing" (SM 14.6, under the hood): a fuller answer adds them after the four points.

### "Explain the characteristics of marginal costing" (ICAI's six)

(1) All costs classified into fixed and variable, semi-variable costs split; (2) variable costs are the cost of the product; (3) finished goods and WIP at marginal cost only, variable selling and distribution excluded; (4) fixed costs are period costs; (5) prices set with reference to marginal cost and contribution; (6) profitability of departments and products judged by contribution.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the fixed overhead that sneaked into marginal cost** **Fix:** marginal cost is prime cost plus variable overheads; fixed costs are deducted from total contribution, never from a unit.
- **Contribution that forgot the salesman** **Fix:** contribution is after *all* variable costs: deduct variable administration and selling costs too (₹94,50,000).
- **Closing stock valued with the selling commission in it** **Fix:** stock is valued at variable manufacturing cost only (₹130); variable selling and distribution costs are excluded from inventory.

### Recap

- Marginal cost = the variable cost of one more unit (prime cost + variable overheads); it is the same at every volume within the range.
- Marginal costing values products and stock at variable (manufacturing) cost only; all fixed costs are period costs charged to the period's profit and loss account.
- Contribution = sales − all variable costs = fixed cost + profit. Marginal cost equation: S − V = C = F ± P.
- Product contribution margin = revenue − variable manufacturing costs; contribution margin also deducts variable administration and selling costs.
- P/V ratio = contribution ÷ sales × 100, or change in contribution (profit) ÷ change in sales × 100; C = S × P/V; variable cost ratio = 100% − P/V.
- The P/V ratio moves only with price and variable cost per unit; volume and fixed cost leave it alone; equal % changes in both leave it alone too.
- Unit total cost wobbles with volume (₹95 at 20,000 jars, ₹85 at 30,000); marginal cost does not.

## Lesson 14.2: Break-even point, margin of safety and angle of incidence

*The volume at which contribution has just paid the fixed cost, in units and rupees; how far sales can fall before a loss; and what the angle of incidence shows.*

### Key idea

Contribution pays the fixed cost first; only what is left over is profit. The break-even point is the volume at which the fixed cost has just been paid, and the margin of safety is how far expected sales sit above it.

### Formats and formulas

Every break-even answer starts from the marginal cost statement or one of these formulas. *Reproduce the statement and the formulas from memory.*

| Marginal cost statement | Amount (₹) |
|---|---:|
| Sales | xxx |
| Less: Variable cost | xxx |
| **Contribution** | **xxx** |
| Less: Fixed cost | xxx |
| **Profit** | **xxx** |

**[formula] The marginal cost equation**
$$S - V = C = F \pm P$$
S = sales, V = variable cost, C = contribution, F = fixed cost, P = profit or loss (SM 14.13). At the BEP, P = 0, so C = F.

**[formula] Break-even point in units**
$$\text{BEP (units)} = \frac{\text{Fixed cost}}{\text{Contribution per unit}}$$

**[formula] Break-even sales in rupees**
$$\text{BES} = \frac{\text{Fixed cost}}{\text{P/V ratio}} \qquad = \frac{\text{Fixed cost}}{\text{Contribution}} \times \text{Sales}$$
P/V ratio = contribution ÷ sales × 100.

**[formula] Cash break-even point**
$$\text{Cash BEP (units)} = \frac{\text{Cash fixed costs}}{\text{Contribution per unit}}$$
ICAI: the break-even point "calculated only with those fixed costs which are payable in cash" (SM 14.15); depreciation and other non-cash fixed costs are left out.

**[formula] Sales for a target profit**
$$\text{Units} = \frac{\text{Fixed cost} + \text{Desired profit}}{\text{Contribution per unit}} \qquad \text{Sales value} = \frac{\text{Fixed cost} + \text{Desired profit}}{\text{P/V ratio}}$$

**[formula] Margin of safety**
$$\text{MOS} = \text{Total sales} - \text{BES} \qquad = \frac{\text{Profit}}{\text{P/V ratio}}$$
Total sales are the actual or budgeted sales; in units, units sold − BEP units.

**[formula] Margin of safety ratio and profit**
$$\text{MOS ratio} = \frac{\text{Total sales} - \text{BES}}{\text{Total sales}} \times 100 \qquad \text{Profit} = \text{MOS} \times \text{P/V ratio}$$

**[formula] P/V ratio from two periods**
$$\text{P/V ratio} = \frac{\text{Change in profit}}{\text{Change in sales}} \times 100$$
Valid when price, variable cost per unit and fixed cost are the same in both periods. A loss is a negative profit.

### Which formula from which data

| You are given | Find first | Then |
|---|---|---|
| Price, variable cost per unit, fixed cost | Contribution per unit | BEP = fixed cost ÷ contribution per unit |
| Sales and profit of two periods | P/V = change in profit ÷ change in sales | Fixed cost = contribution − profit; BES = fixed cost ÷ P/V |
| Profit and P/V ratio | MOS = profit ÷ P/V | BES = sales − MOS |
| Fixed cost, P/V ratio, MOS ratio | BES = fixed cost ÷ P/V | Sales = BES ÷ (1 − MOS ratio) |
| MOS in units and in ₹, BEP units | Price = MOS (₹) ÷ MOS (units) | Sales = (MOS + BEP) units × price |

**[definition] Break-even point (BEP)**
The level of sales where an entity neither earns profit nor incurs loss. BEP is indicated in both quantity and monetary value terms. (SM 14.49)

**[definition] Margin of safety (MOS)**
The margin between sales and the break-even sales is known as margin of safety. It can either be indicated in quantitative or monetary terms. (SM 14.49)

**[definition] Angle of incidence**
This angle is formed by the intersection of sales line and total cost line at the break-even point. This angle shows the rate at which profit is earned once the break-even point is reached. (SM 14.31)

**[trick] The three-line identity**
BES × P/V ratio = fixed cost; MOS × P/V ratio = profit; so sales × P/V ratio = fixed cost + profit. Any two of sales, BES and MOS give the third. Conditions: one product (or a constant mix), with price, variable cost per unit and fixed cost constant over the range.

**[trick] BES% + MOS% = 100%**
On the same total sales, break-even sales and margin of safety are its two parts: if the MOS is 28% of sales, BES = sales × 72%.

### The theory parts

The chapter's own theory questions (SM 14.52) include these three. Write the points in this order.

**Short note on margin of safety**

1. Definition: expected (actual) sales minus break-even sales, in units or rupees.
2. Formulas: sales − BES; profit ÷ P/V ratio; MOS ratio = (sales − BES) ÷ sales.
3. Meaning: the larger the margin of safety, the higher the chances of making profits.
4. A large angle of incidence with a high margin of safety: an extremely favourable position.
5. A two-line numerical example.

**Short note on angle of incidence**

1. The angle formed by the intersection of the sales line and the total cost line at the BEP.
2. It shows the rate at which profit is earned once the BEP is reached (below the BEP, the rate of loss).
3. The wider the angle, the greater the rate of earning profit; it widens with the P/V ratio.
4. A large angle with a high margin of safety: an extremely favourable position.
5. A labelled sketch.

**Explain and illustrate the BEP with a break-even chart**

1. Definition: no profit, no loss; total cost equals total sales.
2. Formula in units and in rupees.
3. The chart: costs and revenues up the side, activity along the bottom; fixed cost, total cost and sales lines.
4. The BEP where sales cut total cost; margin of safety and profit read off.
5. A labelled sketch with figures.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the loss that lost its minus sign** **Fix:** a loss is a negative profit, so the change is ₹21,00,000 − (−₹9,00,000) = ₹30,00,000 and the P/V ratio 30%.
- **The margin of safety measured against the wrong sales** **Fix:** divide by the total (actual or budgeted) sales: 9,000 ÷ 24,000 = 37.5%. Test: BES% and MOS% must add up to 100%.
- **Depreciation smuggled into the cash break-even point** **Fix:** leave out depreciation and other non-cash fixed costs, and nothing else.
- **The semi-variable cost that stayed in one piece** **Fix:** split it first; the variable part joins the variable cost per unit, the fixed part the fixed cost.

### Recap

- Contribution per unit = price − variable cost per unit; P/V ratio = contribution ÷ sales × 100.
- BEP (units) = fixed cost ÷ contribution per unit; BES (₹) = fixed cost ÷ P/V ratio.
- Cash BEP = cash fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit (depreciation and other non-cash costs out).
- Target profit: (fixed cost + desired profit) ÷ contribution per unit, or ÷ P/V ratio for rupees.
- MOS = total sales − BES = profit ÷ P/V ratio; MOS ratio = MOS ÷ total sales; profit = MOS × P/V ratio.
- Two periods: P/V ratio = change in profit ÷ change in sales, and a loss is a negative profit.
- Angle of incidence: where the sales line cuts the total cost line at the BEP; the wider, the faster profit is earned. Large angle plus high MOS: extremely favourable.
- Fixed cost up: BEP rises, angle unchanged. (Bucket first, then the glass.)

## Lesson 14.3: Break-even and profit-volume charts

*How to draw and read the three CVP pictures ICAI teaches: the break-even chart, the contribution break-even chart and the profit-volume chart, and what each shows that the others hide.*

### Key idea

Every CVP chart is straight lines through two easy points. The break-even point is where sales cross total cost (or the profit line crosses zero), and each chart is chosen for what it lets you read: the break-even chart shows costs, the contribution chart adds contribution, the profit-volume chart shows profit directly.

### Formats and formulas

Each chart is plotted from a small table of points; write it as a working note above the graph. *Reproduce the line list for each chart from memory.*

| Chart | Lines to draw | Point at zero activity | Point at maximum activity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Break-even | fixed cost | F | F |
| | total cost | F | F + V × units |
| | sales | 0 | price × units |
| Contribution break-even | variable cost | 0 | V × units |
| | total cost | F | F + V × units |
| | sales | 0 | price × units |
| Profit-volume | profit | −F | C × units − F |

**[formula] The lines as equations (x = units)**
$$\text{Sales} = p\,x \qquad \text{Total cost} = F + v\,x \qquad \text{Variable cost} = v\,x \qquad \text{Profit} = (p - v)\,x - F$$
p = selling price per unit, v = variable cost per unit, F = fixed cost. With sales value S along the horizontal axis: profit = S × P/V ratio − F.

**[formula] Where two profit lines cross**
$$\text{Units at which profits are equal} = \frac{F_2 - F_1}{C_2 - C_1}$$
C₁, C₂ = contribution per unit, F₁, F₂ = fixed costs of the two options. When the option with the higher contribution per unit also has the higher fixed cost (the usual trade-off), it earns more above this volume and the other earns more below it (the crossing in SM Example 5, 14.23; solved this way in January 2025 Q1(a)(ii)). If one option has both the higher contribution and the lower fixed cost, the lines never cross and it is better at every volume.

**[definition] Break-even chart**
A breakeven chart records costs and revenues on the vertical axis and the level of activity on the horizontal axis. The breakeven point is that point where the sales revenue line intersects the total cost line. (SM 14.20)

**[definition] Contribution breakeven chart**
Based on the same principles as a conventional breakeven chart except that it ==shows the variable cost line instead of the fixed cost line==; contribution can be read as the difference between the sales revenue line and the variable cost line. (SM 14.20–14.21)

**[definition] Profit-volume chart**
A chart in which the vertical axis represents profits and losses and ==the horizontal axis is drawn at zero profit or loss==; the breakeven point is where the profit line intersects the horizontal axis. (SM 14.21)

### Answer frame

### "Explain the profit-volume chart and its advantage"

1. **What it plots:** profit and loss on the vertical axis, the horizontal axis drawn at zero profit, activity (units or sales) along it.
2. **How it is drawn:** a line from the loss at nil activity (equal to the fixed cost) to the profit at a given level or the BEP.
3. **Reading:** the BEP where the line cuts the horizontal axis; profit or loss at any activity as the height of the line.
4. **Advantage (ICAI's "biggest"):** it depicts clearly the effect on profit and the break-even point of any change in the variables (price, variable cost, fixed cost), by drawing a second line on the same graph.
5. **A labelled sketch with figures.**

### "Distinguish the break-even chart from the contribution break-even chart"

| Point | Break-even chart | Contribution break-even chart |
|---|---|---|
| Lines drawn | fixed cost, total cost, sales | variable cost, total cost, sales |
| Bottom line | horizontal at fixed cost | from the origin (variable cost) |
| Contribution | cannot be read (its major limitation) | gap between sales and variable cost lines |

## Lesson 14.4: Marginal versus absorption costing and profit reconciliation

*Why the two methods report different profits when stock changes, ICAI's income statement for each, and how to reconcile them through the fixed overhead in opening and closing stock.*

### Key idea

The two methods differ only in fixed production overhead. Absorption costing carries part of it forward in closing stock (and brings last period's part back with opening stock); marginal costing charges all of it to the period. So the difference in profit is exactly the fixed overhead in closing stock minus the fixed overhead in opening stock.

## Lesson 14.5: Limiting factor and product mix

*When one resource runs short, rank products by contribution per unit of that resource, fill demand in rank order, and earn the most from what you have.*

### Key idea

When a resource is scarce, rank products by contribution per unit of that resource, not by contribution (or profit) per unit of product. Fixed costs that stay the same whatever the mix never change the ranking.

### Answer frame

### "Identify the type of cost and state whether it is relevant"

Write a three-column table, as ICAI's suggested answer does: **Situation | Type of cost | Relevant / Non-relevant**. ### "Explain the framework for decision making"

1. **Identification of problem**: the goal set to reach the objectives, such as low-cost production; a cost leader must achieve the 3Es: economy in inputs, efficiency in process and operations, effectiveness in output (SM 14.32).
2. **Identification of options**: all possible options are explored. ICAI's list for low-cost production (SM 14.32–14.33): (a) purchase of inputs from a specialised market: local vs import; (b) make the input in its own factory: make or buy; (c) bulk purchase to avail a discount: how much to purchase; (d) make in-house: make vs outsource; (e) bulk processing: how much to produce; (f) an efficient machine: old machine vs new machine; (g) optimisation of key resources: product mix decisions.
3. **Evaluation of the options** against the objective: financial measures (impact on profit, market share, return on investment) and non-financial factors (customer satisfaction, impact on existing market or customers, ethics); two tasks: identification of the costs and benefits of each option (controllability, relevance) and estimation of their amount (variability, traceability).
4. **Selection of the option**: the best option is selected and implemented.

### "Should the special order be accepted?"

1. **Relevant cost of the order**: variable cost, costs specific to the order, and the contribution lost on any regular sales it displaces; fixed costs that continue (or are already recovered by budgeted sales) are excluded.
2. **Price against relevant cost**: the margin the order adds to profit.
3. **Capacity**: whether the order fits the idle capacity or displaces regular sales.
4. **Non-financial factors** (SM 14.33): impact on the existing market or customers (regular buyers may ask for the same price), customer satisfaction, ethics of the decision.
5. **Recommendation**: accept, reject, or accept with conditions, with the reason.

---
**Full marks**

Contribution per unit; a column for each option (continued / shut down) with unavoidable fixed and closing costs; decision in words; shut-down point by formula, in units (and as a % of capacity where asked).

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the apportioned rent that killed the bun maska** **Fix:** compare its contribution with its avoidable fixed costs only.
- **Full cost against the buy price** **Fix:** count only the variable cost, the fixed costs that would really disappear, and any contribution the capacity could earn elsewhere.
- **The special order with no room for it** **Fix:** displaced regular sales and price concessions are opportunity costs: add the contribution lost to the cost of the order.
- **The feasibility report that keeps billing you** **Fix:** sunk and committed costs are the same whichever option is chosen: leave them out.

### Recap

- Relevant cost: a future cost that differs between the options (SM 14.33); sunk, historical and committed costs are irrelevant; opportunity cost is relevant; notional cost only if a benefit is really forgone.
- Framework: identify the problem, identify options, evaluate (controllability, relevance; variability, traceability; non-financial factors), select.
- Make or buy: buy price against variable cost + avoidable fixed cost + contribution forgone on the alternative use.
- Discontinue: drop only if the avoidable fixed cost (plus rent from freed space) exceeds the contribution lost; apportioned common costs stay.
- Shut down: compare the loss if continued with the shut-down cost (unavoidable fixed + closing costs).
- Shut-down point = (total fixed cost − shut-down cost) ÷ contribution per unit; in rupees ÷ P/V ratio.
- Special order: accept if price exceeds variable + specific costs; with no idle capacity add the contribution of displaced sales. Minimum price = relevant cost ÷ units.
- If it would sit in both columns at the same figure, it cannot decide anything. (The rent will be there tomorrow.)

## Lesson 14.7: Multi-product break-even analysis

*With several products sharing fixed costs, break-even needs the sales mix: a weighted contribution per unit for a mix in units, a weighted P/V ratio for a mix in values.*

### Key idea

With products sharing fixed costs, the break-even point exists only for a stated sales mix. Weight the contributions by the mix, divide the fixed cost by the weighted figure, then split the result in the same mix.

### Formats and formulas

*Reproduce ICAI's statement from memory: it is the backbone of every value-mix answer (SM 14.77–14.78).*

| Particulars | A | B | C | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Selling price (₹) | xxx | xxx | xxx | |
| Less: Variable cost (₹) | xxx | xxx | xxx | |
| Contribution per unit (₹) | xx | xx | xx | |
| P/V ratio | x% | x% | x% | |
| Sales mix | x% | x% | x% | 100% |
| **Contribution per rupee of sales (P/V ratio × sales mix)** | **x%** | **x%** | **x%** | **x%** |

Then: total contribution = total sales × composite P/V ratio; less fixed costs = profit; break-even sales = fixed costs ÷ composite P/V ratio.

**[formula] Composite contribution per unit (mix in units)**
$$\text{Composite contribution} = \sum (C \times w)$$
C = a product's contribution per unit; w = its units weight, its units in the mix ÷ total units in the mix. The result is a contribution per (average) unit. ICAI's Example 4: 4 units of J (₹40) to 3 of K (₹20) gives ₹40 × 4/7 + ₹20 × 3/7 = ₹31.43 (SM 14.16).

**[formula] Composite break-even point**
$$\text{BEP (units)} = \frac{\text{Common fixed cost}}{\text{Composite contribution}} \qquad \text{Product BEP} = \text{BEP} \times w$$

**[formula] Composite P/V ratio (mix in values)**
$$\text{Composite P/V} = \sum (\text{P/V} \times \text{mix \%}) \qquad = \frac{\text{Total contribution}}{\text{Total sales}}$$
Mix % = each product's share of total sales value.

**[formula] Composite break-even sales**
$$\text{Composite BES} = \frac{\text{Fixed cost}}{\text{Composite P/V}} \qquad \text{Product BES} = \text{BES} \times \text{mix \%}$$

ICAI's Illustration 11 also shows a second, equivalent method: share the fixed cost among the products in the ratio of their weighted contributions, then each product's BEP = its share of fixed cost ÷ its own contribution per unit (SM 14.39).

### Exam angle

**[exam] Where multi-product break-even appeared**

- **RTP September 2024 Q17** and **MTP Series II for September 2025 Q1(a), 5 marks**: a single product at a 60% margin of safety (net return on sales after tax), then a second product with "sales mix of X : Y would be 5 : 3": break-even units of each.

- The Study Material: Example 4, Illustration 11, practical problems 8 and 20.

**[exam] The traps**
- A mix "per batch" or "for every": compute one batch's contribution, then the number of batches.
- Sales of each product given in rupees, not as percentages: composite P/V = total contribution ÷ total sales.
- A replacement product: recompute the whole composite, with the new mix, the new total sales and the new fixed cost.
- Rounding: ICAI's Illustration 11 gives 23,145.80 composite units and rounds each product to whole units (16,202 and 6,944).

**[trick] Batch it**
Turn any units mix into one "batch" (5 namkeen + 3 mathri). Fixed cost ÷ contribution per batch = break-even batches; multiply by each product's units in the batch. No fractions until the end. Condition: the mix stays constant at every volume.

### Answer frame

### "Advise whether the company should change over to the new mix"

1. Statement of contribution to sales ratio at the existing mix (P/V ratio × sales mix for each product, total).
2. Existing total contribution, profit and break-even sales.
3. The same statement and figures for the proposed mix, with its own sales and fixed costs.
4. Comparison: composite P/V ratio, break-even sales, profit (and margin of safety).
5. Conclusion in words, with the reason (higher composite P/V ratio and profit), and the assumption that the mix will be achieved.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the P/V ratios that were simply averaged** **Fix:** weight each P/V ratio by its share of sales value, or divide total contribution by total sales.
- **The right weights on the wrong figures** **Fix:** units weights go with contribution per unit; value weights go with P/V ratios. Convert one into the other with the prices if needed.
- **Splitting the answer in the wrong ratio** **Fix:** split composite units by the units ratio (25,000 and 15,000 packs), then multiply by the prices.

### Recap

- Several products sharing a fixed cost break even only for a stated sales mix (CVP assumption: the mix stays constant).
- Mix in units: composite contribution = Σ contribution per unit × units weight; composite BEP = common fixed cost ÷ composite contribution; split by the units ratio.
- Batch method: contribution of one batch of the mix; fixed cost ÷ that = batches.
- Mix in values: composite P/V = Σ P/V ratio × sales mix % (contribution per rupee of sales) = total contribution ÷ total sales; BES = fixed cost ÷ composite P/V; split by the sales mix %.
- A shift towards high-P/V products lowers break-even sales and raises profit; a replacement product means a new composite.
- Margin of safety = actual (or budgeted) sales − composite break-even sales; profit = MOS × composite P/V ratio.
- "Sales mix 5 : 3" with no unit stated: ICAI's answers treat it as units; state the assumption. (Break even on thalis, not on rotis.)
