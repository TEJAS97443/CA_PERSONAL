## Lesson 1.1: What an audit is, why it is needed and its objective

*An audit is an independent examination of financial information with a view to expressing an opinion: where it came from, why users rely on it, who appoints the auditor and what it aims at.*

### Key idea

An audit does not make the accounts; it makes them believable. An independent person examines the financial information and expresses an opinion on it, and that opinion gives every user confidence: high, but never a guarantee.

### Exam angle

**[exam] Where these topics appeared, May 2024 to September 2026**
In the new-scheme ICAI documents read for this lesson (past papers, RTPs and MTPs, May 2024 to September 2026), these topics appeared in Mock Test Papers, not in the past papers read:

- **Benefits and need of audit:** MTP January 2025 Series II Q2(c) (4 marks: lenders doubt a trading company's accounts; "Elucidate the benefits and need of an audit") and MTP May 2026 Series II Q1(b) (5 marks: a director calls the statutory audit a mere cost). Both Suggested Answers give the seven benefits of SM 1.17–1.18 in ICAI's order, almost word for word. As a 2-mark MCQ: MTP May 2025 Series I MCQ 15 ("which is not a likely benefit of auditing?"; the odd one out claims audited information is connected to future events).
- **Meaning of audit:** MTP September 2026 Series II Q4(c) (4 marks: a charitable trust that thinks independence hardly matters; "explain the meaning of auditing and give an incisive analysis of the principles emerging from its definition"): the definition, its three points, applied.
- **Who appoints:** MTP May 2024 Series I Q2(d) (3 marks): a company owned by the Union and State Governments, so the C&AG appoints (the Companies Act rules are in [[aud.entities.govt|government audit]]).
- **The objective:** asked in its SA 200 form: see [[aud.nature.sa200|SA 200]].

**[exam] How the marks are earned**
The Suggested Answers start from ICAI's own words (the definition, or the list) and only then apply them to the case. In a "benefits" answer each point carries its reason ("a moral check on employees from committing frauds for the fear of being discovered by audit"), not just a label. In a "meaning" answer, the word **independent** must be explained, not merely quoted.

### Answer frame

### "Explain the meaning of audit" (with an incisive analysis)

1. **Definition**, word for word: "An audit is an independent examination of financial information of any entity, whether profit oriented or not, and irrespective of its size or legal form, when such an examination is conducted with a view to expressing an opinion thereon."
2. **Point 1, independence:** judgment not subordinate to the wishes or direction of another person who might have engaged him; opinion formed without influence; objectivity without bias.
3. **Point 2, any entity:** profit oriented or not; any size; any legal form (name a few).
4. **Point 3, purpose:** to express an opinion on the financial statements through a written report; preparation is management's responsibility.
5. **Apply** each point to the facts, then **conclude**.

### "Explain the benefits and need of an audit"

The seven points of SM 1.17–1.18 in order, one line each with its reason, then one closing line tying them to the case (the loan, the absent owners, the cash-handling staff).

**[cols] **Loses marks****

"Audit is useful: it catches fraud and banks like it."

Two benefits named, no reasons, nothing from the case.
---
**Passes**

Five or six benefits as bare labels: quality, shareholders, tax, banks, fraud.

The list is recognisable but thin, and the moral check and controls are missing.
---
**Full marks**

All seven in ICAI's order, each with its one-line reason, applied to the case (the lender's credit decision, the sleeping partner, the cash-collecting staff).

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the auditor who "prepared and audited" the accounts** **Fix:** preparation and presentation of the financial statements is the responsibility of management; the auditor expresses an opinion on them in a written report (SM 1.6).
- **The report addressed to the management** **Fix:** the report is submitted to the person making the appointment: the shareholders of a company, the partners of a firm (SM 1.19).
- **Benefits in a heap** **Fix:** learn the seven in ICAI's order with the mnemonic, and give each its reason.

### Recap

- **Audit:** an independent examination of financial information of any entity, whether profit oriented or not, and irrespective of its size or legal form, conducted with a view to expressing an opinion thereon.
- **Independence:** the auditor's judgment is not subordinate to the wishes or direction of anyone who engaged him; no audit of a brother's concern or of a company whose shares he holds.
- Management prepares and presents the financial statements; the auditor expresses an opinion in a written report. The nature of auditing is **assurance**: confidence and credibility for users.
- **Objective (SA 200):** reasonable assurance that the financial statements as a whole are free from material misstatement, whether due to fraud or error, enabling an opinion; then report and communicate as the SAs require. The same objective for every entity.
- **Seven benefits, in order:** quality information, shareholders' interest, moral check, tax authorities, lenders, fraud or error detected, deficiencies in controls.
- Not always mandatory: companies by law, others under tax laws or for grants, many voluntarily.
- Appointed by the owners (company: members in the AGM; government company: C&AG; firm: partners) or by a government authority under a law; the report goes to whoever appointed the auditor.
- Origin: Kautilya's Arthashastra; *audire*, "to hear"; first Auditor General 1860; ICAI 1949. (Kesar still offers to read his accounts aloud.)

## Lesson 1.2: SA 200: overall objectives, reasonable assurance and professional judgment

*SA 200's two overall objectives, why reasonable assurance is high but never a guarantee, how evidence and audit risk produce it, and where professional judgment and skepticism do the work.*

### Key idea

Reasonable assurance is a high level of assurance, never a guarantee. It is reached when sufficient appropriate audit evidence has reduced audit risk to an acceptably low level, and deciding when that point has been reached is a matter of professional judgment.

### Exam angle

**[exam] Where SA 200 appeared, May 2024 to September 2026**
Of the new-scheme ICAI documents read for this lesson, 8 examined SA 200's objectives or reasonable assurance and 3 examined professional judgment (MTP May 2026 Series II did both):

- **"Can the auditor give a guarantee?", 3 to 5 marks.** MTP September 2025 Series I Q6(a) (5 marks: a CEO insists on absolute assurance); MTP September 2026 Series I Q1(b) (5 marks: "complete guarantee", and must the auditor also report?); RTP May 2024 Q1 (assistants see audit as a guarantee); RTP January 2026 Q9 (directors want complete assurance); RTP January 2025 Q9 (after a collusion); the September 2024 paper, Q1(c) (3 marks: a clause making the auditor responsible for detecting frauds; the answer opens with the SA 200 objective).
- **MCQs, 2 marks.** MTP September 2024 Series I MCQ 14 (reasonable assurance means a high level of assurance); MTP May 2026 Series II MCQ 3 (misstatements of unknown origin: reasonable assurance against fraud or error, evaluated in aggregate).
- **Professional judgment.** MTP January 2026 Series II Q1(a) (5 marks with audit risk: how training and experience support the judgment); MTP May 2026 Series II MCQ 11 (judgment, not "a precise calculation"); MTP January 2026 Series I Q3(a) (5 marks on evidence, using SA 200's factors).
- **The premise.** MTP May 2025 Series I Q4(a) (5 marks), through SA 700's "Responsibilities of Management" section.
- Old scheme, for practice: May 2022 Q2(c) (3 marks: an opinion without ascertaining whether the statements as a whole are free from material misstatement does not meet SA 200); January 2021 Q1(b) (2 marks: "the auditor is expected to reduce audit risk to zero and can therefore obtain absolute assurance": incorrect).

**[exam] How the marks are earned**
Most of the Suggested Answers above quote the objectives in full and the points of SM 1.8–1.9, then apply them ("In the given case ... therefore the contention is not correct"). The phrases that carry marks: "reasonable assurance", "financial statements as a whole", "free from material misstatement, whether due to fraud or error", "in all material respects", "applicable financial reporting framework", "report ... and communicate as required by the SAs".

**[trick] Objective (a) in five checkpoints**
**R**easonable assurance, **W**hole statements, **M**aterial misstatement, **F**raud or error, **F**ramework, in all material respects: "Really Wise Men Fear Frauds". Then add objective (b): report and communicate. The mnemonic only checks that no phrase is missing; write the full sentence.

### Answer frame

### "Discuss the objectives of the auditor" or "Can the auditor give a guarantee?"

1. **Provision:** "As per SA 200 ..." with objectives (a) and (b) word for word.
2. **The four points:** reasonable, not absolute, assurance (define both); fraud or error, statements as a whole; the opinion in all material respects per the framework; reporting and communication through a written report.
3. **Why not absolute:** audit risk cannot be reduced to zero; inherent limitations; evidence persuasive rather than conclusive.
4. **Facts and conclusion:** "In the given case, ... hence the contention of ... is not correct."

### A case on judgment or skepticism

Provision (SM 3.9 for judgment, SM 4.12–4.13 for sufficiency, SM 6.10 for documentation, SM 11.12–11.13 for skepticism) → facts → what the judgment or the skeptical step is in this case → conclusion.

**[cols] **Loses marks****

"No, the auditor cannot give a guarantee because he checks on a test basis."

One limitation, no objective, no definition of reasonable assurance.
---
**Passes**

Objective (a) quoted, "reasonable assurance is high but not absolute", and a one-line conclusion.

Objective (b) and the four points are missing.
---
**Full marks**

Both objectives verbatim, the four points, absolute versus reasonable assurance defined, why audit risk cannot be zero, the facts applied, a clear conclusion.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: "reasonable assurance means moderate assurance"** **Fix:** reasonable assurance is a **high** level of assurance; the moderate (limited) level belongs to a review (SM 1.9, 1.24).
- **Half an objective** **Fix:** learn both objectives word for word; each phrase is a mark in ICAI's answers.
- **"Professional judgment means my opinion"** **Fix:** it is exercised by an auditor whose training, knowledge and experience support reasonable judgments, on the evidence obtained, and significant judgments are documented (SM 3.9, 6.10).
- **The audit that took over management's job** **Fix:** the premise of SA 200: management and, where appropriate, those charged with governance accept responsibility for preparing the statements and for the necessary internal control (SM 8.15).

### Recap

- **SA 200** "Overall Objectives of the Independent Auditor and the Conduct of an Audit in Accordance with Standards on Auditing".
- **Objective (a):** reasonable assurance that the financial statements as a whole are free from material misstatement, whether due to fraud or error, enabling an opinion on whether they are prepared, in all material respects, in accordance with an applicable financial reporting framework. **Objective (b):** report and communicate as required by the SAs, in accordance with the findings.
- **Purpose:** to enhance the degree of confidence of intended users in the financial statements.
- **Reasonable assurance** = high, not a guarantee; **absolute assurance** = a complete guarantee, never attainable. Audit risk cannot be reduced to zero; evidence is persuasive rather than conclusive.
- Reasonable assurance is obtained when sufficient appropriate audit evidence has reduced **audit risk** (an inappropriate opinion on materially misstated statements) to an acceptably low level.
- **Premise:** management (and, where appropriate, those charged with governance) accept responsibility for preparing the statements and for the necessary internal control.
- **Professional judgment:** risk assessment, materiality and sufficiency of evidence are judgments, not precise measurements, made by an auditor with the training, knowledge and experience to reach reasonable judgments; significant ones are documented.
- **Professional skepticism:** a questioning mind, alertness to possible misstatement, critical assessment of evidence; honesty in the past does not excuse it. (Bunty's 13-year tick would not have helped either.)

## Lesson 1.3: Inherent limitations of an audit

*Why no audit can give absolute assurance: the five built-in limitations (financial reporting, audit procedures, not an investigation, timeliness, future events) and what they never excuse.*

### Key idea

The limitations are built into every audit, so audit risk can never be reduced to zero and the assurance is reasonable, not absolute. They explain why a properly planned and performed audit can still miss a misstatement; they never excuse an audit that was not properly performed.

### Exam angle

**[exam] Where it appeared, May 2024 to September 2026**
Inherent limitations were examined in 9 of the new-scheme ICAI documents read for this lesson:

- **Past paper:** May 2024 Q2(c) (3 marks: a pager plant shut down after mobile phones arrived; shareholders blame the auditor; "List down the factors giving rise to the inherent limitations ..."). The Suggested Answer gives all five factors with the Study Material's explanations.
- **MTPs:** May 2024 Series I Q2(c) (3 marks: one example each of a practical and a legal limitation); May 2024 Series II Q4(c) (3 marks: the nature of financial reporting); May 2025 Series II Q5(c) (4 marks: "not expected to, and cannot, reduce audit risk to zero ... Explain", answered with SA 200's short form of the five); January 2026 Series II Q5(a) (5 marks: collusion between an accountant and the goods-receiving person).
- **RTPs:** January 2025 Q9 (collusion, with SA 200's objectives); May 2026 Q9 (delayed documents, unsupported "special procurement", denied related parties, uniform-looking documents, test checks).
- **Through other topics:** MTP September 2026 Series I Q5(c) (4 marks: going concern; the answer quotes SA 200 on the greater effect of inherent limitations for future events); the May 2026 paper, Q6(a) (5 marks: the auditor's responsibilities in an engagement letter, ending with the "unavoidable risk" sentence).
- Old scheme: January 2021 Q1(b) (2 marks, correct or incorrect: "the auditor is expected to reduce audit risk to zero": incorrect).

**[exam] How the marks are earned**
Most Suggested Answers open with "The process of audit suffers from certain inbuilt limitations due to which an auditor cannot obtain an absolute assurance ...", then give the five headings in ICAI's order, each with its one-line explanation, and in a case map the facts to the headings. A 3-mark "list" wants all five; a case wants the two or three that fit, explained against the facts, and a conclusion.

**[trick] Five limitations in order**
"**F**ine **A**uditors **N**ever **T**rust **F**utures": Financial reporting, Audit procedures, Not an investigation, Timeliness, Future events. ICAI's order, so the answer reads the way the Suggested Answer does.

### Answer frame

### "List / explain the inherent limitations of audit" (3 to 4 marks)

1. Opening: the process of audit suffers from inbuilt limitations, so absolute assurance is impossible; the auditor is not expected to, and cannot, reduce audit risk to zero (SA 200).
2. The five headings in order, each with one line: judgment in financial reporting (and controls' own limits); practical and legal limits on evidence (samples, information withheld, sophisticated fraud); not an official investigation; relevance falls over time, reliability versus cost; future events.
3. One closing line: hence the auditor cannot provide a guarantee.

### A case ("the auditor should have caught it")

**Provision** (opening + the relevant factors) → **facts** (what the control was, how it failed, what the auditor did) → **analysis** (map each fact to a factor) → **conclusion** (reasonable, not absolute, assurance; fault depends on whether the audit was properly planned and performed in accordance with SAs).

**[cols] **Loses marks****

"Audit is done on test check basis, so the auditor cannot detect all frauds."

One limitation, no SA 200, no facts.
---
**Passes**

The five headings listed correctly, but no explanation and nothing from the case.

A list answer earns some marks; a case answer, little.
---
**Full marks**

The opening sentence, the five headings with their one-line explanations, the facts mapped (collusion, samples, documents), and a conclusion with the "properly planned and performed" condition.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the internal-control list in an audit answer** **Fix:** write the five audit factors of SA 200; mention control limitations only inside the first factor.
- **"Cost is an inherent limitation, so I skipped the stock count"** **Fix:** as explained in SA 200, difficulty, time or cost is not in itself a valid basis to omit a procedure for which there is no alternative, or to be satisfied with evidence that is less than persuasive (SM 4.71). The cost limitation is about not verifying every matter, not about skipping necessary procedures.
- **"An audit is not an investigation, so fraud is not my concern"** **Fix:** the objective covers material misstatement "whether due to fraud or error". "Not an investigation" refers to the audit's purpose and the auditor's lack of powers of search and examination on oath; doubts about documents or indications of fraud still require further investigation within the audit (SM 1.12, 11.13).

### Recap

- **Inherent limitations:** inbuilt limits of the audit process, due to which an auditor cannot obtain absolute assurance that the financial statements are free from misstatement due to fraud or error.
- **SA 200:** the auditor is not expected to, and cannot, reduce audit risk to zero; most audit evidence is persuasive rather than conclusive.
- **(1) Nature of financial reporting:** management's judgments; controls with their own limits (collusion at the goods-receiving stamp).
- **(2) Nature of audit procedures:** practical (samples) and legal (cannot force information; can only report) limits; sophisticated fraud and fabricated documents; not an expert in authentication; paper related party transactions.
- **(3) Not an investigation:** no powers of search or of statements on oath. **(4) Timeliness:** relevance falls over time; reliability versus cost. **(5) Future events:** the entity may be adversely affected later.
- Hence no guarantee: there is an unavoidable risk that some material misstatements are not detected even in an audit properly planned and performed in accordance with SAs.
- **Never an excuse:** difficulty, time or cost does not justify omitting a procedure with no alternative (SM 4.71). (Bunty's 20 working days of ticking would not have caught the potato man either.)

## Lesson 1.4: Scope of audit, engagement types and qualities of an auditor

*What an audit of financial statements covers and what it never does (another expert's work, authenticating documents, investigations), what an engagement is, and the qualities ICAI expects of an auditor.*

### Key idea

The scope of an audit is set by its purpose, an opinion on the financial statements as a whole. Everything that opinion needs (coverage of all relevant aspects, reliable and sufficient records, proper disclosure) is inside; preparing the statements, other professions' work, authenticating documents and investigations are outside.

### Exam angle

**[exam] Where the scope of audit appeared, May 2024 to September 2026**
In the ICAI documents read for this lesson, the scope of audit was examined in 9 of them:

- **"Does this matter fall within the scope of audit?", a case**: RTP May 2025 Q9 (an undisclosed change in the inventory valuation method); RTP September 2025 Q9 (unreported project revenue, unsupported vouchers, an undisclosed change in depreciation method); September 2026 Q4(c), 4 marks (an undisclosed consumer claim, plus "state the important points in relation to the scope"; answers not yet published).
- **Outside the scope, and audit versus investigation**: MTP May 2026 Series I Q1(b), 5 marks; MTP September 2024 Series I Q2(c), 4 marks (a board member objects to calling a fraud probe an "audit"); September 2024 Q1(c), 3 marks (a fraud-detection clause in the appointment letter).
- **Multiple choice, 2 marks**: September 2025 MCQ 2 (which falls within the scope); May 2026 MCQ 8 (which falls outside: the physical condition and life of buildings and machinery); MTP September 2025 Series I MCQ 15.

**[exam] Qualities of an auditor**
No new-scheme paper, RTP or MTP among those read asked it. The old scheme did, for 4 marks, in MTP July 2021 and MTP December 2021 (both Series I, Q2(b)), opening with Lord Justice Lindley's words in the London and General Bank case. Those answers attribute the list of qualities to Dicksee and add knowledge of law and taxation; the current Study Material gives the list without attribution. Write the Study Material's version: the eleven qualities, the shine of culture, integrity with independence, position of trust, expert knowledge, accounting as the sine qua non.

**[exam] How the marks are earned**
ICAI's suggested answers list the relevant inclusions (or exclusions) in the Study Material's words, sometimes after a line on the purpose of the audit (RTP May 2025), apply each to the facts and close with a verdict on the contention: "well within the scope of audit", "management's contention is not correct". In audit-versus-investigation answers, the sentence "The scope of audit is general and broad whereas scope of investigation is specific and narrow" appears every time.

### Answer frame

### A case: "Does the matter fall within the scope of audit?"

1. **Provision**: the purpose of an audit (enhancing the degree of confidence of intended users through an opinion), then the inclusion that decides the case, in the Study Material's words (coverage, reliability and sufficiency, proper disclosure), or the exclusion (competence, authentication, investigation).
2. **Facts**: "In the given case, …", with the figures.
3. **Analysis**: link each fact to its inclusion or exclusion.
4. **Conclusion**: "Hence, the matter falls well within the scope of audit; management's contention is not correct", or the reverse.

### "Explain the matters which fall outside the scope of an audit"

The four, each with ICAI's example: preparation and presentation (management's responsibility); duties outside competence (sophisticated machinery, civil structures: engineers); authentication of documents (not an expert); investigation (no powers of search or of recording statements on oath; general and broad versus specific and narrow).

### Three answers to "The notes nobody reads"

**[cols] **Loses marks****

"The auditor checks everything, so all three are in scope."

A conclusion with no provision, and a wrong idea of coverage.
---
**Passes**

The three inclusions listed, then "so all three are within the scope".

The provision is right, but the facts are not linked to it.
---
**Full marks**

Purpose, the three inclusions in ICAI's words, each matter tied to its inclusion (Khurja: coverage; freight: reliability and sufficiency, not authentication; mortgage: disclosure), each of management's arguments answered, and a verdict on every contention.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: Bunty's every-voucher audit** **Fix:** coverage means organising the audit to cover every aspect relevant to the financial statements; the auditor tests samples and does not verify each and every transaction (SM 1.14–1.15, Case Study 1).
- **"Notes and disclosures are the company secretary's business"** **Fix:** proper disclosure of financial information is one of the three inclusions, judged against statutory requirements too.
- **Calling a fraud hunt an audit** **Fix:** that is an investigation; an audit is not an official investigation, and its scope is general and broad, not specific and narrow.
- **The auditor who certifies the machine** **Fix:** both are outside the auditor's competence or expertise; the work belongs to engineers or document experts.
- **"Not an expert, so not my problem"** **Fix:** the auditor does not certify genuineness, but where there is doubt about the reliability of evidence he must investigate further and modify or add procedures (SM 11.13); and a missing lorry receipt is a question of reliability and sufficiency of financial information, which is inside the scope.

### Recap

- Purpose of audit: enhance the degree of confidence of intended users, by an opinion on whether the financial statements are prepared, in all material respects, in accordance with the applicable financial reporting framework (Schedule III for companies).
- Scope includes coverage of all aspects of the entity relevant to the financial statements, reliability and sufficiency of financial information, proper disclosure of financial information, and the opinion.
- The auditor evaluates selection and consistent application of accounting policies; management chooses them.
- Audit is based on historical financial information.
- Scope excludes: preparation and presentation (management); duties outside competence (machinery, buildings); authentication of documents; investigation.
- Investigation: a critical examination of the accounts with a special purpose. Audit general and broad, investigation specific and narrow.
- Engagement: a formal agreement under which the auditor agrees to provide auditing services, in an engagement letter; external audit engagements are reasonable assurance engagements.
- Qualities: tact, caution, firmness, good temper, integrity, discretion, industry, judgment, patience, clear headedness, reliability; integrity backed by independence; accounting knowledge the sine qua non. (Kesar's kiln still needs an engineer.)

## Lesson 1.5: Auditing and its relationship with other disciplines

*Why auditing is interdisciplinary: what the auditor borrows from accounting, law, economics, behavioural science, statistics and mathematics, data processing, financial management and production, in ICAI's words.*

### Key idea

Auditing draws from accountancy, law, behavioural science, statistics, economics and financial management (and, in ICAI's chart, data processing and production). Each gives the auditor a tool; a sound knowledge of accounting principles is the one requirement ICAI calls fundamental.

### Exam angle

**[exam] Where the relationship with other disciplines appeared**
In the ICAI documents read for this lesson, it was examined in 4 of them, once in the new scheme:

- **New scheme, case, 4 marks**: May 2026 Q4(c). An infrastructure company with contractor disputes, pressure on site managers, automated data processing and aggressive working capital management; "explain why an auditor is expected to have working knowledge of disciplines other than auditing", for Statistics & Mathematics, Financial Management, Data Processing and Behavioural Science. ICAI's answer gives each discipline's one-line link from the Study Material, then ties it to a fact of the case.
- **Old scheme**: MTP November 2019 Series I Q2(b), 4 marks ("The relationship between auditing and law is very close one"); RTP November 2020 Q2(b) (accounting and auditing); RTP November 2021 Q2(a) (knowledge of human behaviour).

**[exam] Old answers, new syllabus**
The old-scheme answers draw on the old Study Material and add points the current one does not make (logic, ethics, language, the internal auditor's dealings with people, the law of contracts and negotiable instruments). For January 2027, write the current Study Material's points: the eight one-liners above, with the paragraph's explanation.

### Answer frame

### "Explain why the auditor needs knowledge of X, Y and Z" (a case)

1. One opening line: auditing is interdisciplinary in nature; it draws from diverse subjects and makes use of them.
2. For each discipline asked: its one-line link in the Study Material's words ("Auditing and Behavioural Science: knowledge of human behaviour is essential for an auditor to effectively discharge his duties").
3. Then the fact of the case it explains, and what the auditor does with it.
4. A closing line: mere verification of the records is not enough to form the opinion.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the essay that forgot the case** **Fix:** in May 2026 the marks were for each discipline's link *and* the fact it explains: two sentences per discipline.
- **Statistics without mathematics** **Fix:** ICAI's line has two halves: statistical sampling for meaningful conclusions, and mathematics for verification of inventories.
- **Six disciplines, or the link made up from the name** **Fix:** learn the eight one-liners in ICAI's words ("familiar with the overall economic environment of the client"), and keep their emphasis: knowledge of accounting principles is called a fundamental requirement, and knowledge of human behaviour is called essential.

### Recap

- Auditing is interdisciplinary: it draws from accountancy, law, behavioural science, statistics, economics and financial management; ICAI's chart adds data processing and production.
- Accounting: auditing reviews financial statements, the result of the accounting process; sound knowledge of accounting principles is the fundamental requirement.
- Law: good knowledge of business laws (and taxation laws) affecting the entity.
- Economics: the overall economic environment of the client.
- Behavioural science: human behaviour, because the auditor seeks information and makes inquiries of many people.
- Statistics and mathematics: statistical sampling for meaningful conclusions; mathematics for verification of inventories.
- Data processing: EDP auditing is developing as a discipline in itself. Financial management: working capital, funds flow, ratios, capital budgeting, and financial markets.
- Production: understand the client's business functions, production, cost system, marketing. (Kesar's samosa line counts.)

## Lesson 1.6: Assurance engagements and the engagement and quality control standards

*What an assurance engagement is and its five elements, how audit, review and engagements on prospective information differ in assurance, and which ICAI Standards (SAs, SREs, SAEs, SRSs, SQC 1) govern each.*

### Key idea

An assurance engagement is a practitioner's conclusion that raises the confidence of intended users, other than the responsible party, in a subject matter measured against criteria. Audit gives reasonable (high) assurance, review limited (lower) assurance, prospective information only moderate assurance; related services give none. SAs, SREs, SAEs and SRSs are the Engagement Standards; SQC 1 applies to all of them.

**[exam] The traps**
Three wrong options that recur in ICAI's MCQs and in the Study Material's Case Study 2: a limited assurance engagement obtains "only limited evidence" (it obtains sufficient appropriate evidence and draws limited conclusions); the third party is the bankers, the income tax authorities or the internal audit staff (it is the intended users); SQC 1 does not apply because no audit report is issued, or the work follows the SRSs "and SA 220" (SQC 1 applies to every service covered by Engagement Standards; SA 220 applies to audits only).

### Answer frame

### "Which Standards apply, and what assurance?" (a case)

1. **Provision**: the four families of Engagement Standards, with where each applies, and "SQCs are to be applied for all services covered by Engagement Standards".
2. **Facts**: what is examined (historical or prospective; financial or controls) and whether a conclusion giving assurance is expressed.
3. **Analysis**: the family; the Standard the Study Material names, if any (SRE 2400 (Revised), SRE 2410, SAE 3400, SAE 3420, SRS 4400, SRS 4410 (Revised)); the level of assurance.
4. **Conclusion**: "Hence, the Standards on … are relevant; SQC 1 applies".

### "Distinguish reasonable and limited assurance engagements"

The four rows of ICAI's table: level of assurance; procedures (elaborate and extensive, or fewer); conclusions (reasonable, or limited, both on sufficient appropriate evidence); example (audit, or review).

### "Distinguish historical and prospective financial information" and its effect on assurance

Definitions (past events that have occurred; assumptions about future events and possible actions), then the four things the practitioner obtains evidence on, why no opinion on achievement is possible (future-oriented evidence), the "nothing has come to the practitioner's attention" report, and "moderate".

### "Discuss the elements of an assurance engagement"

The five in ICAI's order, one line each, with the three parties defined.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: "limited assurance, so limited evidence"** **Fix:** a limited assurance engagement involves obtaining **sufficient appropriate evidence** to draw **limited conclusions**; it performs fewer procedures (SM 1.25). This was the wrong statement in MTP September 2026 Series II MCQ 15.
- **The bank as the third party** **Fix:** the third party is the **intended users**, the persons for whom the report is prepared, other than the responsible party.
- **SQC 1 only for audits** **Fix:** SQCs are to be applied for all services covered by Engagement Standards, related services included (SM 1.31; Case Study 2 answer (c), SM 1.36).
- **Certifying the future** **Fix:** he obtains evidence that the assumptions are not unreasonable, the forecast is properly prepared and presented and material assumptions are disclosed; he cannot give an opinion on achievement.
- **"Assurance means historical financial information"** **Fix:** assurance engagements are not restricted to audit, nor to historical financial information: prospective information and controls are subject matters of assurance engagements too (SM 1.25, 1.38–1.40).

### Recap

- Assurance engagement: a practitioner expresses a conclusion designed to enhance the degree of confidence of intended users other than the responsible party about the evaluation or measurement of a subject matter against criteria.
- Five elements: a three party relationship (practitioner, responsible party, intended users), an appropriate subject matter, suitable criteria, sufficient appropriate evidence and a written assurance report in appropriate form.
- Audit: reasonable assurance (high), elaborate and extensive procedures. Review: limited assurance (lower), fewer procedures, still sufficient appropriate evidence, limited conclusions.
- Prospective financial information: based on assumptions about future events and possible actions; evidence on assumptions not unreasonable, proper preparation, presentation and disclosure; no opinion on achievement; "nothing has come to our attention"; moderate assurance.
- Engagement Standards: SAs (audit of HFI), SREs (review of HFI), SAEs (other assurance), SRSs (agreed-upon procedures, compilation). Purpose: high quality standards and guidance.
- SQC 1: firm's system of quality control; applies to all services covered by Engagement Standards.
- Standards needed for benchmarks at par with global practice, better reporting, uniformity, knowledge and skill, audit quality.
- Departure: document how alternatives achieve the purpose, and the reason unless clear; report draws attention; disclosure does not absolve. (Dev's stamp stays undiscounted.)
