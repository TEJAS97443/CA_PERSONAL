## Lesson 4.1: SA 500: sufficient appropriate audit evidence

*What counts as audit evidence, why "sufficient" means quantity and "appropriate" means quality, how reliability is judged, and what SA 500 asks when evidence comes from management's expert or disagrees with itself.*

### Key idea

Audit evidence must be sufficient (more of it when the item is material, risky or from a large, mixed population) and appropriate (relevant to the assertion tested, and reliable given its source, nature and circumstances). More evidence may not make up for poor quality.

### Exam angle

**[exam] Where SA 500 appeared, May 2024 to September 2026**
In the ICAI documents read for this lesson, SA 500 was examined in 17 questions across 10 documents.

- **Descriptive, 4 or 5 marks.** The five generalisations about reliability: MTP January 2025 Series I Q5(b) (5 marks). How the auditor obtains sufficient appropriate audit evidence, answered with the SM 4.12 paragraph: MTP January 2026 Series I Q3(a) (5 marks). Relevance and the direction of testing, on the accounts payable example: MTP January 2026 Series I Q1(c) (4 marks). Using a management's expert: an actuary for gratuity, September 2024 Q5(b) (4 marks); chartered engineers valuing inventory, MTP September 2024 Series I Q1(c) (4 marks). Selecting specific items for testing (the factors, and the items that may be selected): May 2024 Q4(a) (4 marks).
- **Multiple choice, 2 marks each.** Definition, and accounting records versus other information: MTP January 2026 Series I MCQs 1–3. The factor that does *not* affect sufficiency: September 2025 MCQ 6 (nature and source of evidence), RTP January 2025 MCQ 5. Internal or external: May 2025 MCQ 13 (a purchase invoice is not internal); RTP September 2026 MCQ 8 (bank statements and shipping bill data downloaded from official portals, and supplier quotations in the company's inbox, stay external; the company's own delivery challans are internal). Weak records and oral explanations: MTP May 2026 Series II MCQ 6. An independent source for completeness: RTP September 2026 MCQ 7. 100% examination unlikely for tests of controls but proper for tests of details on automated, repetitive calculations: September 2025 MCQ 4, MTP January 2025 Series I MCQ 1.

**[exam] How the marks are earned**
Descriptive answers reproduce the Study Material's sentences and lists in its order: the definition; "sufficiency is the measure of the quantity"; "appropriateness is the measure of the quality; that is, its relevance and its reliability"; the five generalisations; the three factors with "less evidence ... more evidence".

**[trick] SQ-AQ, and MRP**
**S**ufficiency = **Q**uantity, moved by **M**ateriality, **R**isk and **P**opulation ("MRP", the price tag on how much evidence you buy). **A**ppropriateness = **Q**uality = **R**elevance + **R**eliability. Quality can reduce the quantity needed; quantity may not repair quality.

### Answer frame

### A case question: provision, facts, analysis, conclusion

1. **Relevant provision**: "As per SA 500, …" only what the case needs (sufficiency, appropriateness, the reliability generalisation that applies, management's expert).
2. **Facts of the case**: the source, form and origin of the evidence; the size and risk of the item.
3. **Analysis**: relevant to the assertion? Reliable, and why? Enough, given materiality and risk?
4. **Conclusion**: "Hence, the evidence is (not) sufficient appropriate audit evidence; the auditor should …", naming the further procedures.

### "Explain sufficiency of audit evidence and the factors affecting it" (5 marks)

1. Sufficiency is the measure of the quantity of audit evidence.
2. Quantity needed is affected by assessed risks (higher risk, more evidence) and by quality (higher quality, less may be required); more evidence may not compensate for poor quality.
3. Materiality: less material, less evidence; more material, more.
4. Risk of material misstatement (inherent and control risk): lower risk, less; higher risk, more.
5. Size and characteristics of the population: smaller and homogeneous, less; larger and heterogeneous, more.

### "Distinguish sufficiency and appropriateness"

| Point | Sufficiency | Appropriateness |
|---|---|---|
| Measures | Quantity | Quality |
| Made of | Enough evidence | Relevance and reliability |
| Affected by | Materiality, risk of material misstatement, population | Source, nature, circumstances, controls |
| Link | Higher quality, less may be required | Quantity may not compensate for poor quality |

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the 400-page box file** **Fix:** quantity asks "is there enough?", quality asks "is it relevant and reliable?". More copies of internal documents add no independence.
- **"External, therefore reliable"** **Fix:** an external source may not be knowledgeable, a related party may not be independent (greater vigilance), and a management's expert may lack objectivity (SM 4.7, 4.10).
- **The purchase invoice filed as internal evidence** **Fix:** classify by where evidence originates, not where it is filed. The supplier's invoice to Tapri is external, even after it is scanned into Tapri's system (SM 4.7, 4.127).
- **"Nature of evidence" added to the sufficiency factors** **Fix:** the three factors are materiality, risk of material misstatement, and size and characteristics of the population. Source and nature go to reliability. ICAI's September 2025 MCQ 6 was built on this trap.

### Recap

- Audit evidence: information used by the auditor in arriving at the conclusions on which the opinion is based; accounting records plus other information.
- Objective: design and perform procedures to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence to draw reasonable conclusions on which to base the opinion.
- Sufficiency = quantity; appropriateness = quality = relevance + reliability. Higher risk needs more; higher quality needs less; more may not compensate for poor quality.
- Sufficiency factors: materiality, risk of material misstatement, size and characteristics of the population.
- Reliability rises with independent outside sources, effective controls, direct evidence, documentary form and originals (with exceptions).
- Evidence includes contradicting information and, sometimes, the absence of information.
- Management's expert: evaluate competence, capabilities and objectivity; understand the work; evaluate it as evidence for the assertion.
- Inconsistent or doubtful evidence: modify or add procedures; consider the effect on the rest of the audit. (Four hundred photocopies of one voice are still one voice.)

## Lesson 4.2: Financial statement assertions and audit procedures

*The claims hidden in every line of the financial statements (assertions), the seven procedures auditors use to test them, and why each procedure proves some assertions and not others.*

### Key idea

Financial statements are bundles of management's assertions. The auditor picks, for each assertion, a procedure that can actually prove it: evidence for one assertion (existence) is no substitute for evidence about another (ownership, valuation, cut-off).

### Exam angle

**[exam] Where assertions and procedures appeared, May 2024 to September 2026**
In the ICAI documents read for this lesson, this topic was examined in 27 questions across 10 documents, most of them 2-mark MCQs inside case scenarios.

- **Name the assertion (MCQs).** MTP May 2024 Series II MCQs 1.1, 1.3–1.5 (employee benefits; allowance for doubtful debtors is valuation; invoices in a director's name fail rights and obligations; foreign currency balance restated is valuation). MTP January 2025 Series I MCQs 2–4 (answer "Measurement, Measurement", Chapter 5's word; consignee stock is completeness and rights and obligations). MTP January 2026 Series II MCQs 4–5 (occurrence against completeness). MTP September 2026 Series I MCQs 2 and 6 (loan restated at closing rate: valuation and allocation; original fixed deposit receipts: existence). RTP September 2026 MCQs 2, 4 and 6 (inadequate provision for doubtful debts; purchases near the year end untested, a cut-off risk; foreign currency loan restated, valuation). RTP January 2025 MCQ 8 (ageing analysis for doubtful debts: valuation).
- **Match four procedures to four assertions.** May 2026 MCQ 5, on receivables: ICAI's key pairs the confirmation requests with existence, the ageing review with valuation, the matching of invoices and despatch records around the reporting date with cut-off, and the remaining procedure (invoices selected from the ageing report and compared with supporting documents) with completeness. Place the pairs you are sure of first; the last one falls into place.
- **Name the procedure.** MTP May 2025 Series II Q1(c) (4 marks: inspection, observation, recalculation, analytical procedures). MTP September 2026 Series I MCQs 1 and 3 (reading published annual reports is inspection; confirmations are substantive). RTP January 2025 MCQs 2 and 4. MTP January 2026 Series II Q3(a) (5 marks: inspection, its reliability, and the assertions a physical inspection does not prove); MTP May 2026 Series II MCQ 13.
- **Descriptive "identify and explain".** MTP May 2025 Series II Q2(a) (5 marks, five procedures to five assertions; the suggested answer names each assertion and quotes its meaning in Chapter 5's words); its Q6(c) OR (4 marks, occurrence of purchases). MTP January 2026 Series I Q2(a) (5 marks): what the completeness assertion requires for inventory (stock with a third-party transporter included, consignment goods excluded) and the procedures to verify it.

**[exam] How the marks are earned**
Name the assertion, then quote its meaning in ICAI's words ("Existence: assets, liabilities and equity interests exist"), then one line linking it to the facts. For procedures, name the procedure and give its definition. One-word answers lose most of the marks.

**[trick] Which way is the test pointing?**
From the books to the evidence: "is what is recorded real?" (occurrence, existence). From the evidence to the books: "is everything real recorded?" (completeness). The Study Material's accounts payable example (SM 4.9) is this rule. Holds for tests of details; cut-off is tested at the year-end boundary in both directions. In a matching MCQ, settle the unambiguous pairs first (May 2026 MCQ 5, above).

### Answer frame

### "Identify and explain the assertion" (1 mark a part)

1. **Name** the assertion in the Study Material's term (Chapter 4's list, or Chapter 5's where the question uses captions).
2. **Quote** its meaning: "Cut-off: transactions and events have been recorded in the correct accounting period."
3. **Apply**: one sentence on why the procedure tests it.

### "Distinguish tests of controls and substantive procedures"

| Point | Tests of controls | Substantive procedures |
|---|---|---|
| Designed to | Evaluate the operating effectiveness of controls | Detect material misstatements at the assertion level |
| Looks for | Performance of the control, and deviations | Misstatements in the assertion |
| Comprise | One kind of further audit procedure | Tests of details (transactions: vouching; balances: verification) and substantive analytical procedures |
| When | When required by the SAs or when the auditor has chosen to do so | For each material class of transactions, account balance and disclosure, irrespective of the assessed risks (SM 3.70) |

How the two are planned and combined is [[aud.risk.sa330|SA 330]].

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the van that was counted, so it must be ours** **Fix:** physical inspection gives reliable evidence of existence, not necessarily of ownership (rights and obligations) or amount (valuation); those need documents and calculations (SM 4.16).
- **Occurrence and completeness swapped** **Fix:** "no ghost employees on the payroll" is occurrence; "every employee's cost is accounted for" is completeness.
- **Reperformance called recalculation** **Fix:** recalculation checks arithmetic; reperformance independently redoes a procedure or control the entity performed (a reconciliation, an ageing).
- **Inquiry treated as proof** **Fix:** inquiry alone ordinarily does not give sufficient evidence of the absence of material misstatement or of the operating effectiveness of controls; corroborate it.

### Recap

- Assertions: representations by management, explicit or otherwise, embodied in the financial statements, used by the auditor to consider potential misstatements.
- Transactions: occurrence, completeness, accuracy, cut-off, classification. Balances: existence, rights and obligations, completeness, valuation and allocation. Disclosure: occurrence and rights and obligations, completeness, classification and understandability, accuracy and valuation.
- Chapter 5 captions: income statement (occurrence, completeness, cut-off, measurement, presentation and disclosure); balance sheet (existence, completeness, cut-off, valuation, rights and obligations, presentation and disclosure).
- Procedures: inspection, observation, external confirmation, recalculation, reperformance, analytical procedures, inquiry.
- Further audit procedures: tests of controls and substantive procedures (tests of details: transactions, balances; substantive analytical procedures).
- Inquiry alone ordinarily does not prove the absence of misstatement or that controls operated.
- Inspecting an asset proves existence, not necessarily ownership or value. (Seeing that he is six feet tall tells you nothing about the flat.)

## Lesson 4.3: SA 610: using the work of internal auditors

*When the external auditor may use the internal audit function's work or its people, the three tests (objectivity, competence, a systematic approach), and why the opinion stays the external auditor's alone.*

### Key idea

Using internal auditors never shares the opinion: ==the external auditor has sole responsibility for the audit opinion expressed==. Their work is usable only if objectivity, competence and a systematic and disciplined approach are all present; the more judgment or risk an area involves, the less of it is used.

### Exam angle

**[exam] Where SA 610 appeared, May 2024 to September 2026**
SA 610 was examined in 9 of the ICAI documents read for this lesson:

- **Descriptive, 3 to 5 marks.** Sole responsibility, and what to consider before relying on internal audit (a partner blamed the internal auditor for a wrong ageing): January 2025 Q4(a) (5 marks). When to use less of the work: January 2026 Q6(b) (5 marks). Effective coordination: May 2024 Q3(d) (3 marks). Direct assistance, the "shall not" procedures and the written agreements: September 2026 Q1(b) (3 + 2 marks; the facts suggested management's written approval alone was enough; answers not yet published) and RTP January 2025 Q12. Checking an ageing's accuracy (assignable) versus evaluating the provision on it (not): MTP May 2024 Series I Q6(a) (4 marks).
- **Multiple choice, 2 marks.** Relying on internal audit instead of performing procedures is wrong because the statutory auditor has sole responsibility for the opinion: MTP May 2025 Series I MCQ 4. Going concern evaluation is not for direct assistance: MTP September 2025 Series I MCQ 2. A control failure is internal audit's to report, and no extensive reliance on an internal auditor not reporting to higher management when risk is high: MTP September 2025 Series II MCQs 3 and 4.

**[exam] How the marks are earned**
The suggested answers open with "As per SA 610", quote the list the question needs in the Standard's words and order, apply it to each fact and end with a clear yes or no. Phrases that carry marks: "sole responsibility for the audit opinion expressed", "organisational status and relevant policies and procedures", "systematic and disciplined approach, including quality control", "more than limited judgment", "direction, supervision and review".

**[trick] JROC: when to do more yourself**
**J**udgement more, **R**isk higher, **O**bjectivity support weaker, **C**ompetence lower: each moves work back to the external auditor. Condition: JROC decides *how much* to use once all three tests are passed; failing a test means none is used.

**[trick] SHOD: the four closed doors of direct assistance**
**S**ignificant judgments, **H**igher risk needing more than limited judgment, **O**wn work already reported, **D**ecisions about internal audit itself. Typical assignable work: recalculating, tracing, checking the accuracy of a listing.

### Answer frame

### A case question: provision, facts, analysis, conclusion

1. **Relevant provision**: "As per SA 610, …", only the list the question needs: the three tests; the "shall not use" conditions; JROC; SHOD; the written agreements. For "what should the auditor consider before relying on internal audit", ICAI's answer (January 2025 Q4(a)) gave the three objectives and then the three tests.
2. **Facts of the case**: who internal audit reports to, its other duties, staffing and methods, the area's risk and judgment.
3. **Analysis**: each fact against each condition, one line each.
4. **Conclusion**: can the work (or the person) be used, for what, and what must be done first; sole responsibility where the question invites it.

### Three answers to "Can Lakshmi give the Dewas impairment estimate to internal audit?"

**[cols] **Loses marks****

"No, because internal auditors are not independent."

True, but not SA 610's reason; no provision, no facts.
---
**Passes**

"No. SA 610 does not allow direct assistance on higher-risk areas."

Half the condition: higher-risk work is barred only "where the judgment required is more than limited" (task (v) is higher risk and still assignable).
---
**Full marks**

The "shall not" conditions in full, the facts (a significant risk; estimating an impairment loss is a significant judgment), the conclusion, and that routine tasks may still be assigned after the written agreements.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: "the internal auditor is responsible"** **Fix:** the external auditor has sole responsibility for the opinion; using internal audit's work or people never reduces it.
- **The brilliant team that reports to the CFO** **Fix:** neither objectivity nor competence compensates for the other. If status and policies do not adequately support objectivity, the work shall not be used.
- **Direct assistance on the judgment calls** **Fix:** significant judgments, and higher assessed risks where the judgment required is more than limited, stay with the external auditor.
- **Management's nod instead of two written agreements** **Fix:** before direct assistance, obtain written agreement from an authorised representative of the entity (follow instructions; no intervention) **and** from the internal auditors (confidentiality; report threats to objectivity).

### Recap

- SA 610 uses of internal audit: risk assessment information; its work in partial substitution for direct evidence; direct assistance (the last two unless law or regulation prohibits or restricts them).
- The external auditor has sole responsibility for the opinion; using internal audit never reduces it, and SA 610 never requires its use.
- Three tests: objectivity supported by status and policies; competence; a systematic and disciplined approach including quality control. Fail one, and none of the work is used.
- Objectivity and competence are a continuum, but neither compensates for the other.
- Use less and do more directly the more judgment, the higher the risk, the weaker the objectivity, the lower the competence (JROC).
- Using the work: discuss it, read the reports, perform sufficient procedures on the body of work.
- Direct assistance: evaluate threats (with inquiry) and competence; never for significant judgments, higher risks needing more than limited judgment, their own reported work, or decisions about internal audit itself (SHOD); two written agreements first.
- Bunty's cupboard can save time, not responsibility.

## Lesson 4.4: Internal financial controls and ICFR

*What internal financial controls are, who answers for them under the Companies Act, 2013, which companies' auditors must report on them, and why IFC is wider than internal control over financial reporting.*

### Key idea

==IFC is a wider term; ICFR is narrower==, restricted to internal controls over financial reporting. Directors and management have primary responsibility for internal financial controls; the auditor evaluates, validates and reports on their design and operating effectiveness, in a report separate from the opinion on the financial statements.

### Exam angle

**[exam] Where IFC appeared**
In the documents read for this lesson, IFC was examined in 3 new-scheme papers and 6 earlier-scheme ones (Paper 6, Auditing and Assurance).

- **New scheme.** The four responsibilities (directors, independent directors, audit committee) by section number: May 2024 Q1(c) (3 marks). A case MCQ, "which of the following does not fall within the nature of responsibility": the distractor was a board-appointed expert certifying that IFC is commensurate with the business: September 2024 MCQ 3, repeated almost word for word as May 2026 MCQ 14 (2 marks each).
- **Study Material MCQs.** Who maintains effective IFC: management (SM 3.79–3.81, Test Your Knowledge MCQ 3). Which is not an objective of IFC policies: "assessing audit risk" (SM 4.122, 4.127, MCQ 3).
- **Earlier scheme.** Distinguish IFC and ICFR: January 2021 Q3(b) (4 marks). IFC reporting and interim financial statements: November 2019 Q1(a), MTP July 2021 Series II Q4(a), MTP May 2023 Series II Q1(v). Meaning, the reporting requirement and its applicability: RTP May 2022 Q14. The auditor reports on internal financial controls, not internal controls in general: MTP November 2020 Series I Q1.

**[exam] How the marks are earned**
For the responsibilities question, ICAI's answer is the Study Material's table reproduced: section number, then the responsibility in the Act's words. For "distinguish", the marks are for the meaning of each, the "in addition to and distinct from" point and the "wider versus narrower" conclusion.

**[trick] 134, 177, 149, 143: who does what**
**134** the board *states*, **177** the audit committee *evaluates*, **149** independent directors *satisfy themselves*, **143** the auditor *reports*. Conditions: the section 134(5)(e) statement is for listed companies; the section 143(3)(i) report is not required for the exempt private companies.

### Answer frame

### "Explain the responsibilities under the Companies Act, 2013 for IFC"

1. Opening line: the Act places greater emphasis on effective implementation and reporting on internal controls, and uses "internal financial controls" to cast responsibilities.
2. The four provisions, each with its party and the responsibility in the Act's words (the table in section 2 above).
3. Closing line: directors and management have primary responsibility; auditors evaluate, validate and report on design and operating effectiveness.

### "Is reporting under section 143(3)(i) required?"

1. The provision and the three exemptions, in full.
2. The facts: public or private; OPC or small; turnover as per the latest audited financial statements; the highest borrowings at any point of time during the year.
3. Conclusion for each company, saying which test fails.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the year-end borrowing balance** **Fix:** the limit is for borrowings at any point of time during the financial year, and turnover and borrowings must both be below their limits.
- **Exempting a small public company** **Fix:** the exemptions are for private companies only. A public company, listed or not, gets IFC reporting whatever its size.
- **Making the auditor the owner of the controls** **Fix:** directors and management implement and maintain internal financial controls; the auditor evaluates, validates and reports on them.

### Recap

- IFC: policies and procedures for reliable financial reporting, effective and efficient operations, compliance with laws, safeguarding of assets, and prevention and detection of frauds.
- Section 134(5)(e): in listed companies, the Directors' Responsibility Statement says IFC was laid down, is adequate and was operating effectively.
- Section 177(4)(vii): the audit committee evaluates IFC and risk management systems. Section 149(8) with Schedule IV: independent directors satisfy themselves that financial controls are robust and defensible.
- Section 143(3)(i): the auditor reports on adequate IFC with reference to financial statements and their operating effectiveness, in a separate report (Annexure A).
- Exempt: private companies that are OPCs, small companies, or have turnover below ₹50 crore (latest audited) and borrowings below ₹25 crore at all times in the year.
- Directors and management have primary responsibility; auditors evaluate, validate and report.
- IFC is the wider term; ICFR the narrower; an ICFR opinion is in addition to and distinct from the opinion on the financial statements.

## Lesson 4.5: SA 530: audit sampling

*How an auditor tests less than 100% of a population and still concludes on all of it: population and sampling unit, statistical or not, sample design and size, the five selection methods, and evaluating deviations and misstatements.*

### Key idea

A sample earns the right to speak for the population only if it is representative: an appropriate, complete population; a size that reduces sampling risk to an acceptably low level; a selection that gives every sampling unit a chance; and results that are investigated, projected and evaluated.

### Exam angle

**[exam] Where SA 530 appeared, May 2024 to September 2026**
In the ICAI documents read for this lesson, SA 530 was examined in 16 questions across 10 documents.

- **Descriptive, 3 to 5 marks.** Statistical sampling "more scientific": September 2024 Q3(d) (3 marks); with random sampling for over a lakh of sales, September 2025 Q4(c) (4 marks). Systematic sampling, its pattern risk and multiple starts, plus "the first 150 accounts" as block sampling: May 2026 Q1(b) (5 marks) and MTP September 2024 Series II Q1(b) (5 marks). Desired assurance and stratification as sample-size factors: MTP May 2026 Series I Q3(a) (5 marks). Projecting misstatements: November 2020 (old scheme) Q2(c) (3 marks).
- **Multiple choice, 2 marks each.** Naming the method: RTP January 2025 MCQ 1; MTP May 2025 Series II MCQ 3 (every 11th of 550 creditors); MTP September 2025 Series I MCQs 4–5 (population 100, interval 5, sample 20). Sample-size factors: September 2025 MCQ 5; MTP September 2024 Series I MCQ 8. Sampling and non-sampling risk: September 2025 MCQ 3, May 2026 MCQ 2. Anomalies: May 2026 MCQ 3; MTP September 2024 Series I MCQ 9 (projected plus anomalous misstatement as the best estimate; its wrong options say that a sample whose projected misstatement exceeds tolerable misstatement still "provides a reasonable basis").

**[exam] A key to know: "transactions exceeding ₹8,000"**
In RTP January 2025 MCQ 1, the four selections were random number tables, no structured method, "transactions exceeding ₹8,000" and "the first 200 sales invoices of July", and ICAI's key reads random, haphazard, **monetary unit** and block sampling. In the Study Material, examining all items whose recorded values exceed a certain amount is one of SA 500's ways of *selecting specific items* (SM 4.31), while monetary unit sampling is value-weighted selection of individual monetary units (SM 4.56, 4.63). If an MCQ forces a choice among sampling methods for a value-based selection, monetary unit sampling is the option ICAI keyed; in a descriptive answer, use the Study Material's terms.

**[exam] How the marks are earned**
Theory answers reproduce the Study Material's lists in order: "more scientific" (three points), the six advantages, the factor tables with "increase / decrease". Method questions want the name, a one-line description and the caution (pattern for systematic, inferences for block, bias for haphazard).

**[trick] Sample size: worry up, tolerance down**
Anything that makes the auditor **want more assurance or expect more errors** (more reliance on controls, higher expected deviation or misstatement, higher risk of material misstatement, higher desired assurance) **increases** the sample. Anything that makes the auditor **tolerate more or rely on something else** (higher tolerable rate or misstatement, other substantive procedures on the same assertion, stratification) **decreases** it. Population size: negligible for large populations (except monetary unit sampling's value effect).

### Answer frame

### A selection-method case (5 marks)

1. **Name** the method: "CA R is using systematic (interval) sampling."
2. **Explain** it with the case's numbers: "interval = 1,000 ÷ 100 = 10; every 10th account from the start."
3. **Caution**: the interval must not correspond with a pattern in the population.
4. **Remedy**: more than one random starting point.
5. **Second method**: "the first 150 accounts" is block sampling, rarely appropriate for conclusions on the population.

### "Distinguish statistical and non-statistical sampling"

| Point | Statistical | Non-statistical |
|---|---|---|
| Selection | Random selection of sample items | Auditor's experience and knowledge |
| Evaluation | Probability theory, including measurement of sampling risk | Judgment; closeness to the population cannot be measured |
| Bias | No personal bias | Personal bias cannot be eliminated |
| Strength | Objective and defensible; sampling risk and precision can be measured | Simplicity in operation |
| Leans on | Wide application to large populations of similar items | The auditor's experience and knowledge of the client's business to evaluate the findings |

Common to both: the sample must be representative, and sample size is not a valid criterion to distinguish the two (SM 4.47, 4.51).

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the 120 samples of café 17** **Fix:** check the population's structure before using an interval; use multiple random starts, random selection or stratification.
- **"Stratified sampling is for homogeneous populations"** **Fix:** the reverse: it divides a heterogeneous population into homogeneous sub-populations (SM 4.55, 4.128).
- **Projecting a stratum's result to the whole population** **Fix:** results from a stratum are projected only to that stratum; the strata are then combined (SM 4.55–4.56, 4.129).
- **Calling the first misstatement an anomaly** **Fix:** an anomaly needs a high degree of certainty, from additional procedures, that it is not representative; otherwise project it.

### Recap

- Audit sampling: procedures on less than 100% of a population, all sampling units having a chance of selection, to give a reasonable basis for conclusions about the whole.
- Population must be appropriate (direction of testing), complete and reliable; the sample must be representative.
- Sampling risk: the sample's conclusion differs from the population's; type (i) hurts effectiveness (the main concern), type (ii) efficiency. Non-sampling risk: any other reason.
- Statistical sampling: random selection plus probability theory to evaluate results; sample size does not distinguish it.
- Process: design, size, selection, procedures, nature and cause, projection, evaluation.
- Size up: more reliance on controls, higher expected errors, higher risk, more assurance. Size down: higher tolerable rate or misstatement, other substantive procedures, stratification.
- Methods: random (simple, stratified), systematic (interval = population ÷ sample size; watch for patterns), monetary unit, haphazard (no conscious bias), block (rarely appropriate).
- Untestable item: deviation or misstatement. Anomaly only with a high degree of certainty. Tests of controls: sample deviation rate = projected rate. (Stir the chai before you taste it.)

## Lesson 4.6: SA 501: inventory, litigation and segment information

*The three items SA 501 singles out, and the special evidence each needs: attending the inventory count, writing to the entity's lawyers about litigation, and checking how segment information was built.*

### Key idea

SA 501's objective is sufficient appropriate audit evidence about the ==existence and condition of inventory==, the completeness of litigation and claims, and the presentation and disclosure of segment information. - SA 501 objective: evidence on existence and condition of inventory, completeness of litigation and claims, and presentation and disclosure of segment information.

- General inconvenience is not impracticability; difficulty, time or cost is not in itself a valid basis to omit a procedure for which there is no alternative.
- Third-party inventory: confirmation of quantities and condition, inspection or other procedures, or both.
- Litigation: inquiries (including in-house counsel), minutes and lawyer correspondence, legal expense accounts; on a risk, a letter of inquiry to external counsel, specific if a general one will not be answered.
- Refusal of access to counsel plus failed alternatives: modify under SA 705.
- Segments: understand and test management's methods, plus analytical procedures; the responsibility is for the financial statements as a whole. (Bunty's spreadsheet is not a warehouse.)

## Lesson 4.7: SA 505: external confirmations

*How the auditor gets written evidence straight from third parties: keeping control of the requests, positive and negative confirmations, what to do when management refuses, and how to read the replies.*

### Key idea

SA 505's core rule: ==the auditor shall maintain control over external confirmation requests==, from choosing the information and the confirming party to designing, addressing, sending and following up. A reply that passes through the entity is not a direct response to the auditor. Negative confirmations give significantly less persuasive evidence than positive ones.

### Exam angle

**[exam] Where SA 505 appeared, May 2024 to September 2026**
SA 505 was examined in 11 of the ICAI documents read for this lesson.

- **Negative confirmations, the favourite.** Which request suits many small retail debtors with low risk and effective controls, and the conditions: May 2025 Q4(a) (1 + 4 marks). Conditions, and whether negative confirmations are as persuasive as positive ones: MTP September 2024 Series I Q4(a) (5 marks). Negative requests to a few large high-risk balances are inappropriate: MTP September 2024 Series II MCQ 1. Confirming parties respond more readily when the information is not in their favour: September 2025 MCQ 9.
- **Management's refusal, 4 to 5 marks.** The three steps, TCWG and SA 705: MTP January 2025 Series II Q1(b) (5 marks), MTP September 2025 Series I Q1(c) (4 marks).
- **Definitions and evaluation.** Name the term for five definitions: RTP January 2026 Q12. A positive request answered with a different amount is an exception: September 2024 Q6(c). Classifying 25 results: MTP May 2026 Series I Q4(c). Evaluating mixed results (agreements, a doubtful email reply, non-responses, a dispute) and the four categories: MTP September 2026 Series II Q1(a) (5 marks). Confirmations obtained through the client are not in line with SA 505: RTP September 2024 MCQ 3.

**[exam] How the marks are earned**
The suggested answers quote SA 505's list in order (the four elements of control; the three refusal steps; the four negative-confirmation conditions; the four result categories), then apply it to the facts. In "do you agree" questions the conclusion must say *why*, for example "significantly less persuasive audit evidence than a response to a positive confirmation request".

**[trick] ISDS: keep control**
**I**nformation, **S**electing the party, **D**esigning (properly addressed, replies to the auditor), **S**ending (with follow-ups). If any of the four sits with the client, control is lost.

**[trick] Negative only if: Low, Large, Little, Listening**
**Low** risk with controls tested, a **large** number of small homogeneous items, **little** exception expected, recipients **listening** (no reason to ignore the request). All four, or no negative requests as the sole substantive procedure.

### Answer frame

### A case question on SA 505

1. **Relevant provision**: "As per SA 505, External Confirmations, …", the list the facts call for.
2. **Facts of the case**: who controlled the requests; the type of request; the population and risk; the refusal and its reason; the replies.
3. **Analysis**: element by element, condition by condition, reply by reply.
4. **Conclusion**: proper or not; which request type; justified refusal or TCWG and SA 705; what further procedures.

### "Is the CFO's refusal justified?"

**[cols] **Loses marks****

"No. The auditor must always confirm."

Ignores SA 505's steps and the legitimate reasons.
---
**Passes**

"The auditor inquires into the reasons and performs alternative procedures."

Two of the three steps; no test of reasonableness.
---
**Full marks**

All three steps; the common reason (legal dispute or negotiation) and the fraud risk; a conclusion on the facts; and what follows if the refusal is unreasonable or alternatives fail (TCWG under SA 260, SA 705).

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: replies forwarded by the client** **Fix:** the response must be a direct written response to the auditor; the auditor sends the requests and receives the replies.
- **Silence counted as agreement** **Fix:** a failure to respond to a negative request does not show the request was received or the information verified; it is significantly less persuasive evidence.
- **Negative requests for the big balances** **Fix:** negative requests can be the sole substantive procedure only with low risk and tested controls, many small homogeneous items, a very low expected exception rate, and no reason for recipients to ignore them.
- **Every exception is a misstatement** **Fix:** analyse the reason first: payments and goods in transit are timing differences; disputes may be misstatements, to be assessed against the whole population.

### Recap

- External confirmation: audit evidence obtained as a direct written response to the auditor from a third party, on paper or by electronic or other medium.
- Positive: reply in all cases (agree, disagree or fill in); negative: reply only if disagreeing; non-response: no or incomplete reply to a positive request, or returned undelivered; exception: a difference.
- Control: determine the information, select the confirming party, design (properly addressed, replies to the auditor), send and follow up.
- Blank positive requests reduce agree-without-checking, but lower response rates.
- Refusal: inquire and test the reasons, re-assess risks (including fraud), perform alternatives; if unreasonable or alternatives fail, TCWG under SA 260 and SA 705.
- Negative requests as the sole substantive procedure only if: low risk with controls tested, many small homogeneous items, very low expected exceptions, no reason to disregard.
- Results: agreement, unreliable, non-response, exception; evaluate, then decide on further procedures (SA 330).
- If the reply went to sales@client, it confirmed nothing.

## Lesson 4.8: SA 510: opening balances in initial engagements

*Why a first-year auditor must obtain evidence on the balances the year started with, the three-step procedure SA 510 prescribes, and when the problem leads to a qualified, adverse or disclaimer of opinion.*

### Key idea

Opening balances are not last year's problem: they flow into this year's profit. A predecessor's audit helps the new auditor get evidence about them; it never replaces getting it.

### Formats and formulas

### Why an opening figure is a this-year figure

**[formula] Cost of goods sold**
$$\text{Cost of goods sold} = \qquad \text{Opening inventory} + \text{Purchases} - \text{Closing inventory}$$

Every rupee of overstatement in opening inventory is a rupee of overstated cost of goods sold, and so a rupee of understated profit, this year.

Tapri Cold Chain's 2025-26 figures (₹ crore) show it. Suppose ₹0.90 crore of the ₹15 crore opening stock of frozen snacks had in fact spoiled before 31 March 2025:

| Particulars | As recorded | If opening stock was right |
|---|---:|---:|
| Opening inventory | 15.00 | 14.10 |
| Add: purchases | 117.00 | 117.00 |
| Less: closing inventory | 33.00 | 33.00 |
| **Cost of goods sold** | **99.00** | **98.10** |

This year's cost of goods sold is overstated, and its profit understated, by ₹0.90 crore, though the error was made last year.

### The reporting grid

*Reproduce this from memory: each row is a separate requirement in SA 510.*

| Problem with opening balances | Opinion (SA 705) |
|---|---|
| Sufficient appropriate evidence cannot be obtained | Qualified or disclaimer |
| Material misstatement not properly accounted for, presented or disclosed | Qualified or adverse |
| Policies not consistently applied, or a change not properly accounted for, presented or disclosed | Qualified or adverse |

### Exam angle

**[exam] Where SA 510 appeared, May 2024 to September 2026**
In the ICAI documents read for this lesson, SA 510 was asked 8 times in 7 new-scheme documents:

- **The new auditor who relies on the predecessor**, 4 or 5 marks: MTP September 2025 Series II Q1(c) (4 marks); MTP May 2026 Series II Q3(a) (5 marks: examine the approach, state the procedures). The Study Material's own Test Your Knowledge has the same case (Q8, SM 4.125).

- **Factors deciding the nature and extent**: MTP January 2026 Series II Q4(a) (5 marks, a predecessor's qualification on receivables); MTP September 2026 Series II Q2(c) (4 marks).

4. **Conclusion**: "Hence, the approach of … is not correct", or the opinion: qualified or disclaimer; qualified or adverse.

|---|---|---|

**[cols] **Loses marks****

"Not correct, because the auditor must verify opening balances."

Right conclusion, no definition, no procedure.
---
**Passes**

Defines an initial audit engagement, applies it to the facts, concludes "not correct".

---
**Full marks**

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: Bunty's borrowed tick** **Fix:** an engagement after a predecessor is an initial audit engagement by definition. Perusing the predecessor's audited statements is one way to get evidence, inside procedure (c); it is not an exemption from (a), (b) and (c).
- **The closing count that "proves" opening stock** **Fix:** this year's closing inventory procedures give little evidence of opening inventory; reconcile the current count back to opening quantities, test opening valuation, and test gross profit and cut-off.
- **Mixing up the reporting pairs** **Fix:** missing evidence: qualified or disclaimer. Misstatement, or inconsistent policies: qualified or adverse.
- **The new partner who thought the audit was "initial"** **Fix:** a predecessor auditor is one "from a different audit firm". ### Recap

- Initial audit engagement: the prior period was not audited, or was audited by a predecessor auditor (from a different audit firm).
- Opening balances: balances at the start of the period, built from last year's closing balances and policies, including contingencies and commitments existing then.
- Objective: evidence whether opening balances contain misstatements that materially affect this year, and whether policies are consistently applied or changes properly accounted for and disclosed.
- Procedure: read the latest statements and predecessor's report; then (a) brought forward correctly, (b) appropriate policies, (c) one or more of: peruse audited statements, use this year's procedures, perform specific procedures.
- Nature and extent depend on: policies; nature of balances and risks; significance of opening balances; whether audited and whether the predecessor's opinion was modified.
- Receivables and payables: this year's collections and payments. Inventory: current count rolled back, valuation, gross profit and cut-off. Non-current items: underlying records; confirmations for long-term debt and investments.
- Reporting: no evidence: qualified or disclaimer; misstatement not properly dealt with, or policies inconsistent: qualified or adverse.
- Misstatements found: additional procedures, then communicate to management and those charged with governance. (Read the meter on moving day.)

## Lesson 4.9: SA 550: related parties

*Who counts as a related party, why some related party transactions carry a higher risk of material misstatement, and what SA 550 asks the auditor to inquire about, understand and inspect.*

### Key idea

A related party transaction is not wrong in itself. The risk is that it is hidden, or not on normal terms; so the auditor first identifies the relationships, then tests and checks the disclosure of the transactions.

### Exam angle

**[exam] Where SA 550 appeared, May 2024 to September 2026**
In the ICAI documents read for this lesson, SA 550 was asked 8 times in 8 new-scheme documents, plus an old-scheme paper:

- **Records or documents that reveal related parties**, 5 marks: MTP September 2024 Series I Q6(b); MTP May 2025 Series II Q6(a); MTP September 2025 Series II Q3(a) ("list any ten"). As an MCQ: January 2025 MCQ 7, where "inventory records" is the suggestion that is not correct.
- **Inquiries of management and understanding controls**, 5 marks: MTP January 2026 Series II Q1(b) (a restructuring brings new related parties); September 2026 Q5(a) (transactions with no exchange of consideration; suggested answers not yet published).
- **Higher risks, with examples**, 4 marks: MTP September 2026 Series I Q3(c) ("do they always involve a higher risk?"); old-scheme November 2023 paper, Q3(d) (3 marks).
- **MCQ on documentation**: MTP September 2025 Series I MCQ 11 (what should not be documented: "transactions at close length basis").
- The Study Material's Test Your Knowledge adds an MCQ: many related party transactions mean a **higher** risk that management overrides controls (SM 4.122, answer (b)).

**[exam] The traps**
- "Related party transactions always carry a higher risk" is wrong: ICAI's answer starts with "Many related party transactions are in the normal course of business…" and only then gives the three examples.
- "All entities under common control by a state are related parties" is wrong (old-scheme November 2019 Q1(c)).
- A list of records is marked item by item in ICAI's wording: "statements of conflicts of interest", "significant contracts re-negotiated", "life insurance policies acquired by the entity".

**[trick] C-I-N for the higher-risk examples**
**C**omplex structures; **I**nformation systems that miss them; **N**ot at normal market terms (even no consideration). Condition: these are the Study Material's examples of when risk *may* be higher; open the answer by saying that transactions in the normal course may carry no higher risk.

### Answer frame

### "Explain the risks; do related party transactions always carry higher risk?"

1. **Normal course first**: many related party transactions are in the normal course of business and may carry no higher risk than similar transactions with unrelated parties.
2. **However**: the nature of related party relationships and transactions may, in some circumstances, give rise to higher risks.
3. **Three examples**, in ICAI's words: complex relationships and structures; information systems ineffective at identifying or summarising; not under normal market terms, some with no exchange of consideration.
4. **Facts applied**: match each example to the case.
5. **Conclusion**: not always; here the risk is higher, so the auditor exercises professional skepticism and responds.

### "What should the auditor inquire and understand?"

| Inquire of management about | Understand controls to |
|---|---|
| (a) Identity of related parties, with changes from the prior period | (a) Identify, account for and disclose related parties and transactions |
| (b) Nature of the relationships | (b) Authorise and approve significant related party transactions |
| (c) Transactions in the period: type and purpose | (c) Authorise and approve significant transactions outside the normal course |

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: "related party means fraud"** **Fix:** many related party transactions are in the normal course of business and carry no higher risk; the risk rises with complexity, weak information systems and non-market terms.
- **"Below materiality, so ignore it"** **Fix:** for related party transactions, users' expectations about disclosure may make smaller amounts material (SM 3.22); and the disclosure itself is what users rely on.
- **Taking management's list as the evidence** **Fix:** inquiry of management is the start, not the end. Understand the controls, then stay alert and inspect the records SA 550 lists; obtain written representations at the end.
- **Mixing the two lists of (a), (b), (c)** **Fix:** inquiries are about *who, how related, what transactions*; controls are about *identify and disclose, approve related party deals, approve deals outside the normal course*.

### Recap

- Related party: as defined in the framework; if the framework is minimal, a party that controls or significantly influences the entity, an entity it controls or influences, or one under common control (ownership, close family owners, key management).
- Entities under common control by a state are not related unless they transact or share resources significantly.
- Control: power to govern financial and operating policies to obtain benefits. Significant influence: power to participate in those decisions, not control; by share ownership, statute or agreement.
- Many related party transactions are normal and carry no higher risk; higher risk can arise from complex structures, ineffective information systems, or non-market terms (even no consideration).
- Inquire: identity (and changes), nature of relationships, transactions (type and purpose).
- Understand controls to: identify, account for and disclose; approve significant related party transactions; approve significant transactions outside the normal course.
- Stay alert while reviewing records: tax returns, regulatory filings, shareholder registers, conflict-of-interest statements, contracts with key management, unusual or re-negotiated contracts, internal audit reports.
- Significant related party transactions outside the normal course are always significant risks; obtain written representations. (Check the nephew's runs twice.)

## Lesson 4.10: SA 520: analytical procedures

*How auditors test numbers against what they should be: the meaning and objectives of SA 520, its use at planning, testing and completion, the four requirements for substantive analytical procedures, and how to investigate a difference.*

### Key idea

An analytical procedure compares a recorded amount with what it should be, given a plausible relationship. Its power depends on how good the expectation is; and a significant difference must be investigated (inquiry, then corroborating evidence), never simply accepted or simply booked as an error.

### Exam angle

**[exam] Where SA 520 appeared**
In the ICAI documents read for this lesson, analytical procedures were examined in 22 questions across 17 documents (9 new-scheme, 8 old-scheme past papers).

- **New scheme, descriptive 3 to 5 marks.** Rental income predicted from apartments, rent and vacancy: why it is a substantive analytical procedure, when such procedures are more applicable, and how they give persuasive evidence: MTP January 2026 Series I Q4(a) (5 marks). Reliability of data (aggressive budgets, general industry data, untested IT reports): MTP May 2026 Series II Q1(c) (4 marks). Purpose of analytical procedures with expense ratios: MTP September 2026 Series I Q1(c) (4 marks). Analytical procedures on the overall reasonableness of purchase quantity and price: May 2024 Q4(d) (3 marks), and November 2019 (old scheme) Q3(d); the May 2024 suggested answer is Chapter 5's list for purchases (consumption analysis, stock composition analysis, creditors and stock turnover ratios, quantitative reconciliation of stock; SM 5.103–5.104).
- **New scheme, MCQs.** Matching four procedures to trend, reasonableness, ratio and structural modelling: May 2026 MCQ 9. Hostel income from occupancy and fee as a reasonableness test, then investigating a variance: MTP May 2026 Series I MCQs 5–6. Receivables up 48% against credit sales up 12%: inquire, evaluate, perform further procedures: MTP September 2026 Series II MCQ 4. **[exam] How the marks are earned**
Write the definition with "plausible relationships among both financial and non-financial data". In case questions, name the technique, state the investigation requirement in its two limbs (inquiry with corroborating evidence; other procedures) and conclude. Factor and reliability questions want the Study Material's list in order, each with its one-line example.

**[trick] Trend looks back, reasonableness looks sideways**
**Trend analysis** compares with **prior periods**. A **reasonableness test** uses **non-financial data of the same period** (rooms, rates, units) and ignores prior periods. **Ratio analysis** relates **one account to another**. **Structural modelling** fits a **statistical model** to past data. Holds for the Study Material's four techniques.

### Answer frame

### "Identifies inconsistencies: what should the auditor do?" (3 or 4 marks)

1. **Provision**: "As per SA 520, if analytical procedures identify fluctuations or relationships that are inconsistent with other relevant information or that differ from expected values by a significant amount, the auditor shall investigate such differences by:"
2. **(i) Inquiry and evidence**: inquiring of management and obtaining appropriate audit evidence relevant to management's responses, evaluated against the auditor's understanding of the entity and other evidence.
3. **(ii) Other procedures**: performing other audit procedures as necessary, for example when management cannot explain or the explanation is not adequate.
4. **Application**: "In the given case, CA … should …".

### "Distinguish trend analysis and reasonableness tests"

| Point | Trend analysis | Reasonableness test |
|---|---|---|
| Basis | Prior period balances or a trend of several periods | Non-financial data for the audit period |
| Relies on past periods | Yes | No |
| Best for | Accounts that move in a pattern over time | Income statement accounts, certain accruals and prepayments |
| Example | Salary over five years | Interest expense against interest-bearing obligations |

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: booking the difference as the error** **Fix:** a difference is a signal, not a misstatement. Investigate first: inquiry with corroborating evidence, then other procedures.
- **Accepting "milk cost more in the monsoon"** **Fix:** management's response needs audit evidence; then re-test the expectation with it. The ₹61 rate still left ₹72 lakh unexplained.
- **"Analytical procedures are for planning only"** **Fix:** required at planning and at completion, and often used in testing (SM 4.104, 4.129).
- **"Substantive analytical procedures suit small volumes"** **Fix:** they are generally more applicable to large volumes of transactions that tend to be predictable over time; and for significant risks, evidence solely from them is unlikely to be sufficient (SM 4.107, 4.110).

### Recap

- Analytical procedures: evaluations of financial information through analysis of plausible relationships among both financial and non-financial data, including investigating significant or inconsistent fluctuations.
- Objectives: relevant and reliable evidence from substantive analytical procedures; an overall conclusion near the end on consistency with the auditor's understanding.
- Used at planning (required), testing (often), completion (required).
- Factors: availability of data, disaggregation, account type, source, predictability, nature of assertion, inherent risk.
- Techniques: trend analysis, ratio analysis, reasonableness tests, structural modelling.
- Four requirements: suitability; reliability of data; a sufficiently precise expectation; the acceptable difference (smaller as risk rises).
- Investigate: inquiry with evidence for the responses; other procedures as necessary. A new risk at completion means revising the assessment (SA 315). (Where did the milk go? Ask, then check.)

## Lesson 4.11: Audit trail

*What an audit trail (edit log) is, why entities and auditors value it, what it costs, and what the company auditor reports on accounting software with an audit trail feature under Rule 11.*

### Key idea

An audit trail is a documented flow of a transaction from the source document to the financial statements, with a record of system and user activity. It reduces fraud, material errors and unauthorised use, helps fix responsibility and rebuild events, and lets the auditor verify whether controls operated effectively and transactions were performed by authorised persons, which increases the reliability of audit evidence.

### Exam angle

**[exam] Where the audit trail appeared**
Audit trail was asked directly in 5 of the ICAI documents read for this lesson; several more use the phrase "controls which leave no audit trail" in tests-of-controls answers (for example, September 2024 Q2(a)).

- **Reporting, 3 to 5 marks.** The Rule 11 reporting requirements for software with an audit trail (edit log) facility: November 2023 (old scheme) Q2(d), 3 marks; the suggested answer lists the facility and its three limbs (operated throughout the year for all transactions, not tampered with, preserved). An auditor wanting software that tracks user activity and changes to entries: what is he looking for, and what does he report? MTP November 2023 Series II (old scheme) Q3(d), 3 marks. The Rule 11 matters other than the intermediaries representations: September 2026 Q3(a), 5 marks (answers not yet published); RTP November 2023 (old scheme) carried the full Rule 11 list as a Study Material update.
- **Multiple choice, 2 marks.** Are audit trails useful only for auditors, and must an LLP keep its accounts in software with an audit trail? MTP January 2025 Series I MCQ 7: ICAI's key is that audit trails are useful for businesses as well as auditors, and that keeping accounts in such software is not compulsory for LLPs.
- **In the Study Material**, Test Your Knowledge asks for the advantages of the feature for auditors (SM 4.125).

**[exam] How the marks are earned**
For "advantages for the auditor", write the three SM points (controls operating effectively; transaction performed by an authorised person; reliability of evidence) after the definition. For the reporting question, quote the Rule 11 line and split it into its limbs. In a case, name the feature ("audit trail (edit log)") before reporting on it.

### Answer frame

### "Discuss the advantages of an audit trail feature for the auditor"

1. **Definition**: a documented flow of a transaction, from the source document to the account entry to the financial statements; audit evidence of authentication and integrity.
2. **For the entity**: reduces fraud, material errors and unauthorised use; enhances internal control and data security; fixes responsibility; rebuilds events.
3. **For the auditor**: inspires confidence; verifies whether controls operated effectively; whether a transaction was performed by an authorised person; increases the reliability of evidence.
4. **Cost, and the answer to it**: system expenditure and analysis time; automated tools.

### "What are the reporting requirements?"

Software with an audit trail (edit log) facility, and: (1) operated throughout the year for all transactions; (2) feature not tampered with; (3) trail preserved as per statutory record retention requirements. A negative or qualified answer states the reasons (section 143(4)).

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: "the audit trail is the auditor's working papers"** **Fix:** the audit trail is a record kept in the entity's system of how transactions flowed and who changed what; audit documentation is the auditor's own record of procedures, evidence and conclusions.
- **Reporting only "the software has an audit trail"** **Fix:** the Rule 11 line has more limbs: operated throughout the year for all transactions, not tampered with, and preserved as per record retention requirements. A feature that exists but was switched off for a week fails the "operated throughout the year for all transactions" limb.
- **"Audit trails are only for auditors"** **Fix:** their purpose is the entity's: reducing fraud, material errors and unauthorised use, enhancing internal control and data security, fixing responsibility and rebuilding events. The auditor benefits as a user of that evidence.

### Recap

- Audit trail: a documented flow of a transaction, from source document to account entry to financial statements; audit evidence of the authentication and integrity of a transaction.
- It is also a record of system and user activity (in banks, log-on attempts and the device used).
- Purpose: to reduce fraud, material errors and unauthorised use; it enhances internal control and data security, fixes responsibility, rebuilds events and shows whether the system operates as intended.
- Cost: system expenditure and analysis time; automated tools analyse the volume.
- For the auditor: confidence; checks whether controls operated effectively and whether transactions were performed by authorised persons; more reliable evidence.
- Rule 11 reporting: software with an audit trail (edit log) facility, operated throughout the year for all transactions, not tampered with, preserved as per retention requirements; reasons for any negative answer (section 143(4)).
- Controls that leave no audit trail are tested by inquiry and observation. (A camera "off for repairs" is itself a finding.)
