## Lesson 10.1: Process accounts, normal loss, abnormal loss and abnormal gain

*How costs are charged to process accounts and averaged over units, why normal loss raises the cost of good units, and how abnormal loss and gain are valued and closed.*

### Key idea

Cost is averaged over the **normal output** (input minus normal loss). Good units, abnormal loss and abnormal gain are all valued at that one normal cost per unit. Only the normal loss is absorbed by the good units, and only its scrap value comes back to the process.

**[formula] Normal cost per unit**
$$\text{Normal output} = \text{Input units} \qquad - \text{Normal loss units}$$

$$\text{Cost per unit} \qquad = \frac{\text{Total cost} - \text{Scrap value}}{\text{Normal output}}$$
Scrap value is that of the normal loss. ICAI's words: (Total cost − Realisable value of normal loss) ÷ (Total input units − Normal loss units). The rate values units transferred, abnormal loss and abnormal gain (SM 10.7–10.10).

**[formula] Value of normal loss**
$$\text{Value of normal loss} \qquad = \text{Normal loss units} \qquad \times (\text{Realisable value} - \text{Cost to sell})$$
ICAI: "Scrap realisable value less cost to sale" (SM 10.6).

**[formula] Abnormal units**
$$\text{Abnormal loss (gain)} \qquad = \text{Normal output} - \text{Actual output}$$
Positive: abnormal loss; negative: abnormal gain.

**[formula] What reaches the Costing P&L**
$$\text{Net amount} = \text{Abnormal units} \qquad \times (\text{Cost per unit} - \text{Scrap rate})$$
A debit to the Costing P&L for an abnormal loss, a credit for an abnormal gain (ICAI's income statement, SM 10.15).

**[definition] Process loss**
The loss of material arising during the course of a processing operation, equal to the difference between the input quantity of the material and its output. (SM 10.5)

**[definition] Normal process loss**
The loss of material which is inherent in the nature of work. It can be reasonably anticipated from the nature of the material, nature of operation, experience and technical data; it is unavoidable because of the nature of the material or the process. It also includes units withdrawn from the process for test or sampling. (SM 10.6)

**[definition] Abnormal process loss**
The loss in excess of the pre-determined loss (normal process loss). It may occur due to carelessness of workers, a bad plant design or operation, sabotage etc.; it cannot be estimated in advance, but it can be kept under control by taking suitable measures. (SM 10.8)

**[definition] Abnormal gain**
An unexpected gain in production under normal conditions: the difference between actual and expected loss, or actual and expected production, when the loss is less than the normal figure. It arises from over-estimation of process loss, improved efficiency of workers, better technology etc. (SM 10.9)

**[trick] Three checks before you move on**
Units balance; rupees balance; good units, abnormal loss and abnormal gain carry the same cost per unit. When one fails, the normal loss is usually on the wrong base or at actual instead of normal units. Holds for any process account, with or without scrap value.

### Answer frame

### Theory: "How will you treat normal loss, abnormal loss and abnormal gain in process costing?"

ICAI's suggested answer (May 2023 Q6(e), old scheme) is the Study Material's three treatment paragraphs, in this order:

1. **Normal loss.** The cost of normal process loss is absorbed by good units produced under the process. The amount realised by the sale of normal process loss units is credited to the process account.
2. **Abnormal loss.** ==The cost of an abnormal process loss unit is equal to the cost of a good unit.== The total cost of abnormal process loss is credited to the process account from which it arises; it is not treated as a part of the cost of the product, and is debited to the Costing Profit and Loss Account.
3. **Abnormal gain.** The process account under which abnormal gain arises is debited with the abnormal gain and credited to the abnormal gain account, which is closed by transfer to the Costing Profit and Loss Account. The cost of abnormal gain is computed on the basis of normal production.

Open each heading with one line on the loss's meaning (normal: inherent, anticipated; abnormal: beyond the pre-determined loss, controllable). For "What is process costing? State its features.": ICAI's definition, the six features in ICAI's order, three industries.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the abnormal loss that went to the Costing P&L at the wrong rate** **Fix:** abnormal loss costs what a good unit costs, (total cost − scrap value of normal loss) ÷ normal output = ₹65. The scrap rate enters later, in the abnormal loss account.
- **The abnormal gain that forgot the dust it replaced** **Fix:** the abnormal gain account is debited with the scrap value of the gain units (100 × ₹20 = ₹2,000, to the normal loss account); only ₹4,500 reaches the Costing P&L.
- **Normal loss credited at the actual loss** **Fix:** the process account always credits the **normal** loss, 500 kg × ₹20 = ₹10,000; the abnormal gain account handles the 100 kg difference.

### Recap

- Process costing: costs charged to processes and averaged over units produced.
- Normal loss = input × normal %; its scrap value (realisable value less cost to sell) is credited to the process.
- Cost per unit = (total cost − scrap value of normal loss) ÷ (input − normal loss units).
- Abnormal loss (gain) = normal output − actual output (or the reverse), valued at the same cost per unit.
- Abnormal loss: credited to the process; scrap credited in the abnormal loss account; balance debited to the Costing P&L.
- Abnormal gain: debited to the process; the abnormal gain account gives up the scrap value of the gain units; balance credited to the Costing P&L.
- The process is credited with the normal loss on the expected figure, even in a month of abnormal gain.
- Units and rupees must both balance. (Steam is a cost of chai; a boil-over is not.)

## Lesson 10.2: Equivalent units: FIFO and weighted average

*How partly finished units become equivalent finished units for each cost element, and how the FIFO and weighted average methods value output, losses and work-in-process.*

### Key idea

Equivalent units = units × percentage of work done, worked out separately for each cost element. FIFO counts only this period's work (opening work-in-process gets only its remaining percentage) and divides only this period's cost; weighted average adds the opening work-in-process cost to this period's and counts every completed unit as 100%.

### Formats and formulas

### Statement of equivalent production

ICAI's statement has ten columns: input details and units, output particulars and units, then the percentage and units for materials, labour and overheads. Below, the input side comes first and the percentages are left out to fit a phone; on paper, write a % column before each element's units. *Reproduce it from memory.*

| Units introduced (or transferred in) | xxx |

|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Opening WIP (1) | xxx | xxx | xxx | xxx |
| Finished output (2) | xxx | xxx | xxx | xxx |
| Normal loss (3) | xxx | – | – | – |
| Abnormal loss (4) | xxx | xxx | xxx | xxx |

### Statement of evaluation (also called apportionment or distribution of cost)

| FIFO | Weighted average |
|---|---|
| (i) Opening WIP: cost b/f + equivalent units to complete × cost per EU | (i) Units completed and transferred × cost per EU |
| (ii) Units started and completed × cost per EU | (ii) Abnormal loss (or gain), element-wise |
| (iii) Abnormal loss (or gain), element-wise | (iii) Closing WIP, element-wise |
| (iv) Closing WIP, element-wise | |

The process account then shows units and amounts as in [[cma.proc.accounts|process accounts]], with opening WIP on the debit side and closing WIP on the credit side.

**[formula] Equivalent units, FIFO**
$$\text{EU} = \text{Opening WIP} \qquad \times (100\% - \%\ \text{done before}) \qquad + \text{Started and completed} \qquad + \text{Abnormal loss} \times \% \qquad - \text{Abnormal gain} \qquad + \text{Closing WIP} \times \%$$
One line per cost element.

**[formula] Equivalent units, weighted average**
$$\text{EU} = \text{Units completed} \qquad + \text{Abnormal loss} \times \% \qquad - \text{Abnormal gain} \qquad + \text{Closing WIP} \times \%$$

**[formula] Cost per equivalent unit**
$$\text{Cost to divide, FIFO} \qquad = \text{Period's cost} - \text{Scrap of normal loss}$$

$$\text{Cost to divide, weighted average} \qquad = \text{Opening WIP cost} \qquad + \text{Period's cost} - \text{Scrap of normal loss}$$

$$\text{Cost per EU} = \frac{\text{Cost to divide}}{\text{Equivalent units}}$$

Scrap of normal loss is its realisable value. Work each cost element separately.

**[formula] The bridge between the two**
$$\text{EU (weighted average)} - \text{EU (FIFO)} \qquad = \text{Opening WIP} \qquad \times \%\ \text{done last period}$$
Holds for each cost element; a quick check on both statements.

**[definition] Equivalent units**
Equivalent units or equivalent production units means converting the incomplete production units into their equivalent completed units. (SM 10.16)

**[definition] FIFO method**
Units completed and transferred are taken from both opening WIP and freshly introduced inputs. The cost to complete the opening WIP and the other completed units are calculated separately; the cost of opening WIP is added to the cost of completing it. Closing WIP is valued at current cost. (SM 10.19)

**[definition] Weighted average method**
The cost of opening work-in-process and the cost of the current period are aggregated, and the aggregate cost is divided by output in terms of completed units. Equivalent production consists of the work-load already contained in opening WIP and the work-load of the current period. (SM 10.21)

### Answer frame

### "Explain briefly the procedure for the valuation of work-in-process"

The chapter's own theory question (SM 10.31); ICAI's answer is paragraph 4 (SM 10.15–10.16):

1. The average cost per unit is total cost ÷ units produced, but in continuous manufacture the period's cost covers opening WIP, closing WIP and completed units.
2. So the cost of WIP at the beginning and at the end must be ascertained to find the cost of each completed unit.
3. WIP holds units at every stage of completion, which makes valuation difficult.
4. It can be valued on an actual basis (materials used and actual labour and expenses), but the accuracy is not satisfactory.
5. The alternative converts partly finished units into equivalent finished units; then name the two methods, FIFO and weighted average.

### "Explain equivalent units"

Definition (converting incomplete units into equivalent completed units); the estimate of percentage completion for each element, which "should be as accurate as possible"; the formula; ICAI's example of 200 units at 25% = 50 completed units (SM 10.16).

### "Distinguish between FIFO and weighted average"

**[compare] | Point | FIFO | Weighted average |**
|---|---|---|
| Opening WIP | Work and cost kept separate; only the remaining work is counted | Units and cost taken in full |
| Cost divided | Current period only | Opening WIP cost + current period |
| Completed units | Opening WIP and fresh units costed separately | All treated as equally complete, one rate |
| Closing WIP | At current cost | At the average cost |

The Study Material's one-line version: "units of opening work in process and their cost are taken in full under average method while under FIFO method only the remaining work done now is considered" (SM 10.22).

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the opening WIP counted at the work already done** **Fix:** the 40% was done in September; FIFO counts October's remaining 60%, 3,600 equivalent kg.
- **Every litre transferred at this month's rate** **Fix:** under FIFO the opening WIP brings its own b/f cost: ₹1,69,300 + ₹60,000 to complete + 8,300 × ₹250 = ₹23,04,300.
- **Equivalent units for the normal loss** **Fix:** normal loss has no equivalent units; only its scrap value is deducted from the cost.
- **The abnormal gain that was added** **Fix:** abnormal gain is deducted, at 100% for every element; the units column still balances because the normal loss row is in full.

### Recap

- Equivalent units = units × % of work done, separately for each cost element.
- Five steps: physical flow, EU per element, cost per element, cost per EU, assign costs.
- FIFO: opening WIP gets only its remaining %; divide only the period's cost; opening WIP's b/f cost is added to its completion cost; closing WIP at current cost.
- Weighted average: all completed units at 100%; divide opening WIP cost + period cost.
- EU (weighted average) − EU (FIFO) = opening WIP × % done last period.
- Normal loss: no EU, scrap value deducted. Abnormal loss: at its stage of completion. Abnormal gain: 100%, deducted.
- Order of statements: equivalent production, cost per EU, evaluation, process account. (Half-pickled achaar is 3,000 equivalent kilograms of curing.)

## Lesson 10.3: Inter-process profit and operation costing

*Why processes may transfer output at a profit, how the cost and profit columns keep unrealised profit out of stock, and what operation costing (hybrid costing) is.*

### Key idea

A transfer at a profit shows each process's profit, but profit is earned only on sale outside the business. The inter-process profit that sits in closing stocks is unrealised; the profit column keeps it out of the cost of stock.

### Formats and formulas

### Process account showing the profit element

Write it as ICAI does, with Total, Cost and Profit on both sides, debit on the left and credit on the right. Four columns of lakh amounts do not fit side by side on a screen, so here the credit side sits below the debit side. *Reproduce it from memory.*

**Finished Stock Account**: debit opening stock, the transfer from the last process and the profit (balancing figure, total and profit columns); credit the Costing P&L A/c with sales and the closing stock, each split into cost and profit.

**[formula] Transfer price**
Profit as a fraction p of the transfer price:

$$\text{TP} = \frac{\text{Cost transferred}}{1 - p}$$

Profit as a fraction q of cost:

$$\text{TP} = \text{Cost transferred} \times (1 + q)$$

Cost transferred = total cost − closing stock.

**[formula] Converting a profit percentage**
$$p \text{ on TP} = \frac{p}{1 - p} \text{ on cost} \qquad q \text{ on cost} = \frac{q}{1 + q} \text{ on TP}$$
20% on TP = 25% on cost; 1/3 on cost = 1/4 on TP.

**[formula] Cost element of closing stock in a process (stock at prime cost)**
$$\text{Cost of closing stock} \qquad = \text{Closing stock} \times \frac{\text{Prime cost (cost)}}{\text{Prime cost (total)}}$$
The cost and total columns of prime cost in the process account (SM 10.25).

**[formula] Cost element of closing finished stock**
$$\text{Cost of closing stock} \qquad = \text{Closing stock} \times \frac{\text{Cost of the transfer}}{\text{Transfer price}}$$
When the question values finished stock at the price received from the last process (SM 10.26).

**[definition] Inter-process profits**
The output of one process is transferred to the next process not at cost but at market value or cost plus a percentage of profit; the difference between cost and the transfer price is known as inter-process profits. (SM 10.23)

**[definition] Operation costing**
A product costing system used when an entity produces more than one variant of the final product using different materials but with similar conversion activities; also known as a hybrid product costing system. (SM 10.26)

- **Theory, 5 marks**: "What is inter-process profit? **[exam] The traps**
- **Which base?** "30% profit on the transfer price" (May 2026) means TP = cost ÷ 70%; "1/3rd of cost" (MTP September 2025) means TP = cost × 4/3, which is 1/4 of the TP.
- **"Total profit"** with no stocks (MTP January 2025): the sum of the process profits and the profit on sale, which equals sales less the actual costs.
- **"Total profit realised"** with stocks (May 2026 MCQ 5): the profit column of the sales line, not the sum of the profits shown.

**[trick] Percentages on cost and on transfer price**
p% on the transfer price is p ÷ (100 − p) on cost; q% on cost is q ÷ (100 + q) on the transfer price. So 20% ↔ 25%, 25% ↔ 33⅓%, 30% ↔ 42.86%. Holds for any percentage, because transfer price = cost + profit.

### Answer frame

### "What is inter-process profit? State its advantages and disadvantages."

ICAI's suggested answer (MTP May 2022 Q6(e), old scheme) is the Study Material's paragraph 7, nearly word for word:

1. **Context**: to control cost and to measure performance, processes are designated as separate profit centres.
2. **Definition**: the output of one process is transferred to the next not at cost but at market value or cost plus a percentage of profit; ==the difference between cost and the transfer price is known as inter-process profits==.
3. **Advantages**: comparison between the cost of output and its market price at the stage of completion is facilitated; each process is made to stand by itself as to the profitability.
4. **Disadvantages**: the use of inter-process profits involves complication; the system shows profits which are not realised because of stock not sold out.

### "Operation costing is defined as refinement of process costing. Explain it."

The chapter's own theory question (SM 10.31); ICAI's answers point to paragraph 8, and RTP November 2020's suggested answer reproduces it with two industries:

1. Used when an entity produces more than one variant of the final product using different materials but similar conversion activities.
2. Also known as a hybrid product costing system: material costs accumulated by job order or batch; conversion costs (labour and overheads) accumulated by department and assigned by process costing methods.
3. Conversion costs applied at a predetermined rate based on budgeted conversion costs.
4. Example: Deluxe and Regular grades of one product, with different raw materials but the same conversion activities.
5. Industries: ready-made garments, shoe making, jewellery. The refinement: it determines the cost of each operation rather than of the whole process (SM 10.27).

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: 25% on the transfer price added to cost** **Fix:** profit on transfer price means TP = cost ÷ (1 − 25%) = ₹4,80,000; "cost plus 25%" is the one that multiplies by 125%.
- **Closing stock of Process II carried entirely at cost** **Fix:** Process II's prime cost holds ₹1,65,000 of inter-process profit out of ₹9,60,000, so the closing stock holds the same share: ₹16,500 profit, ₹79,500 cost.
- **Overheads in the ratio** **Fix:** use the ratio of the column the stock is valued at: prime cost, ₹7,95,000 ÷ ₹9,60,000.
- **Adding up the process profits and calling it the business's profit** **Fix:** profit realised is ₹6,14,850: add the inter-process profit released from opening stocks and take out what is locked in closing stocks.

### Recap

- Inter-process profit = transfer price − cost of the output transferred (market value or cost plus a percentage).
- Advantages: cost compared with market price at each stage; each process stands by itself as to profitability. Disadvantages: complication; unrealised profit shown when stock is not sold.
- On transfer price: TP = cost ÷ (1 − p). On cost: TP = cost × (1 + q).
- Three columns (Total, Cost, Profit) on both sides; stock at prime cost split in the ratio of the prime cost columns; finished stock split in the ratio of the last transfer.
- Realised profit = profits shown + profit in opening stocks − profit in closing stocks = sales − actual cost of goods sold. (Selling to your brother is not a profit until he sells to a stranger.)
- Operation costing: variants with different materials and similar conversion; materials by batch, conversion by department at a predetermined rate; hybrid; garments, shoes, jewellery.
