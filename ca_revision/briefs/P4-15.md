## Lesson 15.1: Budgets, budgetary control, the budget manual and key factor

*What a budget is, how budgetary control compares actual with it, who runs the system, what the budget manual holds, and why the key factor is identified before the budgets are drawn up.*

### Key idea

A budget is a plan in numbers for a definite future period, approved in advance; budgetary control is the continuous comparison of actual results with it, with responsibility fixed for the differences. The key factor sets the ceiling for the whole budget, so it is identified before the budgets are built.

### Formats and formulas

*Reproduce these definitions from memory; ICAI asks for them in 1-mark slices of a 5-mark question.*

**[definition] Budget centre**
A section of an organisation developed for the purpose of budgetary control, and intended to facilitate formulation of various budgets with the help of the head of the department. (SM 15.70)

**[definition] Budget period**
The period covered by a budget. There is no general rule governing its selection; the Budget Committee determines the length suitable for the business, normally a calendar year or a period co-terminus with the financial year, divided into months, quarters or periods coinciding with the trading activity of the business. (SM 15.18)

**[definition] Key factor**
A factor which sets a limit to the total activity: usually one or two (sometimes more than two), such as non-availability of power or lack of demand. It must be identified and its influence on production or sales estimated properly while preparing the budget. (SM 15.16)

**[definition] Master budget**
CIMA: "the summary budget, incorporating its component functional budgets, which is finally approved, adopted and employed." (SM 15.55)

**[formula] Budgeted activity under a key factor**
$$\text{Limit} = \frac{\text{Quantity available}}{\text{Quantity per unit}}$$
$$\text{Budgeted output} = \text{the lowest of the limits}$$
Work out the limit of demand and of each resource (in units of output); the factor giving the lowest limit is the key factor. Conditions: one product, or a mix that stays fixed. With several products competing for one scarce resource, rank them by contribution per unit of that resource instead ([[cma.mc.keyfactor|limiting factor and product mix]]).

### Answer frame

### "Discuss the objectives of a budgetary control system"

The nine points ICAI's answer gives, in order (SM 15.7–15.8); a 4-mark question takes any four with their explanation:

1. Portraying with precision the overall aims of the business and determining targets of performance for each section or department.
2. Laying down the responsibilities of each executive, so that everyone knows what is expected and how he will be judged.
3. Providing a basis for comparing actual performance with the predetermined targets and investigating deviations, which helps in adopting corrective measures.
4. Ensuring optimum use of available resources to maximise profit or production, subject to the limiting factors.
5. Co-ordinating the activities of the business, centralising control and yet enabling management to decentralise responsibility and delegate authority.
6. Engendering a spirit of careful forethought, assessment of what is possible and an attempt at it: dynamism without being reckless.
7. Providing a basis for revision of current and future policies.
8. Drawing up long range plans with a fair measure of accuracy.
9. Providing a yardstick against which actual results can be compared.

### "Advantages" and "limitations"

| Advantages (SM 15.12) | One-line explanation |
|---|---|
| 1. Efficiency | Business activities are conducted in an efficient manner |
| 2. Control on expenditure | A yardstick for measuring the performance of individuals and departments |
| 3. Finding deviations | Actual is compared with budget and deviations are communicated to management |
| 4. Effective utilisation of resources | Men, material, machinery and money are planned for |
| 5. Revision of plans | Helps review current trends and frame future policies |
| 6. Implementation of standard costing | Creates suitable conditions for a standard costing system |
| 7. Cost consciousness | Encourages cost consciousness and maximum utilisation of resources |
| 8. Credit rating | Well-ordered budget plans receive greater favour from credit agencies |

| Limitations (SM 15.13–15.14) | One-line explanation |
|---|---|
| 1. Based on estimates | Made on conditions at the time; revision is needed if they change |
| 2. Time factor | Not executed automatically; management must not expect too much in the initial period |
| 3. Co-operation required | Staff co-operation is usually not available initially; success depends on willing co-operation and teamwork |
| 4. Expensive | Needs a proper organisation structure and consumes qualified manpower and equipment |
| 5. Not a substitute for management | Only a managerial tool, to be intelligently applied |
| 6. Rigid document | Should be flexible enough to take in changes in internal and external factors |

### "Distinguish between a forecast and a budget"

| Point | Forecast | Budget |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | An assessment of probable future events | A financial or quantitative plan to be pursued over a period |
| Commitment | Denotes some degree of flexibility | A commitment, a definite target management seeks to attain |
| Order | Made first, on sales, production cost and finance | Prepared on the basis of the forecasts |
| Sales | Projects the available customer demand: reflects the environment | Shows how management intends to react to it (SM 15.31) |
| Common ground | Both relate to a defined period of time | Both relate to a defined period of time |

### Three answers to "What is a key factor?"

**[cols] **Loses marks****

"The most important factor in the budget."

Vague: no limit, no example, no consequence.
---
**Passes**

"A factor which sets a limit to the total activity, such as lack of demand or shortage of power."

The definition with an example, but not what the budget does with it.
---
**Full marks**

The definition; "usually one or two, sometimes more"; examples (power, demand); it must be identified and its influence on production or sales estimated while preparing the budget; where sales are the limit they are the principal budget factor.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the 9,60,000-pack sales budget** **Fix:** find the limit set by each factor first; the lowest is the key factor and caps every budget.
- **Calling the forecast a budget** **Fix:** a forecast is an assessment of probable events and stays flexible; a budget is the target management commits to and acts to achieve, built on the forecast.
- **The budget manual that is only a list of budgets** **Fix:** a budget manual sets out responsibilities, routines, forms and records for budgetary control (CIMA): approvals, timetable, organisation chart, account codes, revision procedure, key assumptions. The budgets themselves are not the manual.
- **Mixing up the three lists of steps** **Fix:** read the question's verb and noun, then write the matching list; for "budgetary control technique", the five steps plus key factor, manual and period.

### Recap

- Budget (CIMA): a financial and/or quantitative statement, prepared and approved before a defined period, of the policy to be pursued to attain a given objective.
- Forecast = assessment of probable events (flexible); budget = commitment, a definite target.
- Budgetary control: establish budgets, compare actual with budget continuously, revise when circumstances change, fix responsibility.
- Budget committee (functional heads) acts through the budget officer; the Chief Executive is ultimately responsible; the board approves the master budget.
- Preparation: objectives, key factor, controller, budget manual, budget period, standard of activity.
- Key factor: limits the total activity (power, demand); identify it first; the lowest limit binds and can move once removed.
- Budget manual: responsibilities, routine, forms and records for budgetary control: 14 typical contents.
- Components: physical, cost, profit, financial budgets; period-wise: long-term (3 to 10 years), short-term (one or two years), current (months, weeks). (The hall seats 400.)

## Lesson 15.2: Functional budgets and the master budget

*How the sales budget drives the production, material, labour and overhead budgets, how each is laid out in ICAI's format, and how they roll up into the master budget.*

### Key idea

Every functional budget is built from the one before it: sales, then production (sales + closing stock − opening stock), then materials (usage + closing stock − opening stock = purchases), labour and overheads. The master budget is their summary, approved and adopted as the standard to work to.

### Formats and formulas

*Reproduce every format from memory: ICAI's suggested answers lay the statements out exactly like this, with a working note for every figure brought in.*

**[formula] Production budget (units)**
$$P = S + C - O$$
P = units to be produced; S = budgeted sales; C = desired closing stock; O = opening stock, all in units of finished goods. If good output is less than output (post-production rejects), divide the good units needed by (1 − rejection rate).

**[formula] Material usage and purchase budgets**
$$U = P \times q \qquad B = U + C - O$$
U = usage; P = units to be produced; q = material per unit; B = quantity to be bought; C and O = closing and opening stocks of the material. Purchase cost = units to be purchased × unit price. A closing stock given as "N days' consumption" is usage × N ÷ days in the period.

**[formula] Hours to be paid in a labour budget**
$$\text{Productive} = \frac{\text{Standard hours}}{\text{Efficiency ratio}}$$
$$\text{Paid} = \text{Productive} + \text{Down-time}$$
All in hours. Standard hours = units to be produced × standard hours per unit. Hours paid beyond the normal hours (workers × normal hours each) are overtime.

**[exam] The traps ICAI sets**
- "Increase over budgeted sales" (May 2026): apply the percentage to the previous year's **budget**, not its actual sales.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the opening stock that was added** **Fix:** need = use + closing − opening, for finished goods and for materials alike.
- **Material bought for the sales instead of for production** **Fix:** material usage comes from the **production** budget, which already includes the finished-goods stock change; then adjust for the material stocks.

### Recap

- Functional budgets: one for each function (15 in ICAI's list), subsidiary to the master budget; the sales budget comes first.
- Production = budgeted sales + desired closing stock − opening stock (units); based on sales, capacity, stock change, outside purchase policy.
- Stabilised production: plant fully used, carrying cost varies; stabilised stocks: lowest carrying cost, capacity may be idle.
- Material usage = production × standard quantity; purchases = usage + closing stock − opening stock; governed by EOQ and the re-order point with safety stock.
- Labour: standard hours ÷ efficiency ratio = productive hours; + down-time = hours paid; beyond normal hours is overtime at the premium rate.
- Cost of goods sold = materials + labour + factory overhead + opening finished goods − closing finished goods (without the stock adjustments: production cost budget).
- Master budget (CIMA): the summary budget, incorporating its component functional budgets, which is finally approved, adopted and employed: budgeted P&L and balance sheet.
- Need = use + closing − opening, every time. (Barfi first, khoya second.)

## Lesson 15.3: Flexible budgets

*Why a budget fixed at one level of activity misleads, how a flexible budget recasts every cost to the level actually reached, and how to prepare one for several capacity levels.*

### Key idea

A fixed budget is for one level of activity and does not change; a flexible budget recognises fixed, semi-variable and variable costs and changes with the level of activity attained. Judge actual costs only against the flexible budget for the actual level.

### Formats and formulas

*Reproduce the format from memory; ICAI's suggested answers show every level side by side, the fixed portions worked out in notes.*

**[formula] Flexible budget allowance**
$$\text{Allowance} = F + v \times A$$
F = fixed cost (including the fixed part of semi-variable costs); v = variable cost per unit of activity; A = activity attained, in units, hours, or sales value (for costs given as a percentage of sales).

**[formula] High and low points method**
$$v = \frac{\text{Change in expense}}{\text{Change in output}} \qquad F = \text{Total expense} - v \times \text{Output}$$
v = variable cost per unit; F = fixed cost. Use two levels at which the fixed cost is the same; the fixed part comes out equal at both.

**[definition] Fixed budget**
CIMA: "a budget designed to remain unchanged irrespective of the level of activity actually attained." (SM 15.19)

**[exam] The traps ICAI sets**
- A transport, bus or batch cost per vehicle is a step cost: round the number of vehicles **up** (unless the loads divide exactly, as in September 2024, where it became a variable ₹1 a unit).
- A fixed cost that starts "above 80%" or "beyond 90%" is added only at the levels above it.
- The fixed portion of a semi-variable cost is worked out at the base level and held constant; percentage increases are applied to the variable rate and to the fixed total separately.
- "Profit 20% on sales" is 25% on cost.
- Compare actual cost with the flexible budget for the **actual** level; a fixed budget shows "savings" that are only lower volume.

**[trick] Fixed cost survives, variable cost travels**
Whatever the level: fixed cost stays as a total, variable cost moves as a rate × level. So total cost per unit always falls as the level rises (until a step cost or a price change intervenes). Condition: costs linear within the range, with step and price changes added separately.

### Answer frame

### "Distinguish between fixed and flexible budgets"

ICAI's five points, in order (SM 15.22–15.23):

| Point | Fixed budget | Flexible budget |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Change | Does not change with the actual volume achieved: rigid or inflexible | Can be re-cast for the activity level achieved: not rigid |
| 2. Levels | One level of activity, one set of conditions; assumes no change, which is unrealistic | Various budgets for different levels of activity |
| 3. Variance analysis | All costs related to one level, so it does not give useful information | Each cost analysed by behaviour, so it gives useful information |
| 4. Cost and price | Cost ascertainment and price fixation mislead when budgeted and actual levels differ significantly | Facilitates cost ascertainment, price fixation and quotations |
| 5. Comparison | Comparing actual with budget is meaningless when activity levels differ | A meaningful basis of comparison of actual with budget |

### "Explain briefly the concept of flexible budget"

1. The definition (the behaviour of fixed and variable costs; CIMA's wording with semi-variable costs).
2. Unlike a static budget: results for different activity levels; a series of static budgets.
3. Costs classified as fixed, variable and semi-variable, the last split into fixed and variable.
4. Where it is used: new ventures, businesses dependent on nature, labour-intensive industries.
5. A three-line numerical illustration (the café).

### "Which budgeting system would you recommend?" (actuals far above a fixed budget)

ICAI's suggested answer (MTP September 2025 Series II Q6(b)): recommend a **flexible budget**; define it (both definitions); state that unlike static budgets it shows expected results for different activity levels; then list its advantages: sales, costs and profit calculated easily at various levels; simple adjustment to changes in business conditions; helps select the production level that earns the predetermined profit; shows the quantity to be produced to earn a determined profit.

### "Suitability for flexible budget" (SM 15.21–15.22)

1. Seasonal fluctuations in sales and/or production, for example the soft drinks industry.
2. A company which keeps introducing new products or changes the design of its products frequently.
3. Industries engaged in make-to-order business, like ship building.
4. An industry influenced by changes in fashion.
5. General changes in sales.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the league table that punished the busiest café** **Fix:** flex the budget to the 36,000 cups actually sold (₹3,62,000): the café stayed ₹8,000 within it. The same error works the other way: Vijay Nagar's "saving" of ₹18,000 against its fixed budget is only lower volume.
- **The budget rate per unit carried to another level** **Fix:** fixed costs do not shrink: allowance = fixed ₹70,000 + ₹5 × 15,000 = ₹1,45,000.
- **Semi-variable costs treated as wholly variable or wholly fixed** **Fix:** split it first, by the percentage the question gives or by the high and low points method (here ₹8,40,000 fixed and ₹12 a bottle); flex only the variable part. And pick two levels with the same fixed cost: the rusk unit's 2025-26 figure includes a second oven line.
- **8.33 vans** **Fix:** vehicles and batches are hired whole: round up (9 vans), and cost the step.

### Recap

- Fixed (static) budget: one level of activity, unchanged whatever is attained; for stable, non-seasonal businesses and fixed expenses.
- Flexible budget (CIMA): recognises fixed, semi-variable and variable costs and changes with the level of activity attained; a series of static budgets.
- Budgeted cost = fixed cost + variable cost per unit × activity; split semi-variables first (high and low points: change in expense ÷ change in output).
- Add the question's steps: price changes at a level, extra fixed costs beyond a capacity, vehicles or batches rounded up.
- Judge performance against the flexible budget for the actual level: a fixed budget's "saving" may be only lower volume.
- Never carry a cost per unit from one level to another: fixed costs do not shrink.
- Fixed vs flexible: change, levels, variance analysis, cost and price, comparison.
- Suitable where sales are seasonal, products or designs keep changing, work is made to order, fashion rules, or sales change generally. (Fuel for the distance you actually drove.)

## Lesson 15.4: Cash budget

*The month-by-month plan of cash receipts and payments that shows in advance when cash will run short or pile up, laid out the way ICAI's answers lay it out.*

### Key idea

A cash budget is a month-by-month plan of cash receipts and cash payments: only cash, and only in the month it moves. It shows in advance when cash will be short (so borrowing is arranged in time) and when it will be in excess (so it can be invested).

**[formula] Closing balance**
$$\text{Closing balance} = \text{Opening balance} \qquad + \text{Total receipts} - \text{Total payments}$$
Each month's closing balance is the next month's opening balance.

**[formula] Collection from debtors in a month**
$$\text{Collection}_{t} = \sum_{k \ge 0} \text{Credit sales}_{t-k} \times p_{k}$$
$p_k$ = the share of a month's credit sales collected $k$ months after the sale ($k = 0$ is the month of sale). The shares add up to 100% less the bad debts.

**[formula] Collections and payments from opening and closing balances**
$$\text{Receipts from debtors} = \text{Opening debtors} \qquad + \text{Credit sales} - \text{Closing debtors}$$
$$\text{Purchases} = \text{Cost of goods sold} \qquad + \text{Closing stock} - \text{Opening stock}$$
$$\text{Payment to creditors} = \text{Opening creditors} \qquad + \text{Credit purchases} - \text{Closing creditors}$$
Used when a question gives budgeted balances instead of collection patterns (FM Study Material 9.48). Bad debts written off are also deducted from the receipts.

**[formula] Surplus or deficit against a minimum balance**
$$\text{Surplus or (deficit)} = \text{Opening balance} + \text{Receipts} \qquad - \text{Payments} - \text{Minimum cash balance}$$
A deficit is borrowed (or investments liquidated); a surplus is invested or repays the borrowing. The closing balance is then the minimum balance, or above it (FM Study Material 9.46).

**[definition] Cash budget**
A detailed budget of cash receipts and cash payments incorporating both revenue and capital items for the budget period. It represents the cash requirements of the business during the budget period: the plan of receipts and payments of cash, analysed to show the monthly flow of cash, drawn up in such a way that the balance can be forecasted at regular intervals. (SM 15.54)

- Working notes first: collections from debtors, payments to creditors, wages and overheads, each re-timed to the month of payment.
- Closing balance = opening balance + receipts − payments; it becomes next month's opening balance.

- Against a minimum balance: borrow the shortfall so the minimum is restored, repay or invest out of later surpluses.
- Objectives (SM 15.55): probable cash position (borrow or invest in time), coordination with working capital, basis for credit and cash control, effect of seasonal needs and delayed collections revealed.
- Profit is a promise, cash is a fact: read the fuel gauge before the trip.

## Lesson 15.5: Zero based budgeting and performance budgeting

*How zero-based budgeting makes every activity justify its cost from the first rupee through ranked decision packages, and how performance budgeting ties money to functions, programmes, activities and results.*

### Key idea

ZBB: every activity, old or new, must justify its cost from the first rupee; without justification the budget allowance is zero, and justified decision packages are ranked and funded in order of priority. Performance budgeting: the budget is presented in terms of functions, programmes and activities, linking the money (inputs) to the results expected (outputs), with responsibility fixed for those results.

### Recap

- ZBB (SM 15.59): each cost element specifically justified, though the activities are not being undertaken for the first time; without justification the budget allowance is zero.
- Starts from scratch, not from trends; activity-based; cost-benefit justification; prioritised on objectives, funds and regulatory requirement.
- Suits corporate and non-corporate entities (mid-day meal, street lights, drinking water); in companies, discretionary costs (R&D, training, advertisement).
- Stages: identify and describe decision packages, evaluate, rank (hence priority-based budgeting), allocate resources.
- Six advantages: systematic evaluation, critical functions, cost-benefit allocation, waste eliminated, departmental budgets linked to corporate objectives, management by objectives. Two limitations: tedious, may not be implemented properly.
- Performance budgeting: funds presented by functions, programmes and activities with quantitative data on accomplishments; links inputs to outputs; synonymous with responsibility accounting.
- ARC: function, programme, activity, project; PB is still annual but output-oriented. To adopt it: concrete objectives, translated into functions to tasks, physical norms and indicators, decentralised responsibility, a reporting system.

## Lesson 15.6: Control ratios, budget variances, motivation, feedback and feed-forward

*The six budget ratios that split a shortfall into speed, hours and days; how to read a budget variance; and how feedback, feed-forward and well-set targets make budgetary control work.*

### Key idea

Activity ratio = efficiency ratio × capacity ratio: output against budget is speed times time. A budget variance is read against a budget flexed to the actual activity, with its cause and its controllability. Control works best when actuals are compared during the period (feed-forward) against targets the managers accept as achievable.

### Formats and formulas

*Reproduce the six formulas and the report layout from memory.* (SM 15.68)

**[formula] Efficiency ratio**
$$\text{Efficiency ratio} = \frac{\text{Standard hours}}{\text{Actual hours}} \times 100$$
Standard hours = standard hours for the actual output (standard hours earned).

**[formula] Activity ratio**
$$\text{Activity ratio} = \frac{\text{Standard hours}}{\text{Budgeted hours}} \times 100$$

**[formula] Calendar ratio**
$$\text{Calendar ratio} = \frac{\text{Available working days}}{\text{Budgeted working days}} \times 100$$

**[formula] Standard capacity usage ratio**
$$\frac{\text{Budgeted hours}}{\text{Maximum possible hours}} \times 100$$
Maximum possible hours in the budgeted period.

**[formula] Actual capacity usage ratio**
$$\frac{\text{Actual hours worked}}{\text{Maximum possible hours}} \times 100$$
Maximum possible working hours in the period.

**[formula] Actual usage of budgeted capacity ratio (the capacity ratio)**
$$\frac{\text{Actual working hours}}{\text{Budgeted hours}} \times 100$$
ICAI's MTPs call it the capacity ratio or capacity utilisation ratio.

**[formula] How three of them fit together**
$$\text{Activity ratio} = \text{Efficiency ratio} \times \text{Capacity ratio} \qquad \text{Efficiency ratio} = \frac{\text{Activity ratio}}{\text{Capacity ratio}}$$
True whenever the capacity ratio is actual hours ÷ budgeted hours, because the actual hours cancel.

### Which ratio from which data

| You are given | Find first | Then |
|---|---|---|
| Employees (or machines), hours a day, days | Maximum and budgeted hours | Both capacity usage ratios |
| Output and standard time (or rate) a unit | Standard hours | Efficiency and activity ratios |
| A capacity usage ratio | Hours = ratio × maximum hours (budgeted from the standard ratio, actual from the actual) | The other ratios |
| Two of efficiency, capacity and activity | The third: activity = efficiency × capacity | The missing hours from the ratio's definition |

**[definition] Budget ratios**
A ratio is a mathematical relationship between two or more related figures. Budget ratios provide information about the performance level, i.e., the extent of deviation of actual performance from the budgeted performance and whether the actual performance is favourable or unfavourable. (SM 15.67, 15.71)

**[definition] Controllable and uncontrollable variances**
Controllable variances are those which can be controlled under normal operating conditions if a responsibility centre takes preventive measures and acts prudently. Uncontrollable variances are those which occur due to conditions beyond the control of a responsibility centre and cannot be controlled even though all preventive measures are in place. (SM 13.11–13.12)

| Units | xxx | actual units | actual units | |
| Sales | xxx | xxx | xxx | xxx (F/A) |
| Variable costs, item by item | xxx | xxx | xxx | xxx (F/A) |

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

### Recap

- Efficiency = standard hours ÷ actual hours; activity = standard ÷ budgeted; calendar = available days ÷ budgeted days; all × 100.
- Standard capacity usage = budgeted ÷ maximum hours; actual capacity usage = actual ÷ maximum; actual usage of budgeted capacity (the capacity ratio) = actual ÷ budgeted.
- 100% or more is favourable; below 100%, unfavourable. Activity = efficiency × capacity.
