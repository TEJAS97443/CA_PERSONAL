## Lesson 7.1: Capital budgeting and estimating relevant cash flows

*Why capital budgeting decisions matter, the types of investment decision, and how to build a project's post-tax incremental cash flows: initial outflow, interim flows and the terminal year.*

### Key idea

Only post-tax incremental cash flows count: what changes in cash because the project is accepted. Depreciation counts only through the tax it saves; sunk costs, allocated overheads and financing costs stay out.

**[formula] Tax shield on depreciation**
$$\text{Tax shield} = \text{Depreciation} \times \text{Tax rate}$$

**[formula] Profit after tax excluding financing cost**
$$\text{PBIT} \times (1 - t) \qquad = \text{PAT} + \text{Interest} \times (1 - t)$$
PBIT = profit before interest and tax, PAT = profit after tax (struck after interest), t = tax rate (SM 7.15).

**[formula] Terminal year: only asset in the block, or other assets in it**
$$\text{STCL (STCG)} = W_0 - S \qquad \text{Depreciation} = (W_0 - S) \times r$$
W₀ = WDV at the beginning of the year of sale, S = sale value, r = depreciation rate. The first when the block ceases; the second when it continues and S is below W₀ (SM 7.13–7.14).

**[definition] Capital budgeting**
The process of evaluating and selecting long-term investments that are in line with the goal of investor's wealth maximization. (SM 7.69)

**[definition] Sunk cost**
An outlay of cash that has already been incurred in the past and cannot be reversed in present. (SM 7.9)

**[definition] Opportunity cost**
Foregoing of a benefit due to choosing an alternative investment option. (SM 7.9)

With straight-line depreciation down to the estimated salvage, selling at that salvage leaves no gain or loss, so no tax on sale. A tax effect arises only when the sale value differs from the tax WDV.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the feasibility fee Kesar paid twice** **Fix:** the fee is sunk and the overheads only allocated. In the samosa example, including them with the interest turns an NPV of ₹20,84,325 into about −₹3.64 lakh: a good project rejected.
- **The interest that was charged twice** **Fix:** the WACC already contains the cost of debt; take cash flows before interest.
- **The working capital that never came home** **Fix:** working capital comes back in full at the end unless the question says otherwise; no depreciation is provided on it.

### Recap

- Capital budgeting: identify projects, estimate post-tax incremental cash flows, select the proposal that maximises return.
- Critical because of substantial investment, long time period, irreversibility, complex decisions.
- Process: planning, evaluation, selection, implementation, control, review.
- Types: replacement and modernisation (cost reduction), expansion, diversification (revenue expansion); mutually exclusive, accept-reject, contingent.
- In: opportunity cost, working capital (out, then released in full), additional investment, incremental overheads, depreciation's tax shield. Out: sunk cost, allocated overheads, interest and dividends.
- Cash flows: initial outflow, interim CFAT (EAT + depreciation), terminal year (salvage, tax on sale, release of working capital).
- Only asset in the block: no depreciation in the year of sale; STCL or STCG on (opening WDV − sale value). Other assets: depreciation on that difference.
- Cash flows and discount rate both post-tax; financing costs live in the WACC. (Two photographs, one difference.)

## Lesson 7.2: Payback, discounted payback and ARR

*How long a project takes to return its cash (payback, and discounted payback in today's rupees), the payback reciprocal, and the accountant's average rate of return, with their decision rules and limits.*

### Key idea

Payback measures how fast the initial cash outlay is recovered: a guide to liquidity and risk, blind to the time value of money and to every cash flow after payback. Discounted payback cures the first blindness, not the second. ARR covers the whole life but uses accounting profit and ignores the time value of money.

### Formats and formulas

*Reproduce these from memory.*

**[formula] Payback period, uniform cash flows**
$$\text{Payback period} = \frac{I}{C}$$
I = total initial capital investment; C = annual expected after-tax net cash flow (SM 7.19).

**[formula] Payback period, non-uniform cash flows**
$$\text{Payback} = Y + \frac{U}{C_{Y+1}}$$
Y = full years before the outlay is recovered; U = unrecovered outlay (outlay − cumulative inflows of those Y years); C with subscript Y + 1 = cash inflow of the next year.

**[formula] Payback reciprocal**
$$\text{Payback reciprocal} = \frac{\bar{C}}{I} \times 100$$
C̄ = average annual cash inflow; I = initial investment (SM 7.22).

**[formula] Accounting rate of return**
$$\text{ARR} = \frac{\text{Average annual net income}}{\text{Investment}}$$
Expressed as a percentage (× 100). Net income = profit after depreciation (and after tax where tax is given). Investment = initial or average investment, as the question says (SM 7.22).

**[formula] Average investment**
$$\tfrac{1}{2}(I - S) + S + W$$
I = initial investment, S = salvage value, W = additional working capital (SM 7.24). With no working capital this is (I + S) ÷ 2.

**The three versions of ARR** (SM 7.23–7.24)

| Version | Numerator | Denominator |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Annual basis | Profit after depreciation of each year | Investment at the beginning of that year; then average the yearly rates |
| 2. Total investment basis | Average annual profit | Investment in the beginning |
| 3. Average investment basis | Average annual profit | Average investment |

**Discounted payback: the working table** (SM 7.43)

| Year | Cash flow (₹) | PV factor | PV (₹) | Cumulative PV (₹) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | xxx | x.xxx | xxx | xxx |
| 2 | xxx | x.xxx | xxx | xxx |
| … | | | | |

Discounted payback = years before the cumulative PV reaches the outlay + unrecovered amount ÷ that year's PV.

**[definition] Payback period**
Time required to recover the initial cash-outflow is called pay-back period. (SM 7.18)

**[definition] Discounted payback period**
The payback period calculated after discounting the cash flows by a predetermined rate. (SM 7.20, 7.42)

**[exam] Where payback and ARR appeared, May 2024 to September 2026**

- **Theory, 2 marks**: "Discuss the relevance of payback reciprocal in capital budgeting decisions" (May 2024 Q4(c)); "Explain the concept of discounted payback period" (MTP Series II for May 2024 Q4(c)); "Write a short note on cut-off rate" (MTP Series I for May 2024 Q2(b)).

- **MCQs, 1–2 marks**: payback reciprocal (MTP Series II for May 2024 MCQ 4: the Study Material's own numbers); payback from the IRR (MTP Series I for January 2025 MCQ 7); payback, ARR and PI of two machines (RTP May 2025 Q1; MTP Series II for May 2026 MCQs 1–3); payback with a tax holiday (September 2026 MCQ 4).

**[exam] The traps ICAI used**
- ARR "based on average investment" (January 2026 Q2(b)): average investment = (₹24,00,000 + ₹2,40,000) ÷ 2, with ARR on average PAT.
- RTP January 2026 Q9 worked backwards: the initial investment from the MIRR and the terminal value (₹5,00,000), the annual cash flow from the simple payback of 5 years (₹1,00,000), and the WACC from the discounted payback of 7 years (PVAF for 7 years = 5, so 9%); then NPV, PI and ARR (profit after depreciation ÷ initial investment, 7.5%).
- Salvage and working capital released are added to the last year's inflow in the discounted payback table, marked "includes salvage value" (SM 7.89; May 2025 Q3(a)).

**[trick] Discounted payback is never shorter**
With an outlay at the start, positive inflows and a positive discount rate, every present value is smaller than its cash flow, so the discounted payback is always at least the simple payback. A discounted payback shorter than the payback is an arithmetic slip.

### Answer frame

**Relevance of payback reciprocal** (May 2024 Q4(c), ICAI's points): reciprocal of the payback is a close approximation of the IRR if the life is at least twice the payback period and the cash inflows are equal; a helpful tool for quick estimation of the rate of return; formula: average annual cash flow ÷ initial investment, or 1 ÷ payback period.

**Concept of discounted payback period** (MTP Series II for May 2024 Q4(c)): payback is the time to recover the investment from project cash flows, focused on liquidity, ignoring time value and profitability; discounted payback uses cash flows discounted at the cost of capital; the period in which discounted cash flows equal the initial outflow; shorter is better; it still ignores cash flows after that period.

**Short note on cut-off rate** (MTP Series I for May 2024 Q2(b), ICAI's points): the minimum rate of return the management wishes to have from any project; usually based on the cost of capital; the management gains only if a project returns more than the cut-off rate; so it can be used as the discount rate or opportunity cost rate. Add the Study Material's example: a proposal yielding less than the cut-off rate is automatically rejected (SM 7.26).

**Advantages and limitations** of payback or ARR: ICAI's lists above, in ICAI's order, a line each.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the payback that rounded itself up** **Fix:** add the fraction: unrecovered ₹6,40,000 ÷ year 4's ₹20,70,000 = 0.31, so 3.31 years. Divide by that year's cash flow, not by the cumulative total.
- **ARR on cash instead of profit** **Fix:** ARR uses average annual net income after depreciation (₹8,40,000), giving 22.70%.
- **The average investment that lost its salvage** **Fix:** ½ (initial − salvage) + salvage + working capital = ₹37,00,000; salvage and working capital stay blocked to the end.
- **The discount that shortened payback** **Fix:** only future inflows are discounted; the year-0 outlay stays at face value, and the discounted payback is never shorter.

### Recap

- Payback = initial investment ÷ annual cash flow after tax (uniform); otherwise cumulate and add the fraction: unrecovered ÷ that year's cash flow.
- Payback: easy, a guide to risk and liquidity; ignores time value, cash flows after payback, long-term projects.
- Payback reciprocal = average annual cash inflow ÷ initial investment; approximates IRR only if life ≥ 2 × payback and inflows are equal.
- ARR = average annual net income (after depreciation) ÷ initial or average investment; the same base for every project.
- Average investment = ½ (initial − salvage) + salvage + additional working capital.
- Discounted payback: payback on discounted cash flows; considers time value; still ignores later cash flows; never shorter than payback.
- Accept: payback ≤ maximum acceptable; ARR ≥ minimum acceptable (cut-off). Mutually exclusive: least payback, highest ARR.
- Last year of a payback or discounted payback table: add salvage and working capital released to that year's inflow. (The cousin's loan: how soon back, how soon back in today's rupees, what it earned by the book.)

## Lesson 7.3: NPV, profitability index and equivalent annual cost

*Bring every cash flow to today at the cost of capital: NPV and its decision rule, the profitability index for ranking, and equivalent annual figures for projects with unequal lives.*

### Key idea

NPV converts every cash flow into today's rupees at the cost of capital and nets them: accept a project when NPV ≥ 0, because a positive NPV is the addition to shareholders' wealth. PI is the same test as a ratio; equivalent annual figures make projects with different lives comparable.

### Formats and formulas

*Reproduce these from memory.*

**[formula] Net present value**
$$\text{NPV} = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{C_t}{(1+k)^t} - I$$
Cₜ = cash flow of year t, k = discount rate, n = life of the project, I = investment (SM 7.27–7.28). With outflows later in the life: NPV = PV of cash inflows − PV of cash outflows.

**[formula] Profitability index**
$$\text{PI} = \frac{\text{Sum of discounted cash inflows}}{\text{Discounted cash outflow}}$$
The denominator is the initial cash outlay or the total discounted cash outflow, as the case may be (SM 7.31).

**[formula] Equivalent annualised NPV and equivalent annual cost**
$$\text{EAN} = \frac{\text{NPV}}{\text{PVAF}_{k,n}} \qquad \text{EAC} = \frac{\text{PV of costs}}{\text{PVAF}_{k,n}}$$
PVAF = present value annuity factor at the discount rate k for the project's own life n (SM 7.56). Higher EAN or lower EAC wins.

**The NPV statement** (SM 7.29)

| Year | Net cash flow (₹) | PV factor @ k | Discounted cash flow (₹) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | (xxx) | 1.000 | (xxx) |
| 1 | xxx | x.xxx | xxx |
| … | | | |
| **Net present value** | | | **xxx** |

For equal yearly flows, one line: cash flow × PVAF for years 1 to n.

**[definition] Net present value**
The net present value of a project is the amount, in current value of amount, the investment earns after paying cost of capital in each period. (SM 7.27)

### Exam angle

**[exam] Where NPV, PI and equivalent annual figures appeared, May 2024 to September 2026**
In the documents read for this lesson, NPV was the end point of most capital budgeting numericals:

- **Descriptive, 6 to 7 marks**: NPV and PI of a replacement (May 2024 Q3(a), 7 marks) and of a new plant (January 2025 Q3(a), 6 marks); NPV, PI, discounted payback and MIRR from an IRR (May 2025 Q3(a), 6 marks); NPV and PI of two machines with lives of 5 and 7 years, then the choice on the **equivalent annualised criterion** (May 2026 Q2(a), 7 marks: machine M won on EAN although N had the higher NPV).
- **MCQs, 2 marks each**: equivalent annual NPV of two proposals with lives of 5 and 3 years (May 2025 MCQ 7); NPV and equivalent annual cost of two warehouses (MTP Series I for September 2025, five MCQs); equivalent annual cost of buying two scanners or renting one (MTP Series II for September 2025, five MCQs); which project NPV and PI recommend (RTP January 2026 MCQ 2); PI of two machines (RTP May 2025 Q1(v)).
- **Theory**: "Do the profitability index and the NPV criterion lead to the same acceptance-rejection and ranking decisions? In what situations will they give conflicting results?" (RTP January 2025 Q11(c)).

**[exam] The traps ICAI used**
- A subsidy received at the end of year 1 (May 2024 Q3(a)): shown as a reduction of the outlay, PI = 1.224; shown as a year-1 inflow, PI = 1.195. The NPV is ₹4,71,640 either way: state your treatment, since PI depends on it.
- Working capital "in year 1" (January 2025 Q3(a)): taken at year 0, PI 4.62; at the end of year 1, 4.70.
- Two mutually exclusive machines with lives of 5 and 6 years in RTP September 2026 Q7: ICAI's answer recommended Machine I because it ranked first on two of the three methods asked (discounted payback and IRR). On NPV (₹1,18,074 against ₹86,909), the Study Material's rule for mutually exclusive projects, Machine II comes first, and also on equivalent annualised NPV (₹28,714 against ₹24,108, dividing by 4.112 and 3.605, the sums of the RTP's yearly factors at 12%; these figures are our working, not the RTP's). When lives differ and the question asks which to choose, show the equivalent annual figure and state the rule you follow.

**[trick] NPV from PI, PI from NPV**
PI = 1 + NPV ÷ outlay, and NPV = (PI − 1) × outlay, whenever the whole outlay is at year 0. RTP January 2026's MCQ needs only this: NPV 15 on 60 is a PI of 1.25.

### Answer frame

**"Discuss NPV. How is it calculated?"** (SM 7.74, theory question 2): definition (the amount, in current value, the investment earns after paying cost of capital in each period); formula; ICAI's five steps; decision rule (accept if NPV ≥ 0; highest NPV among mutually exclusive projects); advantages and limitations, a line each.

**"Describe the advantages and disadvantages of profitability index"** (SM 7.74, question 5): the formula and its other names; two advantages; three limitations; the conclusion that PI cannot be used indiscriminately.

**PI and NPV: same decisions?** (RTP January 2025 Q11(c), ICAI's points): in most situations the same accept-reject decision (PI > 1 means NPV > 0); conflict in ranking mutually exclusive projects; a two-project example (larger NPV, smaller PI); reason: NPV is absolute, PI a ratio; PI is the better technique under capital rationing.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the ₹4 lakh profit that was ₹62,300** **Fix:** discount each year's cash flow first. At 12% the inflows are worth ₹10,62,300 and the NPV is ₹62,300.
- **The eight-year machine that won on paper** **Fix:** with unequal lives, compare equivalent annualised NPVs or NPVs over a common life. Here the two disagree, because K4's replacement costs more, and the common-life chain built on the supplier's price decides: K8, at ₹4,14,150 against ₹2,95,373.
- **Picking by PI when NPV should decide** **Fix:** between mutually exclusive projects, the highest NPV is selected; PI decides only when NPVs are the same, or when capital is rationed.
- **The cheaper scooter that was dearer** **Fix:** divide each by the PVAF for its own life; the lower equivalent annual cost wins.

### Recap

- Discount every cash flow to today at the cost of capital (WACC); PV factor = 1 ÷ (1 + k)ᵗ.
- NPV = PV of cash inflows − PV of cash outflows; accept if NPV ≥ 0; among mutually exclusive projects, the highest NPV.
- NPV is the addition to shareholders' wealth; its limitations: hard calculations, forecasts of cash flows and rate, an absolute measure.
- PI (desirability factor, present value index) = discounted inflows ÷ discounted outflow; accept if PI ≥ 1; it ranks by ratio and fails with indivisible projects under rationing.
- NPV and PI agree on accept-reject; they can conflict on ranking; with NPVs different, NPV decides.
- Unequal lives: replacement chain (common life) or equivalent annualised criterion, NPV ÷ PVAF for each life; the higher wins.
- Costs only: equivalent annual cost = PV of costs ÷ PVAF; the lower wins.
- Use the PV factors the question gives and say which: a printed annuity factor (4.111 at 12% for 6 years) and the sum of three-decimal yearly factors (4.112) differ in the last digits. (Convert the currencies first: a 2030 rupee is not a 2026 rupee.)

## Lesson 7.4: IRR, MIRR and the NPV-IRR conflict

*The discount rate at which NPV becomes zero, found by interpolation; MIRR, which reinvests at the cost of capital; and why NPV and IRR can rank mutually exclusive projects differently.*

### Key idea

IRR is the discount rate at which NPV is zero. Accept a project when its IRR is at least the cost of capital: for a single project this always agrees with NPV. When mutually exclusive projects differ in size, timing or life, IRR and NPV can rank them differently, and the NPV ranking is the one consistent with wealth maximisation.

### Formats and formulas

*Reproduce these from memory.*

**[formula] Internal rate of return**
$$\sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{C_t}{(1+r)^t} - I = 0$$
r is the IRR: the rate at which the present value of the inflows Cₜ equals the investment I (SM 7.32).

**[formula] Fake payback factor (uniform inflows)**
$$A = \frac{\text{Total initial investment}}{\text{Annual (equal) cash inflow}}$$
Look up A in the PVAF table in the row for the project's life (SM 7.33).

**[formula] Interpolation**
$$\text{IRR} = LR + \frac{\text{NPV at } LR}{\text{NPV at } LR - \text{NPV at } HR} \times (HR - LR)$$
or, with present values,
$$\text{IRR} = LR + \frac{\text{PV at } LR - CI}{\text{PV at } LR - \text{PV at } HR} \times (HR - LR)$$
LR = lower rate, HR = higher rate, CI = capital investment (SM 7.33).

**[formula] Modified internal rate of return**
$$\text{MIRR} = \left(\frac{\text{TV}}{\text{Initial outflow}}\right)^{1/n} - 1$$
TV, the terminal value: each inflow compounded to year n at the cost of capital, (1 + k)ⁿ⁻ᵗ. Alternatively, find outflow ÷ terminal value in the year-n row of the PV table (SM 7.45).

**[definition] Modified internal rate of return**
Under this method, all cash flows, apart from the initial investment, are brought to the terminal value using an appropriate discount rate (usually the Cost of Capital). This results in a single stream of cash inflow in the terminal year. The discount rate which equates the present value of the terminal cash inflow to the zero year outflow is called the MIRR. (SM 7.43)

**[trick] The two-line IRR sanity check**
NPV positive at a rate means the IRR is above that rate; NPV negative means below. So the IRR must lie between LR and HR, and nearer the rate whose NPV is smaller in size. Condition: conventional cash flows (one sign change). If your answer falls outside the bracket, the signs in the denominator are wrong.

### Answer frame

**"Write a short note on IRR"** (MTP Series II for May 2025, 2 marks; ICAI's points): the rate at which discounted cash inflows equal discounted cash outflows, the rate that discounts the cash flows to zero; it can be stated as discounted inflows ÷ discounted outflows = 1; found by trial and error, since the rate is unknown while the cash flows are known; compared with the cut-off rate: accept if more, otherwise reject.

**"Identify the limitations of IRR"** (old scheme May 2022, 4 marks): the four points of SM 7.42 in ICAI's order (tedious with interspersed outflows and multiple IRRs; peculiar results with different inflow/outflow patterns; reinvestment at the IRR; mutually exclusive projects with different outlays).

**"Describe MIRR"** (SM theory question 6; MTP Series I for January 2025, 2 marks): what it addresses (multiple IRRs, the reinvestment rate, consistency with NPV); the terminal value method in ICAI's words; the single outflow and single inflow; the decision rule.

**"Distinguish between NPV and IRR"**

| Point | NPV | IRR |
|---|---|---|
| Result | Absolute amount (₹) | Percentage |
| Discount rate | Cost of capital, given | Found; then compared with the cost of capital |
| Reinvestment assumed at | The discount rate | The project's IRR |
| Multiple results | One NPV at a given rate | Several IRRs possible when signs change |
| Mutually exclusive projects | Highest NPV; consistent with wealth maximisation | May conflict with NPV (size, timing, lives) |

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the minus sign that sank the IRR** **Fix:** the denominator is NPV at LR − NPV at HR = 2,400 − (−1,600) = 4,000, so IRR = 18% + 1.2% = 19.2%.
- **Choosing the mutually exclusive project with the higher IRR** **Fix:** with mutually exclusive projects that conflict, choose the higher NPV and give the reason: NPV is consistent with wealth maximisation; IRR ignores scale and assumes reinvestment at the IRR.
- **Compounding the last year's inflow, or the outlay** **Fix:** an inflow of year t takes (1 + k)ⁿ⁻ᵗ; check that the year-1 inflow has the largest factor.
- **Reading the fake payback factor as the answer** **Fix:** discount the actual cash flows at two rates and interpolate.

### Recap

- IRR: the discount rate that equates the PV of the expected cash inflows with the initial cash outflow (NPV = 0).
- Uniform inflows: fake payback = investment ÷ annual inflow, read in the PVAF row for the life, then interpolate. Uneven inflows: trial and error at two rates, then interpolate.
- IRR = LR + NPV at LR ÷ (NPV at LR − NPV at HR) × (HR − LR); the IRR lies between LR and HR.
- Accept if IRR ≥ cut-off rate (WACC), reject if lower; for one project this always agrees with NPV ≥ 0.
- NPV and IRR can conflict for mutually exclusive projects: size disparity, time disparity, unequal lives. Follow NPV (wealth maximisation); for unequal lives, the equivalent annualised NPV.
- Reinvestment: NPV at the discount rate, IRR at the IRR; sign changes can give multiple IRRs.
- MIRR (terminal value method): compound inflows to year n at the cost of capital; MIRR = $(\text{TV} \div \text{outlay})^{1/n} - 1$; accept if MIRR > required rate.
- State your factors: once-rounded annuity factors and summed yearly factors differ in the last digit. (A project's own FD rate is impressive only if you can redeposit at it.)

## Lesson 7.5: Capital rationing

*When every project has a positive NPV but the budget cannot fund them all: rank divisible projects by NPV per rupee, and test combinations of indivisible ones for the highest total NPV.*

### Key idea

Under capital rationing, maximise the total NPV the budget can buy. Divisible projects: rank by NPV per rupee (the profitability index) and fill the budget, the last project in part. Indivisible projects: examine the combinations within the budget and select the one with the highest total NPV, even if it skips a higher-ranked project.

### Formats and formulas

*Reproduce these from memory.*

**[formula] NPV per rupee of investment**
$$\text{NPV per ₹1} = \frac{\text{NPV}}{\text{Investment}} \qquad \text{PI} = 1 + \frac{\text{NPV}}{\text{Investment}}$$
The PI form holds when the whole outlay is made at year 0; both give the same ranking (SM 7.53–7.54).

**[formula] NPV of a part project**
$$\text{NPV of the part taken} = \text{NPV} \times \frac{\text{Funds remaining}}{\text{Investment}}$$

**[exam] What earns the marks**

**[trick] NPV per rupee from PI**
NPV per ₹1 = PI − 1, so a PI of 1.249 is 24.9 paise of NPV per rupee. Condition: the whole outlay at year 0 (otherwise PI's denominator is the PV of all outflows).

### Answer frame

**"What is capital rationing? How are projects selected under it?"**

1. Meaning: funds are restricted, and management has to choose from amongst available alternative investments, all with positive NPVs.
2. Objective: maximise the total NPV obtainable within the funds available (wealth maximisation).
3. Independent and divisible projects: modify the NPV rule; rank by NPV per rupee of capital (equivalently PI); accept in order, the last project in part.
4. Indivisible projects: rank by absolute NPV, examine combinations within the limit, and select the combination with the highest total NPV.
5. Note PI's limitation for indivisible projects, and that negative-NPV projects are never included.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: ranking divisible projects by the size of their NPV** **Fix:** a rationed budget is spent rupee by rupee; rank divisible projects by NPV per rupee.
- **Taking indivisible projects in PI order and stopping** **Fix:** list the combinations that fit and compare their total NPVs; here P + Q + S gives ₹20.41 lakh.
- **Spending the leftover on a negative-NPV project** **Fix:** a project with NPV < 0 (PI < 1) is rejected under rationing as it is without it.
- **Taking the full NPV of a part project** **Fix:** NPV of the part = NPV × funds remaining ÷ investment.

### Recap

- Capital rationing: funds are restricted, so management chooses among positive-NPV projects; the aim is the highest total NPV within the budget.
- Independent, divisible projects: rank by NPV per rupee of capital (NPV ÷ investment), accept in order, take the last in part with the same fraction of its NPV.
- NPV per ₹1 = PI − 1 when the outlay is at year 0: the same ranking; PI is the better technique under capital rationing.
- Indivisible projects: rank by absolute NPV, examine combinations within the limit, select the highest total NPV; a lower-ranked project may replace a higher-ranked one (Illustration 13: E + B + D beats E + B + C).
- PI fails as a guide where projects are indivisible (SM 7.32).
- Negative-NPV projects are never accepted, even with idle funds; unused budget is not a reason to accept.
