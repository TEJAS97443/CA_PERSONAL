## Lesson 8.1: Forms of dividend, buy-back and determinants of dividend policy

*How profit after tax reaches shareholders (cash dividend, buy-back, bonus shares), what a stock split does, the factors that shape a dividend policy, payout policies and Lintner's model.*

### Key idea

Dividend is the part of profit after tax distributed to shareholders; the rest is retained for growth (g = b × r). Cash dividends and buy-backs pay out cash, bonus shares only capitalise reserves, and stock splits only change the number of shares. The right payout depends on investment opportunities, cost of capital, shareholders' expectations, legal and tax constraints, and the share price.

### Formats and formulas

*Reproduce these from memory.*

**[formula] Payout and retention**
$$\text{Payout ratio} = \frac{\text{DPS}}{\text{EPS}} \qquad b = 1 - \text{Payout ratio}$$
DPS = dividend per share; b = retention ratio.

**[formula] Growth from retained earnings**
$$g = b \times r$$
b = retention ratio, r = rate of return on investment (SM 8.8).

**[formula] Lintner's model**
$$D_1 = D_0 + [(\text{EPS} \times \text{Target payout}) - D_0] \times A_f$$
Af = adjustment factor (speed of adjustment), between 0 and 1 (SM 8.14).

**[formula] Share buy-back**
$$\text{Shares bought back} = \frac{\text{Amount}}{\text{Buy-back price}} \qquad \text{BVPS after} = \frac{\text{Book value before} - \text{Amount paid}}{\text{Shares remaining}}$$
Cash paid out reduces net worth; with earnings unchanged, EPS after = earnings ÷ shares remaining (SM 8.54, problem 9).

**Ex-dividend price** = cum-dividend price − dividend per share (SM 8.4; 8.54, problem 8).

**[definition] Stock split**
Stock split means splitting one share into many, say, one share of ₹500 into 5 shares of ₹100. (SM 8.33)

**[definition] Share buyback**
Share buyback, in simple terms, means buying/repurchasing own shares by the company resulting into decrease in share capital of the company. Thus, the shares bought back are cancelled leading reduction in outstanding number of shares. (SM 8.33)

**[exam] The traps ICAI used**
- "Options other than cash used for distributing profits": the Study Material's answer is **bonus shares**, not "both bonus shares and stock split" (SM 8.41, MCQ 8; answer 8.47). A split distributes nothing.
- Lintner in reverse (January 2026 MCQ 8): 50 = D₀ + (80 − D₀) × 0.25, so 0.75 D₀ = 30 and D₀ = ₹40.
- Buy-backs at a premium: the price paid, not the market price, reduces book value (MTP Series II for September 2024).
- Reinvesting a dividend: buy at the ex-dividend price (SM problem 8: ₹50 − ₹10 = ₹40, so ₹10,00,000 buys 25,000 shares).

**[trick] Lintner closes a fixed share of the gap**
Each year the dividend moves Af of the way from last year's dividend to the target (EPS × target payout). With Af = 0.5 it halves the gap; reversed, D₀ = (D₁ − Af × target) ÷ (1 − Af). Condition: Af between 0 and 1.

### Answer frame

**"State dividend decision. Explain the factors which govern it"** (SM theory question 1; ICAI's answer points to paragraphs 1, 2 and 6 of the chapter): the dividend decision as one of the three long-term finance decisions, with the financing and investment decisions; meaning (part of PAT distributed; the rest retained for growth); then the nine determinants in ICAI's order, one line each: availability of funds, cost of capital, capital structure, stock price, investment opportunities, trend of industry, expectation of shareholders, legal constraints (Section 123), taxation.

**"Explain the advantages and disadvantages of stock dividend"** (SM theory question 2): advantages to shareholders (three) and to the company (two), then limitations to shareholders and to the company, as in the tables above.

**"Discuss the practical considerations in dividend policy"** (SM theory question 3): (a) financial needs, with the mature and growth company contrast; (b) constraints: legal, liquidity, access to capital market, investment opportunities; (c) payout policies: (i) constant dividend policy (fixed amount, dividend equalisation reserve); (ii) stable dividend policy (constant payout ratio; dividends based on long-term sustainable earnings, Lintner's target payout).

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the stock split that "distributed profits"** **Fix:** bonus shares capitalise reserves and are a form of dividend; a stock split only divides each share into more shares of lower face value.
- **Using the market price instead of the buy-back price** **Fix:** in Tapri's tender offer, ₹96 crore buys 16 lakh shares at ₹600, not 20 lakh at the market price of ₹480.
- **Lintner without the brake** **Fix:** only the fraction Af of the gap is closed: ₹8 + (₹12 − ₹8) × 0.5 = ₹10, not ₹12.
- **Payout in place of retention in g = br** **Fix:** b is the retention ratio, 1 − payout.

### Recap

- Dividend: the part of profit after tax distributed to shareholders; the rest is retained for growth, g = b × r.
- Forms: cash dividend; share repurchase (treasury or cancelled shares), same effect on wealth as a cash dividend; stock dividend (bonus shares), which capitalises reserves and leaves net worth unchanged.
- Bonus conditions (SEBI): not in lieu of cash dividend; authorised by AOA, all shares fully paid; no default on loans, interest or statutory dues; only from share premium and free reserves.
- Stock split: one share into many of lower face value; makes shares affordable; costs money and may attract speculators. Not a distribution of profits.
- Buy-back: open market or tender offer; reduces shares outstanding; BVPS after = (book value − amount paid) ÷ shares remaining.
- Determinants: funds, cost of capital, capital structure, stock price, investment opportunities, industry trend, shareholders' expectations, legal constraints, taxation.
- Payout policies: constant dividend (fixed amount, dividend equalisation reserve); stable dividend (constant payout ratio, or dividends based on long-term sustainable earnings). Lintner: D₁ = D₀ + [(EPS × target payout) − D₀] × Af, two parameters.
- (A bonus issue cuts the pie into more slices: nobody gets more pie.)

## Lesson 8.2: Dividend theories: traditional, Walter, Gordon and Modigliani-Miller

*Does the payout change the share price? Walter and Gordon say yes, through r against Ke; Graham and Dodd weight dividends more; Modigliani and Miller say no, in a perfect market.*

### Key idea

Walter and Gordon: dividend policy is relevant; compare r with Ke. r > Ke: zero payout is optimum; r < Ke: 100%; r = Ke: no optimum (Walter: every payout is optimum). MM: dividend policy is irrelevant; value depends on earnings and investment policy. Graham and Dodd: P = m(D + E/3), so dividends weigh more than retained earnings.

### Formats and formulas

*Reproduce these from memory; write the formula, then the figures.*

**[formula] Graham and Dodd (traditional approach)**
$$P = m\left(D + \frac{E}{3}\right)$$
m = a multiplier (SM 8.39).

**[formula] Walter's model**
$$P = \frac{D + \dfrac{r}{K_e}(E - D)}{K_e}$$
r = return on investment; Ke = capitalisation rate (SM 8.22).

**[formula] Gordon's model**
$$P_0 = \frac{E_1(1 - b)}{K_e - br} \qquad P_0 = \frac{D_0(1 + g)}{K_e - g} = \frac{D_1}{K_e - g}$$
b = retention ratio; g = br; valid only if Ke > g (SM 8.27, 8.29).

**[formula] Zero growth and multi-stage DDM**
$$P_0 = \frac{D}{K_e} \qquad P_0 = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{D_t}{(1+K_e)^t} \qquad + \frac{D_{n+1}}{K_e - g} \times \frac{1}{(1+K_e)^n}$$
The second term is the price at the end of the high-growth years, discounted (SM 8.30; 8.51 and 8.54).

**[formula] Modigliani-Miller**
$$P_0 = \frac{P_1 + D_1}{1 + K_e} \qquad nP_0 = \frac{(n + \Delta n)P_1 - I + E}{1 + K_e}$$
Δn = funds required ÷ P₁, where funds required = I − (E − nD₁) (SM 8.17–8.18).

**The MM four steps** (SM 8.19–8.21, Illustration 1)

| Step | Dividend paid | Dividend not paid |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Year-end price | P₁ = P₀(1 + Ke) − D₁ | P₁ = P₀(1 + Ke) |
| 2. Funds required | I − (E − nD₁) | I − E |
| 3. New shares | Δn = funds required ÷ P₁ | Δn = funds required ÷ P₁ |
| 4. Value of firm | [(n + Δn)P₁ − I + E] ÷ (1 + Ke) | Same formula: same value |

**[exam] The traps ICAI used**
- Preference dividend first: EPS = (net profit − preference dividend) ÷ equity shares (May 2026 Q3(a); Illustrations 3 and 4).
- "Retention ratio of 20%" means payout 80%: D = 0.8 × EPS (May 2026 Q3(a)).
- Gordon in its D form needs D₁ = D₀(1 + g): January 2026 Q1(c) took 10 × 1.2 ÷ (0.25 − 0.20) = ₹240.
- Walter's optimum when r = Ke is "every payout ratio"; Gordon's is "no optimum ratio" (September 2025 Q2(b)).
- MM funds required subtract retained earnings (E − nD₁), not total earnings, when a dividend is paid.
- "The P/E ratio at which the dividend policy will have no effect": the one at which Ke = r, so P/E = 1 ÷ r (for r = 15%, a P/E of 6.67; SM 8.50).

**[trick] The r-versus-Ke switch**
One comparison answers every "optimum payout" question in Walter and Gordon: r > Ke, zero; r < Ke, 100%; r = Ke, payout does not matter. At 100% payout both models give P = E ÷ Ke. Condition: the models' assumptions (r and Ke constant, retained earnings the only finance).

### Answer frame

**"MM hypothesis is based on certain assumptions. What are they?"** (May 2026 Q4(a), 4 marks): the five, in ICAI's order and words: perfect capital markets; no taxes (with the reason: universal applicability, since tax rates differ between countries); fixed investment policy; no floatation or transaction cost; no risk of uncertainty, one discount rate for all securities and periods.

**"List any four assumptions of Gordon's model"** (September 2024 Q4(b), 4 marks): all-equity firm; IRR constant; Ke constant; retention ratio constant; growth rate g = br constant; Ke > g; investments financed by retained earnings only. Give the reason ICAI attaches to each.

**"Briefly explain the assumptions of Walter's model"** (old scheme May 2022 Q6(b), 4 marks): the six assumptions above.

**"State the limitations of Walter's model"** (MTP Series I for January 2026, 2 marks): does not consider all factors, and Ke is difficult to determine; ignores taxation, legal and contractual obligations, management policy and attitude.

**Numerical answers**: working notes first (EPS after preference dividend, DPS, r and Ke, including Ke from a P/E ratio), then the formula, the figures and the result; end with the r against Ke conclusion in one sentence.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: forgetting the preference dividend** **Fix:** deduct the preference dividend first: (72 − 18) ÷ 6 = ₹9.
- **Last year's dividend in Gordon's numerator** **Fix:** the formula needs next year's dividend, D₀(1 + g) = ₹12, so P = ₹240.
- **MM with the wrong funds required** **Fix:** in Sheetal Cold Chain's Policy B, ₹56,00,000 − (₹70,00,000 − ₹60,00,000) = ₹46,00,000, at the dividend-case price of ₹125; I − E would even say ₹14,00,000 is left over.
- **The optimum payout as a "balanced" 50%** **Fix:** compare r with Ke and write the corner; only r = Ke makes the payout irrelevant.

### Recap

- Relevance: Walter and Gordon; irrelevance: MM; Graham and Dodd weight dividends more: P = m(D + E/3).
- Walter: P = [D + (r ÷ Ke)(E − D)] ÷ Ke; r > Ke zero payout, r < Ke 100%, r = Ke every payout optimum.
- Gordon: P₀ = E₁(1 − b) ÷ (Ke − br) = D₁ ÷ (Ke − g); needs Ke > g; r = Ke has no optimum ratio.
- Bird-in-hand: risk-averse investors prefer certain current dividends. DDM: zero growth D ÷ Ke; constant growth; multi-stage = PV of dividends + PV of the price at the end of the high-growth phase.
- MM: value depends on earning power and investment policy; P₀ = (P₁ + D₁) ÷ (1 + Ke); nP₀ = [(n + Δn)P₁ − I + E] ÷ (1 + Ke); Δn = [I − (E − nD₁)] ÷ P₁.
- MM assumptions: perfect markets, no taxes, fixed investment policy, no floatation or transaction cost, no uncertainty; dividends and repurchases are equivalent.
- Walter limitations: not all factors, Ke hard to determine, ignores taxes, legal and contractual obligations, management attitude.
- Preference dividend before EPS; D₁, not D₀, in Gordon; Ke = 1 ÷ P/E when only P/E is given. (Plant the seeds only if they beat the market.)
