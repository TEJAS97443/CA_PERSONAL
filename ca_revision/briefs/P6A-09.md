## Lesson 9.1: Working capital, the operating cycle and financing policy

*What working capital is (gross, net, permanent, temporary), what decides how much a business needs, the operating cycle R + W + F + D − C, and how investment and financing policies trade liquidity for profit.*

### Key idea

Working capital is the money stuck in the operating cycle: current assets minus current liabilities. The longer the cycle (days in raw material, work-in-progress, finished goods and debtors, less suppliers' credit), the more of it a business needs. Holding more is safer but earns less; holding less earns more but risks running dry. The permanent part is financed long-term, the temporary part short-term.

### Formats and formulas

*Reproduce the operating cycle formula and the five component formulas from memory: the denominators carry the marks.*

**[formula] Working capital**
$$\text{Net working capital} = \text{Current assets} - \text{Current liabilities} \qquad \text{Gross working capital} = \text{Current assets}$$

**[formula] Operating cycle and number of cycles**
$$\text{Operating cycle} = R + W + F + D - C \qquad \text{Number of cycles} = \frac{360 \text{ or } 365}{\text{Operating cycle}}$$

**[formula] The five components (SM 9.20)**
$$R = \frac{\text{Average stock of raw material}}{\text{Average cost of raw material consumption per day}}$$
$$W = \frac{\text{Average work-in-progress inventory}}{\text{Average cost of production per day}}$$
$$F = \frac{\text{Average stock of finished goods}}{\text{Average cost of goods sold per day}}$$
$$D = \frac{\text{Average receivables}}{\text{Average credit sales per day}} \qquad C = \frac{\text{Average payables}}{\text{Average credit purchases per day}}$$
Average = (opening + closing) ÷ 2. Raw material consumed = opening stock + purchases − closing stock. Work-in-progress inventory may also be termed works cost (SM 9.19).

| Item | Numerator | Denominator (per day) |
|---|---|---|
| R | Average raw material stock | Raw material consumed |
| W | Average WIP | Cost of production |
| F | Average finished goods | Cost of goods sold |
| D | Average receivables | Credit sales |
| C | Average payables | Credit purchases |

**[formula] The ratio tests of the optimum (SM 9.7)**
$$\text{Current ratio} = \frac{\text{Current assets}}{\text{Current liabilities}} \qquad \text{Quick ratio} = \frac{\text{Current assets} - \text{Inventory} - \text{Prepaid expenses}}{\text{Current liabilities}}$$
Thumb rule: current ratio 2 for a manufacturing firm, quick ratio at least 1; the real optimum depends on the business.

**[formula] Investment policy measures (SM 9.15–9.16)**
$$\frac{\text{Current assets}}{\text{Fixed assets}} \qquad \text{Return on total assets} = \frac{\text{EBIT}}{\text{Total assets}}$$
Higher CA/FA ratio: conservative; lower: aggressive (fixed assets constant).

**[definition] Permanent and temporary working capital (SM 9.5)**
**Permanent working capital** refers to the base working capital, which is the minimum level of investment in the current assets that is carried by the entity at all times to carry its day-to-day activities. **Temporary working capital** refers to that part of total working capital which is required by an entity in addition to the permanent working capital; it is also called variable or fluctuating working capital.

**[exam] The traps ICAI set**
- Raw material storage uses **consumption**, not purchases: compute opening + purchases − closing first (TYK Problem 1, SM 9.129).
- WIP divides by **cost of production**, finished goods by **cost of goods sold** (September 2024 Q1(b)); receivables by **credit** sales and payables by **credit** purchases (SM 9.20).
- September 2025 MCQs ran the formulas backwards: from the number of cycles to the periods. Know each formula well enough to solve for its numerator.
- The paper's instruction: "Working notes should form part of the answer."

**[trick] Which flow empties the tank?**
Each period = average balance ÷ the daily flow that **leaves** that stage: raw material leaves by consumption, WIP by completion (cost of production), finished goods by sale (cost of goods sold), debtors by collection (credit sales), creditors by payment (credit purchases). Holds whenever the periods are measured in days of cost (or sales, for debtors).

### Answer frame

### "Discuss the factors to be considered while planning working capital requirement"

Name and explain the nine factors in ICAI's order, one line of example each: need for cash; desired level of inventory; receivables; short-term financing options; nature of business; market and demand conditions; technology and manufacturing policies; operating efficiency; price level changes and exchange rate fluctuations. In a case ("a pharmacy chain facing rising costs and uncertain demand"), tie each factor to a fact of the case.

### "Discuss the liquidity vs profitability issue"

1. Each rupee of capital bears a cost; liquidity must be maintained with that cost in mind.
2. Higher working capital may raise revenue and profit, but funds tied up in idle assets reduce liquidity and the opportunity to earn better returns.
3. Hence a trade-off is required that increases profitability without disturbing day-to-day functioning, through the 3 Es: economy in financing, efficiency in utilisation, effectiveness in achieving objectives.
4. The component table: inventory, receivables, prepaid expenses, cash, payables, with the higher side, the trade-off tool and the lower side.

### "Permanent and fluctuating working capital are both necessary. Describe."

Define each in ICAI's words (base level carried at all times, financed long-term; the part in addition to it, due to fluctuations in sales, e.g. seasonal inventory); a sketch of the two diagrams (flat and rising permanent line with the temporary bulge above); conclude that both facilitate production and sales through the operating cycle, and that the permanent part is financed by long-term and the temporary part by short-term sources.

### "Explain the approaches of working capital investment"

| Point | Aggressive | Conservative |
|---|---|---|
| Current assets | Minimal | High |
| Inventory, credit, cash | Low, strict, little | High, liberal, ample |
| Liquidity and risk | Poor liquidity, higher risk | Greater liquidity, lower risk |
| Return | Higher | Lower |
| Danger | Stock-outs, lost growth | Cost of capital, obsolescence, bad debts |
| CA ÷ FA | Lower | Higher |

Add: moderate lies between and is what most firms follow.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the samosas measured against the wrong denominator** **Fix:** each stage over the flow that leaves it (the trick above); raw material consumed = opening + purchases − closing; only credit sales and credit purchases for D and C.
- **The creditors that were added** **Fix:** R + W + F + D − C, and check: the net cycle is always shorter than the gross cycle.
- **"The higher the current ratio, the better"** **Fix:** in theory answers say "neither too high nor too low"; the thumb rule of 2 (and quick ratio of 1) depends on the business.
- **Swapping aggressive and conservative** **Fix:** aggressive takes risk to save funds; conservative pays for safety.

### Recap

- Net working capital = current assets − current liabilities; gross working capital = current assets (SM 9.3–9.4).
- Permanent: the base carried at all times, financed long-term; temporary (variable, fluctuating): the extra due to sales fluctuations, financed short-term.
- Too much: idle funds, low return, over-capitalisation. Too little: insolvency risk, lost sales. Thumb rule: current ratio 2, quick ratio 1.
- Nine determinants: cash, inventory, receivables, short-term financing, nature of business, market and demand, technology and manufacturing policy, operating efficiency, price and exchange rates.
- Investment approaches: aggressive (minimal current assets, high return, high risk), conservative (the reverse), moderate; higher CA ÷ FA = more conservative.
- Operating cycle = R + W + F + D − C; number of cycles = 360 (or 365) ÷ operating cycle.
- Denominators: consumption, cost of production, cost of goods sold, credit sales, credit purchases.
- Hedging (matching) approach: finance each asset with funds of the same maturity. (Diwali cabins are hired, not bought.)

## Lesson 9.2: Estimating working capital requirement

*Turning the operating cycle into rupees: the operating cycle shortcut, the item-by-item statement of working capital (ICAI's format), the cash cost basis, the half-period rule for WIP and the effect of double shift working.*

### Key idea

Each current asset = the cost flowing through it per period × the periods it is held; each current liability = the expense that flows into it per period × the periods it stays unpaid. Value each item at the cost actually paid in (for cash cost basis: no profit, no depreciation), take half the conversion cost for work-in-progress when it accrues evenly, subtract the liabilities and add the safety margin.

### Formats and formulas

*Reproduce ICAI's statement from memory: the headings, the order and "Safety Margin" carry marks.*

| Estimation of Working Capital Requirements (SM 9.24–9.25) | ₹ | ₹ |
|---|---:|---:|
| **I. Current Assets:** | | |
| Inventories: Raw materials; Work-in-process; Finished goods | --- | --- |
| Receivables: Trade debtors; Bills | --- | --- |
| Prepaid expenses | | --- |
| Minimum cash balance | | --- |
| **Gross Working Capital** | | **---** |
| **II. Current Liabilities:** | | |
| Trade payables; Bills payable | --- | |
| Wages payable; Payables for overheads | --- | --- |
| **III. Excess of current assets over current liabilities [I − II]** | | **---** |
| IV. Safety margin | | --- |
| **V. Net Working Capital [III + IV]** | | **---** |

**[formula] Operating cycle method**
$$\text{Working capital} = \frac{\text{Annual operating cost}}{\text{Number of operating cycles}}$$
Cash cost basis: annual operating cost less depreciation.

**[formula] Current assets**
$$\text{Item} = \frac{\text{Annual cost base}}{12 \text{ months (or 365 days)}} \times \text{Holding period}$$

| Item | Annual cost base (total cost basis) | Cash cost basis |
|---|---|---|
| Raw materials | Material consumed | Same |
| Work-in-progress | Material + share of conversion cost | Without depreciation |
| Finished goods | Cost of production | Cash cost of production |
| Debtors | Cost of sales or sales value | Cash cost of sales |
| Prepaid expenses | The expense paid in advance | Same |
| Trade payables | Credit purchases | Same |
| Wages, overheads payable | The expense paid in arrear | Same (no depreciation) |

**[formula] Cash costs (Illustration 4, SM 9.30)**
$$\text{Cash cost of production} = \text{Materials} + \text{Wages} \qquad + \text{Cash manufacturing expenses}$$
$$\text{Cash cost of sales} = \text{Cash cost of production} \qquad + \text{Administrative expenses} \qquad + \text{Selling expenses}$$
Adjust for opening and closing finished stock when they differ (TYK Problem 5, SM 9.139).

**[formula] Work-in-progress (material at the start, conversion accruing evenly)**
$$\text{WIP} = \text{Material for the period in process} \qquad + \tfrac{1}{2} \times \text{Conversion cost for that period}$$

**[exam] The traps ICAI set**
- "Safety margin 15% of the net working capital **inclusive of the margin amount**" (MTP January 2025 Series II): the requirement is (CA − CL) ÷ 0.85, not × 1.15.
- Cash "to the extent of 50% of current liabilities" (September 2026 Q1(c)): compute the liabilities first.
- Depreciation inside "cost of production" or "manufacturing overheads": take it out for the cash cost basis (May 2025 MCQs; RTP May 2025).
- The paper's instruction: "Working notes should form part of the answer." Every line of the statement needs its working.

**[trick] Months, weeks or days: divide by the same unit**
Annual base ÷ 12 × months, ÷ 52 × weeks, or ÷ 360 (365) × days, whichever unit the question uses for the periods. Never mix: a "1½ months" credit with a 360-day year is 45 days ÷ 360, the same as 1.5 ÷ 12.

### Answer frame

### "Discuss the estimation of working capital need based on the operating cycle process" (SM 9.115, Q3)

1. The operating cycle is one of the most reliable methods of computing working capital (the others: ratio of sales, ratio of fixed investments); it estimates working capital from the average holding period of current assets, related to costs.
2. Operating cycle = R + W + F + D − C, each component defined, with its formula (average balance ÷ daily cost or sales).
3. Number of operating cycles = 360 (365) ÷ operating cycle; working capital = annual operating cost ÷ number of cycles.
4. Item by item: raw materials, WIP, finished goods, debtors (at cash cost, excluding profit and depreciation), cash; less trade payables, wages and overheads payable.
5. Add a safety margin; the shorter the cycle, the lower the requirement.

### The numerical, three ways

**[cols] **Loses marks****

A statement with bare figures: "Debtors 8,88,750; Creditors 3,75,000…"

No base, no period: an examiner cannot give method marks to a wrong number.
---
**Passes**

Each line shows base × period ("94,80,000 × 1.5/12"), but the cash sales are left inside debtors and depreciation inside the finished goods.

Two of the traps cost the marks.
---
**Full marks**

Working notes first (cash cost of production, WIP, cash cost of sales and the credit sales' share, the prepaid rent), then ICAI's statement with every base and period visible, safety margin last, assumptions stated.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the overdraft that was counted as cash** **Fix:** cash = ₹1,75,000 (SM 9.143); and do not deduct bank borrowing as a current liability.
- **Profit smuggled into the debtors** **Fix:** build the cash cost of sales working note first, take the credit sales' share, and the debtors come from it.
- **WIP charged with a full period of conversion** **Fix:** full material, half conversion: ₹7,92,000. Use the stated percentage when the question gives a degree of completion.
- **WIP doubled in double shift** **Fix:** the quantity in process is unchanged, because work started in the first shift is completed in the second (SM 9.31); only its value per unit changes (a lower fixed wage per unit, a higher night-shift wage, cheaper material if there is a discount).

### Recap

- Three methods: current assets holding period (operating cycle), ratio of sales, ratio of fixed investments (SM 9.16–9.17).
- Operating cycle method: working capital = annual operating cost ÷ number of operating cycles; cash basis: depreciation out first.
- Each item = annual base ÷ 12 (52, 360, 365) × period held; current liabilities other than bank credit are deducted.
- WIP with material at the start and conversion accruing evenly: full material + half conversion for the period in process.
- Cash cost basis: finished goods at cash cost of production, debtors at cash cost of sales (no profit, no depreciation).
- ICAI's statement: current assets (gross working capital) − current liabilities = excess; + safety margin = net working capital.
- Double shift: WIP units unchanged; other stocks rise, though not always in proportion; every item valued at the new cost per unit (lower fixed cost per unit).
- Overdraft limits are not cash; a margin "inclusive of itself" divides. (Count the platform, not the trains.)

## Lesson 9.3: Management of receivables, credit policy, factoring and forfaiting

*How a firm decides whom to give credit, on what terms and how to collect; evaluating credit policies by the total and incremental approaches; and turning receivables into cash through pledging, factoring and forfaiting.*

### Key idea

Credit is worth giving only up to the point where the extra contribution from extra sales exceeds the extra bad debts, cash discount, collection cost and the opportunity cost of the money locked in receivables. Factoring and forfaiting turn receivables into cash: factoring is worth it when its effective cost is below the firm's cost of borrowing.

### Formats and formulas

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---:|
| **A. Annual savings (benefit) on taking factoring service** | |
| Cost of credit administration saved | … |
| Bad debts avoided | … |

| **B. Annual cost of factoring to the firm** | |

**[formula] Effective cost of factoring (SM 9.84)**
$$\text{Rate of effective cost} = \frac{\text{Net annual cost of factoring}}{\text{Advances to be paid}} \times 100 \qquad \text{or} \quad \frac{\text{Net annual cost}}{\text{Amount available for advance}} \times 100$$
Amount available for advance = receivables − factoring commission − factoring reserve; advances to be paid = amount available for advance − interest deducted by the factor. A year is taken as 360 days.

**[formula] Cash discount and average collection period**
$$\text{Cash discount} = \text{Credit sales} \qquad \times \text{Share of customers taking the discount} \qquad \times \text{Rate}$$
Average collection period = the sum, over the groups of customers, of each group's share of credit sales × the days that group takes to pay. From a receivables turnover ratio: collection period in months = 12 ÷ turnover (Illustration 13, SM 9.80).

**[formula] Expected rate of return (Illustration 12)**
$$\text{Expected rate of return} = \frac{\text{Incremental expected profit}}{\text{Incremental investment}} \times 100$$

**[definition] Factoring (SM 9.82, 9.106)**
The outright sale of accounts receivables to a factor or financial agency; a continuous arrangement in which the factor purchases the client's trade debts, with or without recourse, exercises control over the credit extended to customers and administers the client's sales ledger.

**[definition] Forfaiting (SM 9.86)**
An arrangement of bill discounting in which a financial institution or bank buys the trade bills or receivables from exporters, the exporter relinquishing the right to receive payment from the importer, providing immediate finance on a without recourse basis.

**[exam] The traps ICAI set**

**[trick] Credit pays when the margin beats the lock-up**
A longer credit period pays only if extra contribution − extra bad debts − extra cash discount exceeds the extra investment in debtors × required return. This is just the difference between two policies' net benefits, so it always agrees with the total approach. Conditions: fixed costs unchanged, and profit and the required return on the same (pre- or post-tax) basis. Still work out every policy: a step that fails can be followed by one that passes.

### Answer frame

### "Explain the three aspects of management of receivables"

1. **Credit policy**: credit standards, credit terms and collection efforts; the trade-off between profit on additional sales and the cost of carrying debtors and bad debts; credit period ("net 50") and cash discount policy (rate, discount period, net credit period: "3/15 net 60").
2. **Credit analysis**: how risky it is to advance credit to a party; due diligence or reputation check.
3. **Control of receivables**: follow-up and a suitable collection policy; laying down policies and executing them.

### "Describe factoring" / "Describe the salient features of forfaiting"

**Factoring**: definition (outright sale of receivables to a factor, who bears the risk of collection and services the accounts for a fee); with or without recourse; advantages (immediate cash, predictable cash flows, no impact on debt-equity, not a loan, efficient record keeping and collection); the evaluation (savings against commission and interest; accept if the effective cost is below the cost of borrowing).

**Forfaiting**: meaning ("relinquish a right"; bank buys exporters' receivables without recourse); the four functions in order; the five features; Exim Bank's Buyer's Credit as the example.

### "Explain the ageing schedule"

Receivables classified by age from the date of invoice (0–30, 31–60, 61–90, 91–120 days and more); compared with earlier schedules and with other firms; gives closer control over the quality of individual accounts; shows old accounts accumulating; supplements the average collection period. A two-date table makes the point.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the profit locked up in debtors** **Fix:** total cost (or variable cost, when only the contribution ratio is given), and say which; ICAI uses sales only when no cost is given (TYK Problem 11, SM 9.151).
- **The discount rate applied to everybody** **Fix:** credit sales × share taking the discount × rate.
- **Commission for one period compared with an annual saving** **Fix:** put everything on an annual basis: × 360 ÷ 72.
- **Factoring and forfaiting treated as the same thing** **Fix:** forfaiting is always without recourse and for export receivables; factoring may be either way and domestic or international.

### Recap

- Objective: optimise the return on investment in receivables; three aspects: credit policy, credit analysis, control of receivables.
- "3/15 net 60": 3% discount within 15 days, otherwise pay within 60; cash discount = credit sales × share taking it × rate.
- Costs of maintaining receivables: funds blocked (interest or opportunity cost), administration, collection, default.
- Total approach: expected profit − opportunity cost of investment in receivables; incremental approach: incremental profit − required return on incremental investment; both pick the same policy.
- Opportunity cost = total cost of credit sales × collection period ÷ 360 (365) × required rate; fixed cost = (average − variable cost per unit) × present units.
- Factoring: sale of receivables, with or without recourse, no impact on debt-equity; accept if effective cost < cost of borrowing.
- Effective cost of factoring = net annual cost ÷ advances to be paid (or ÷ amount available for advance) × 100.
- Forfaiting: exporters' receivables sold to a bank without recourse; Exim Bank's Buyer's Credit. Ageing schedule: receivables by age, compared over time. (Hand the diary to the agent only if his cut is cheaper than the bank.)

## Lesson 9.4: Treasury and cash management

*What the treasury does, why a firm holds cash, the cash budget with a minimum balance, the four floats and how to cut them, Baumol and Miller-Orr for the optimum balance, and where surplus cash goes.*

### Key idea

Cash management is a balance between liquidity and cost: enough cash to meet every payment on time, no rupee left idle, and every surplus rupee parked where it is safe, short-term and quickly saleable.

### The layout with a minimum balance (ICAI Illustration 6, SM 9.46)

When a minimum balance is kept and surpluses are invested or deficits financed at each month end, ICAI's solution adds these lines below the receipts and payments:

| Line | How it is found |
|---|---|
| Total cash available (A) | Opening balance + receipts |
| Total payments (B) | All cash payments |
| Minimum cash balance desired | Given |

| Investment / financing (D) | Investments in brackets; liquidations and borrowings positive |
| Closing cash balance | A + D − B, which equals the minimum |

**[formula] Baumol: optimum cash balance (SM 9.60)**
$$C = \sqrt{\frac{2U \times P}{S}}$$
C = optimum cash balance (the amount transferred each time); U = annual (or monthly) cash disbursement; P = fixed cost per transaction; S = opportunity cost of one rupee p.a. (or p.m.). U and S must be for the same period.

**[formula] What follows from C**
$$\text{Number of transfers} = \frac{U}{C} \qquad \text{Average cash balance} = \frac{C}{2} \qquad \text{Total cost} = \frac{U}{C} \times P \qquad + \frac{C}{2} \times S$$
The first two are how ICAI's suggested answer to November 2022 Q1(a) (old scheme) worked; the interval between transfers is days in the year ÷ number of transfers. At the optimum the two costs are equal.

**[formula] Cash cycle and minimum operating cash (Illustration 11, SM 9.68)**
$$\text{Cash cycle} = \text{Debtors period} \qquad + \text{Inventory period} \qquad - \text{Creditors period}$$

$$\text{Cash turnover} = \frac{360 \text{ days}}{\text{Cash cycle}} \qquad \text{Minimum operating cash} = \frac{\text{Total annual operating outlay}}{\text{Cash turnover}}$$

**[formula] Is a faster collection system worth it? (SM 9.55)**
$$\text{Funds released} = \text{Collections per day} \qquad \times \text{Days of float saved}$$

$$\text{Accept if: } \text{Funds released} \times \text{Rate of return} \qquad > \text{Added cost of the system}$$

**[definition] Treasury management**
Planning, organizing and controlling the funds and working capital of an enterprise in order to ensure best use of funds, maintain liquidity, reduce overall cost of funds and mitigate operational and financial risk. (SM 9.37)

**[definition] Float**
The periods that affect cash as it moves through the different stages of the collection process: billing float, mail float, cheque processing float and banking processing float. (SM 9.53–9.54)

**[definition] Optimum cash balance (Baumol)**
That level of cash where the carrying costs and transactions costs are the minimum. (SM 9.59)

**[trick] Square root, so a quarter gives a half**

### Answer frame

### "Explain briefly the functions of the treasury department" (4 marks)

The five tasks, in ICAI's order, one or two lines each: **cash management** (collections, payments, surplus funds in an investment portfolio), **currency management** (set off intra-group indebtedness, match receipts and payments, forward contracts), **fund management** (plan and source short, medium and long-term needs; temporary investment of surplus; capital structure; forecast rates), **banking** (relationship and negotiations with bankers; bank loans, commercial paper), **corporate finance** (acquisitions and divestments; investor relations).

### "Explain Baumol's model of cash management"

1. Optimum cash level: where carrying costs and transaction costs are the minimum.
2. Carrying cost = opportunity cost (interest foregone on marketable securities); transaction cost = cost of converting securities into cash (clerical, brokerage, registration).
3. Formula $C = \sqrt{2UP/S}$ with every symbol defined.
4. The diagram: holding cost rising, transaction cost falling, total cost U-shaped, optimum at its lowest point.
5. The four assumptions; the limitation (no fluctuation allowed), which Miller-Orr overcomes.
6. "With example": a three-line calculation.

### "Discuss the Miller-Orr cash management model"

1. Net cash flow completely stochastic; a control limit model; decides the time and size of transfers between an investment account and the cash account.
2. Limits: h (upper), z (return point), zero (lower).
3. At h, invest h − z in marketable securities; at the lower limit, transfer from securities to cash; in between, no transactions.
4. Limits set by the fixed cost of securities transactions, the opportunity cost of holding cash and the degree of likely fluctuations.
5. More realistic than Baumol; the finance manager can set the limits to the firm's liquidity requirements.
6. A labelled sketch.

### Short notes

- **Virtual banking**: meaning, the four advantages, the reason for its popularity (speed, convenience, round the clock access).
- **Kinds of float**: the four, one line each, then concentration banking and the lock box as the cures.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the monthly U with the annual S** **Fix:** put U and S on the same period: $\sqrt{2 \times 24{,}00{,}000 \times 150 / 0.12} = \text{₹}77{,}460$ (or the monthly U with a monthly S of 1%, which gives the same).
- **Borrowing to zero instead of to the minimum** **Fix:** finance to the minimum: ₹43 lakh. ICAI's layout (total cash needed = payments + minimum) makes the slip impossible.
- **Depreciation paid in cash** **Fix:** a cash budget has only cash: ₹8 lakh. The same goes for provisions and write-offs.
- **Calling the optimum balance the average balance** **Fix:** holding cost = (C ÷ 2) × S; transaction cost = (U ÷ C) × P.

### Recap

- Treasury management: planning, organising and controlling funds and working capital for best use of funds, liquidity, lower cost of funds and less risk; five tasks: cash, currency, fund management, banking, corporate finance.
- Cash management balances liquidity against cost; Keynes's three needs: transaction, speculative, precautionary.
- Cash budget: the most significant device for planning cash; with a minimum balance, total cash needed = payments + minimum, and the surplus or deficit is invested or financed (ICAI Illustration 6 layout). Only cash items; no depreciation.
- Four floats: billing, mail, cheque processing, banking processing. Concentration banking cuts the time from remittance to spendable funds; a lock box eliminates the cheque processing float but costs per cheque. Accept if the income on released funds exceeds the added cost.
- Baumol: $C = \sqrt{2UP/S}$; transfers U ÷ C; average balance C ÷ 2; at the optimum, holding cost = transaction cost. Assumes certain, uniform cash use.
- Miller-Orr: random flows; limits h, z and zero; invest h − z at the top, sell securities at the bottom, nothing in between.
- Minimum operating cash = annual operating outlay ÷ cash turnover, where cash turnover = 360 ÷ cash cycle.
- Surplus goes to securities chosen for safety, maturity and marketability. (Fill the wallet once a week, not once a year.)

## Lesson 9.5: Management of inventory

*Inventory as money on a shelf: what inventory management covers, the stock-out against over-stock trade-off, the Paper 4 control tools seen through a finance lens, and what cutting stock days does to working capital.*

### Key idea

Inventory is working capital on a shelf. Hold enough to avoid stock-outs and no more, and price every extra rupee and every extra day of stock at the cost of the funds that finance it.

### Formats and formulas

*Reproduce the EOQ formula and the cost comparison layout from memory.*

**[formula] Economic order quantity (CMA Study Material 2.24)**
$$\text{EOQ} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times A \times O}{C}}$$
A = annual requirement (units); O = cost per order; C = carrying cost per unit per annum, which includes the cost of funds invested in the stock.

**[formula] Average stock and the money in it**
$$\text{Average stock} = \frac{Q}{2} + \text{Safety stock}$$

$$\text{Investment} = \text{Average stock} \times \text{Unit cost}$$

Q = order quantity.

**[formula] Total annual cost of a stock policy**
$$\text{Total cost} = \text{Purchase cost} + \frac{A}{Q} \times O \qquad + \left(\frac{Q}{2} + \text{Safety stock}\right) \times C$$
Q = order quantity. Compare policies on this total; the purchase cost matters whenever the policies change the price.

**[formula] Inventory holding periods (SM 9.20)**
$$R = \frac{\text{Average raw material stock}}{\text{Raw material consumed per day}}$$

$$F = \frac{\text{Average finished goods stock}}{\text{Cost of goods sold per day}}$$
In ICAI's words: average stock of raw material ÷ average cost of raw material consumption per day; average stock of finished goods ÷ average cost of goods sold per day; W = average work-in-progress inventory ÷ average cost of production per day.

**[formula] Working capital released by fewer days of stock**
$$\text{Funds released} = \text{Cost per day} \times \text{Days cut}$$

$$\text{Annual saving} = \text{Funds released} \times \text{Carrying rate}$$
Cost per day on the right base: material consumed per day for raw materials, cost of production per day for finished goods.

### Comparison statement for two stock policies

| Particulars | Policy I (₹) | Policy II (₹) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Purchase cost (units × price) | xxx | xxx |
| Ordering cost (number of orders × cost per order) | xxx | xxx |
| Carrying cost (average stock × carrying cost per unit) | xxx | xxx |
| **Total annual cost** | **xxx** | **xxx** |

**[definition] Inventory control (CIMA, as quoted by ICAI)**
The function of ensuring that sufficient goods are retained in stock to meet all requirements without carrying unnecessarily large stocks. (CMA Study Material 2.21)

**[definition] Economic order quantity**
The size of an order for which total of ordering and carrying cost are minimum. (CMA Study Material 2.23)

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the interest that was saved twice** **Fix:** the cost of funds is inside the carrying cost; count it once.
- **The whole lot on the shelf all year** **Fix:** average stock = Q ÷ 2 + safety stock; the investment and the carrying cost follow from it.

### Recap

- Inventories are a major element of working capital; their objectives are similar to those of cash management (SM 9.69).

- EOQ = √(2AO ÷ C); C includes the cost of funds invested in the stock.
- Average stock = Q ÷ 2 + safety stock; money invested = average stock × cost per unit.
- R, W and F are measured on cost bases; shifting stock faster frees cash.
- Compare stock policies on total cost (purchase + ordering + carrying), and count the interest on released funds only once.
- Inventory is ideally financed by supplier credit; banks lend against it as a cash credit. (Buy for the week, not for the wedding season.)

## Lesson 9.6: Management of payables

*Trade credit as a spontaneous source of finance, its hidden costs and the costs of not using it, and how to price a supplier's cash discount: ICAI's simple and compound formulas and the refuse-or-accept comparison.*

### Key idea

Trade credit costs nothing only when no discount is lost. When there is a cash discount, convert it into an annual rate for the extra days of credit and compare it with the cost (or return) of your own funds: take the discount when forgoing it is dearer; otherwise pay on the last day of credit.

### Formats and formulas

*Reproduce both formulas and the comparison statement from memory.*

**[formula] Nominal annual cost of not taking the discount (SM 9.99)**
$$\frac{d}{100 - d} \times \frac{365 \text{ days}}{t}$$
d = size of the discount (for 6%, d = 6); t = days credit outstanding − discount period. Multiply by 100 for a percentage.

**[formula] Cost of lost cash discount, with compounding (SM 9.99)**
$$\left(\frac{100}{100 - d}\right)^{365/t} - 1$$
Always above the nominal cost. Use 360 for 365 when the question says so.

**[formula] Funds needed to take a discount**
$$\text{Funds} = \text{Purchases} \times \left(1 - \frac{d}{100}\right) \times \frac{t}{365}$$
Funds = the average extra funds tied up; Purchases = annual purchases. Paying t days earlier on every invoice ties up t days of discounted purchases. Net benefit = discount earned − cost of these funds.

### The refuse-or-accept statement (ICAI Illustration 17 layout, SM 9.100)

| Particulars | Refuse discount (₹) | Accept discount (₹) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Payment to supplier | invoice value | invoice value × (1 − d%) |
| Less: return from investing the discounted amount between the discount day and the due date | (xxx) | — |
| **Net cost** | **xxx** | **xxx** |

The lower net cost wins. The return is on the discounted amount (₹98 out of ₹100), for t days, at the rate the firm can earn (or saves on borrowing).

**[definition] Trade credit**
A spontaneous source of finance normally extended to the purchaser organisation by the sellers or service providers; credit obtained without completing much formality is open account trade credit. (SM 9.103)

**[definition] t in the discount formulas**
The reduction in the payment period in days, necessary to obtain the early discount, or days credit outstanding − discount period. (SM 9.99)

### Exam angle

**[exam] How payables appeared, May 2024 to September 2026**
In the ICAI documents read for this lesson, payables came up 5 times in 5 new-scheme documents:

- **Numerical.** MTP January 2026 Series II Q2(ii)–(iii), inside a 10-mark working capital question: the implied annual cost of forgoing a 2/15 net 60 discount (compound formula, 360 days) against the firm's return of 20%, then the refuse-or-accept statement for a ₹10,000 invoice. ### "Discuss the costs of availing trade credit" (4 marks)

However, it carries the following costs:" then the four, each with one line: **(i) Price** (forgone discount, a high implicit cost), **(ii) Loss of goodwill** (discrimination against delinquent customers when supplies are short), **(iii) Cost of managing** (administrative and accounting costs), **(iv) Conditions** (minimum order size or regular orders). ### "Show the difference between trade credit and bank overdraft" (RTP September 2025 Q9(b))

| Point | Trade credit | Bank overdraft |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Credit extended by suppliers, allowing the firm to delay payment for goods or services | Customers may withdraw in excess of the credit balance in their current account |
| Duration | Usually 15 to 90 days | Repayable on demand, but generally continued for long periods by annual renewal of limits |
| Cost | A type of interest free loan; failure to use it has an interest cost | Interest charged on daily balances |
| How it arises | Generated automatically in the course of business | Borrower applies to the bank; operated like cash credit and current accounts |
| Size | Grows automatically with the volume of business | A fixed limit is granted within which the borrower may overdraw |

### "Explain the importance of trade credit and accruals, and their cost"

1. Credit from suppliers in the normal course of trade; a short-term source; small firms depend heavily on it.
2. Advantages: easy availability, flexibility, informality.
3. Not cost-free: the supplier's opportunity cost of funds in receivables is passed on as a higher price or as a cash discount not extended; the forgone discount is an implicit cost.
4. Accrued expenses (wages, salaries, taxes paid at the end of the period) are built-in and automatic, with no explicit or implicit cost (SM 9.103–9.104).

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the 2% that was compared with 12%** **Fix:** annualise the discount over t days first, then compare.
- **t taken as the whole credit period** **Fix:** t = days credit outstanding − discount period = 23; the first 7 days are free either way.
- **d ÷ 100 instead of d ÷ (100 − d)** **Fix:** 1.5 ÷ 98.5, not 1.5 ÷ 100; and in the comparison statement, the return is on ₹4,92,500, not on ₹5,00,000.
- **Stretching sold as a saving** **Fix:** a payment beyond the agreed period oversteps the credit; ICAI lists the loss of goodwill and supply discrimination as costs. If the discount is not taken, pay on the last day of credit, not later.

### Recap

- Trade creditor: a spontaneous short-term source arising from ordinary business; slow payment causes ill-feeling, disrupted supplies and a bad image (SM 9.98).
- Costs of availing trade credit: price (forgone discount), loss of goodwill, cost of managing, conditions.
- Costs of not taking trade credit: impact of inflation, interest, inconvenience.
- Nominal cost of not taking the discount = d ÷ (100 − d) × 365 ÷ t; cost of lost cash discount = (100 ÷ (100 − d))^(365/t) − 1; t = credit days − discount days.
- Take the discount when forgoing it costs more than the cost (or return) of your funds; otherwise pay on the last day of credit, not later.
- Refuse-or-accept: payment refused less the return on the discounted amount for t days, against the discounted payment.
- Never compare a period discount with an annual rate. (₹3 to keep ₹97 for six weeks is not cheap money.)

## Lesson 9.7: Financing of working capital and bank credit

*Where working capital money comes from: spontaneous and negotiated sources, commercial paper, ICDs, bills discounting and factoring; bank finance after MPBF; the seven forms of bank credit; and the effective cost of each source.*

### Key idea

Finance permanent working capital from long-term sources and temporary working capital from short-term ones. Among short-term sources, compare the effective cost per rupee you can actually use, and weigh credit rating, feasibility, reliability and restrictions before choosing.

### Formats and formulas

*Reproduce the effective cost formulas and the comparison layout from memory.*

**[formula] Loan with a compensating (minimum) balance**
$$\text{Effective cost} = \frac{\text{Interest rate}}{1 - b}$$
b = the minimum balance as a fraction of the loan, counted only if the firm would not otherwise keep it. SM 9.156: 16.5% ÷ (1 − 0.10) = 18.33%. Amount to borrow for a usable sum S: S ÷ (1 − b).

**[formula] Discounted bill or commercial paper**
$$\text{Cost} = \frac{\text{Discount} + \text{Charges}}{\text{Net proceeds}} \times \frac{365}{n}$$
Cost = effective annual cost; n = days to maturity; net proceeds = face value − discount − charges. Never divide by the face value.

**[formula] Bank finance as a share of net current assets (SM case scenario)**
$$\text{Bank finance} = 75\% \times \text{NCA}$$

$$\text{Margin} = \text{NCA} - \text{Bank finance}$$
NCA (net current assets) = current assets − current liabilities other than the bank borrowing. The percentage is the bank's stipulation in the question.

### Comparison statement of short-term sources

| Source | Effective annual cost |
|---|---|
| Bank loan with minimum balance | Rate ÷ (1 − b) |
| Bills discounting | Discount and charges ÷ net proceeds × 365 ÷ n |
| Commercial paper | (Face − net proceeds) ÷ net proceeds × 365 ÷ n |
| Trade credit (discount forgone) | d ÷ (100 − d) × 365 ÷ t |

Rank the sources, choose the cheapest that is feasible, and comment on rating, reliability and restrictions.

**[definition] Commercial paper**
An unsecured promissory note issued by a firm to raise funds for a short period; an instrument that enables highly rated corporate borrowers to borrow short term. (SM 9.104)

**[definition] Line of credit**
A commitment by a bank to lend a certain amount of funds on demand, specifying the maximum amount. (SM 9.108)

**[definition] Letter of credit**
An arrangement by which the issuing bank, on the instructions of a customer or on its own behalf, undertakes to pay or accept or negotiate, or authorizes another bank to do so, against stipulated documents subject to compliance with specified terms and conditions. (SM 9.108)

**[definition] Bank guarantee**
A facility that commercial banks extend on behalf of their clients in favour of third parties, who are the beneficiaries of the guarantees. (SM 9.108)

### Exam angle

**[exam] How financing of working capital appeared, May 2024 to September 2026**
In the ICAI documents read for this lesson, it was examined 6 times in 6 new-scheme documents:

- **Forms of bank credit.** May 2024 Q4(b) (4 marks): "describe any four forms of bank credit"; the suggested answer listed the seven of SM 9.107–9.108 and added short-term loans from Chapter 2.
- **MPBF and its cost.** RTP May 2024 Q1 (a case, MCQ (v)): the Study Material's case scenario, 75% of net current assets and an annualised cost of 16.5 ÷ 90% = 18.33%.
- **Spontaneous sources.** RTP January 2026 Q11(b): meaning and the three sources. RTP September 2025 Q9(b): trade credit against bank overdraft, five points.
- **Bank finance in other chapters.** May 2026 Q4(b) (4 marks): identify post-shipment finance and explain three forms. RTP May 2025 Q9: a letter of credit cuts the export debtors' period in a working capital estimate.
- **Old scheme, for practice:** May 2022 Q1(b) and RTP May 2023 (Tandon's three methods); RTP December 2021 Q8 (bank loan with a 10% compensating balance, 15 ÷ 90 = 16.67%, against trade credit and factoring); four MTPs on the features of commercial paper.

**[exam] Traps**
- **Usable funds, not loan.** Divide interest by the funds the firm can use; a compensating balance counts only if it would not otherwise be kept.
- **Net proceeds, not face value**, for any discounted instrument.
- **MPBF is withdrawn.** A theory answer that presents Tandon's norms as current RBI rules contradicts the Study Material.
- **ICAI's order** of the seven forms: cash credit, bank overdraft, bills discounting, bills acceptance, line of credit, letter of credit, bank guarantee.

**[trick] Divide by what you can spend**
Any source whose price is deducted up front or whose funds are partly locked away: effective cost = annual cost in rupees ÷ rupees you can actually spend. Condition: compare all sources over the same year basis (365 or 360, as the question says).

### Answer frame

### "Describe the forms of bank credit" (4 marks, any four)

Write each form with ICAI's one-line description, in ICAI's order: cash credit (continuous facility within a sanctioned limit); bank overdraft (short-term, urgent needs, within limits, callable at short notice); bills discounting (seller discounts the accepted bill; discounting limit); bills acceptance (company draws on the bank, which accepts it); line of credit (commitment to lend on demand up to a maximum); letter of credit (bank undertakes to pay against stipulated documents); bank guarantee (on behalf of the client in favour of a third-party beneficiary). Four well-explained forms earn the marks; ICAI's answer also accepted short-term loans.

### "Explain commercial paper: its advantages and limitations"

1. Definition: an unsecured promissory note for a short period; for highly rated corporate borrowers; freely negotiable interest rate.
2. Maturity 7 days to less than 1 year; denominations of ₹5 lakh or multiples.
3. Advantages (a) to (e) in ICAI's order: unsecured, no restrictive conditions; rollover; tailored maturity; available in a tight money market; cheaper than bank loans.
4. Limitations: only highly rated firms; neither redeemable before nor extendable beyond maturity.

### "Assessment of working capital by banks after 1997"

The four points of SM 9.106–9.107 in order: MPBF withdrawn (April 1997); banks' own Board-approved methods within prudential guidelines and exposure norms; RBI's directed credit and prohibition of credit still apply; MPBF instructions withdrawn and lending-discipline guidelines optional.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the 11% loan that cost 11%** **Fix:** 11% ÷ 0.85 = 12.94%, and borrow ₹2,35,29,412 to have ₹2 crore to use.
- **Discount divided by the face value** **Fix:** divide by the net proceeds.
- **MPBF written as today's rule** **Fix:** RBI withdrew MPBF in April 1997; banks use their own Board-approved methods. Use a percentage only when the question stipulates it.
- **The CP that was repaid early** **Fix:** CP can neither be redeemed before maturity nor extended beyond it; plan the maturity to the need, with a bank limit as standby.

### Recap

- Permanent working capital comes from long-term sources (debt, equity), temporary working capital from short-term sources (SM 9.102).
- Spontaneous sources arise naturally (trade credit, bills payable, accrued expenses); negotiated sources are arranged with lenders.
- Choice of source: cost, impact on credit rating, feasibility, reliability, restrictions, matching of maturities.
- Commercial paper: unsecured promissory note, highly rated firms, 7 days to less than 1 year, ₹5 lakh or multiples; cheaper than bank loans; cannot be redeemed early or extended.
- Tandon and Chore Committees set the old norms; RBI withdrew MPBF in April 1997; banks now use Board-approved methods within prudential guidelines and exposure norms.
- Seven forms of bank credit: cash credit, overdraft, bills discounting, bills acceptance, line of credit, letter of credit, bank guarantee.
- Effective cost = annual cost ÷ usable funds: a loan with a minimum balance costs rate ÷ (1 − balance %); a discounted bill or CP costs its charge ÷ net proceeds, annualised. (Four doors, four rents: read the small print.)
