## Lesson 1.1: Objectives, scope and users of cost and management accounting

*What cost and management accounting is for (five objectives), the seven functions that make up its scope, and who uses its information inside and outside the entity.*

### Key idea

Cost and management accounting exists to ascertain the cost of each cost object and to use it for pricing, cost control, cost reduction and decisions. It serves users inside the entity (who manage it) and outside it (who regulate, audit, own or lend to it).

### Exam angle

**[exam] How ICAI has examined this lesson**
In the ICAI documents read for this lesson (new-scheme papers, RTPs and MTPs from May 2024 to September 2026, and some old-scheme ones), the topic was asked in three of them, and the chapter asks it once more:

- **Users, 5 marks**: "Give two examples of internal users and three examples of external users and explain how they are concerned" (MTP Series I for January 2025 Q6(a)). The suggested answer reproduces the Study Material's list of all eight users with their uses, word for word.
- **Scope, 4 marks**: four descriptions to match with "the most relevant scope of Cost Accounting" (May 2025 Q6(c), an OR option); the answers were cost control, cost reports, cost comparison and cost analysis, matched on the Study Material's own phrases ("detailed examination of each cost", "alternative courses of action").
- **Objectives**: "Narrate the objectives of cost accounting" (RTP November 2023, old scheme, Q14(b)), answered in five points; and the chapter's own question, "Describe the main objectives of introduction of a Cost and Management Accounting System in a manufacturing organization" (SM 1.45).
- No question in the papers read asked for the six definitions alone, but they are the vocabulary of every theory answer in the paper.

**[exam] The wording traps**
- "Explain how they are concerned": a list of names earns little; each user needs its use (tariff determination, subsidies and rate fixation for a regulator; net debt position and stock balances for a lender).
- A scope question matches on the Study Material's phrases. "Factors responsible for variance … fixation of responsibility" is cost analysis, not cost control; "detailed examination of each cost in the light of advantage received" is cost control.
- The old-scheme answer to "objectives" (RTP November 2023) combines cost control with cost reduction and adds "ascertainment of profit of each activity". For January 2027, write the current Study Material's five, in its order.

**[trick] Seven verbs for the scope**
Ritu's week runs in ICAI's order: she **costs** it, **accounts** for it, **analyses** it, **compares** it, **controls** it, **reports** it and **complies** with the statute: Costing, Cost Accounting, Cost Analysis, Cost Comparisons, Cost Control, Cost Reports, Statutory Compliances (SM 1.7). It lists ICAI's seven functions only; it does not mean they happen one after another.

### Answer frame

### "Describe the main objectives of a cost and management accounting system"

1. **Ascertainment of cost**: accumulated, assigned and ascertained for each cost object (unit, job, operation, process, department, service).
2. **Determination of selling price and profitability**: a basis for price fixation and rate negotiation, even where prices are set by external factors.
3. **Cost control**: discipline in expenditure, in four steps: pre-determined standard; measurement of actual performance; comparison; analysis of variance and action.
4. **Cost reduction**: the definition ("real and permanent reduction in the unit cost … without impairing their suitability for the use intended or diminution in the quality"), value added and non-value added activities, continuous research.
5. **Assisting management in decision making**: relevant information for planning, implementing, measuring, controlling and evaluating.

In a case ("in a manufacturing organisation"), add one line of the case's facts under each heading.

### "Give examples of internal and external users and explain how they are concerned" (5 marks)

| Part of the answer | What earns the mark |
|---|---|
| Opening line | The information is used by different stakeholders, broadly categorised into internal and external users |
| Each internal user | Its name as ICAI gives it (policy makers, managers, operational level staff, employees) and its uses |
| Each external user | Its name (regulatory authorities, auditors, shareholders, creditors and lenders) and what it is concerned with |
| Number asked | Exactly as many of each as the question asks, the strongest ones first |

### Three answers to "Explain two external users"

**[cols] **Loses marks****

"Auditors and banks."

Names only, and "banks" is not ICAI's term.
---
**Passes**

"Auditors use cost data for audit. Creditors and lenders use it to give loans."

Right users, thin uses.
---
**Full marks**

"Auditors need costing information and reports reviewed by management, for a financial or cost audit. Creditors and lenders need regular reports on the net debt position and stock balances."

ICAI's names, each with its concern.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: cost reduction that only kept costs on budget** **Fix:** cost reduction is a *real and permanent* reduction in the *unit* cost without impairing suitability or quality; it challenges the standards themselves. Test it against the three-fold assumption: a saving in unit cost, a permanent saving, quality unaffected if not improved.
- **The scope that lost two functions** **Fix:** seven functions, and the Study Material calls **cost reports** the ultimate function of cost accounting (SM 1.8). Use the seven verbs.
- **Auditors inside, employees outside** **Fix:** ICAI's internal users are policy makers, managers, operational level staff and employees; the external users are regulatory authorities, auditors, shareholders, and creditors and lenders (SM 1.12).

### Recap

- Five objectives, in ICAI's order: ascertainment of cost, determination of selling price and profitability, cost control, cost reduction, and assisting management in decision making.
- Scope, seven functions: costing, cost accounting, cost analysis, cost comparisons, cost control, cost reports (the ultimate function) and statutory compliances.
- Internal users: policy makers, managers, operational level staff, employees. External users: regulatory authorities, auditors, shareholders, creditors and lenders.
- Cost control: pre-determined standard → measure actual (same basis) → compare and report → analyse variance and act (amend standards if needed).
- Cost reduction: real and permanent reduction in unit cost without impairing suitability or quality; three-fold assumption: saving in unit cost, permanent, quality unaffected if not improved.
- Costing: "the technique and process of ascertaining costs". Cost, as a noun: expenditure (actual or notional) incurred on or attributable to a specified article, product or activity.
- Cost leadership needs a robust cost and management accounting system. (No navigation app, no shortcut.)

## Lesson 1.2: Cost accounting department and installing a costing system

*What the cost and management accounting department does, the nine factors to study before installing a costing system, the six features of a good one, its limitations, and the practical difficulties of installation.*

### Key idea

Study the business before installing the system, build the system so that people can and do use it, and remember that it must be worth its cost. A costing system that management does not trust produces reports that nobody reads.

### Exam angle

**[exam] Where installation questions appeared**
In the ICAI documents read for this lesson (new-scheme papers, RTPs and MTPs, May 2024 to September 2026, and some old-scheme ones):

- **Essential features of a good cost accounting system, 5 marks**: MTP Series II for May 2024 Q6(b) and MTP Series I for September 2026 Q6(b); in the old scheme, May 2022 Q6(a), MTP November 2019 Q6(a) and MTP Series II for November 2022 Q6(a). The chapter asks it too (SM 1.45, Q4).
- **Factors before installing, 5 marks**: "Enumerate the factors which are to be considered before installing a system of cost accounting in a manufacturing organization" (MTP Series II for May 2025 Q6(a)); "As a consultant … how would you go about assessing the critical factors for designing and implementing a cost accounting system?" (MTP Series II for January 2025 Q6(a)). Both answers are the nine factors, in the Study Material's order, with its examples (the oil refinery, the lender's stock statement).
- **Practical difficulties and how to overcome them, 5 marks**: MTP Series II for September 2024 Q6(a); RTP September 2026 Q16(a).
- **Limitations of cost accounting**: RTP December 2021 (old scheme) Q15(b), answered with the three limitations above.
- No question in the papers read asked for the department's functions alone.

**[exam] Two lists for "essential features"**
The suggested answers of the two newest MTPs (May 2024 Series II, September 2026 Series I) list: informative and simple; accuracy; support from management and subordinates; cost-benefit ("the cost of installing and operating the system should justify the results"); procedure (a carefully phased programme, prepared using network analysis); trust. The Study Material and the old-scheme May 2022 answer list the six in this lesson. Write the Study Material's six; support from management and cost-benefit may be added as further points if time allows.

**[trick] The nine factors as four questions**
Why (objective) → where (nature of business, organisational hierarchy) → what and how (product, production process) → fitting in (information synchronisation, maintenance of cost records, statutory compliances and audit, information attributes). Four questions, nine factors, in ICAI's order (a) to (i). A memory aid only: the Study Material does not group them.

### Answer frame

### "Discuss the essential features of a good cost accounting system" (5 marks)

Six headings, each with one line of ICAI's explanation: informative and simple (tailor-made, no unnecessary details); accurate and authentic; uniformity and consistency (benchmarking, comparability); integrated and inclusive (financial accounting, taxation, statistics, operational research); flexible and adaptive (technological, reporting, regulatory changes); trust on the system (active role of management).

### "Factors to be considered before installing a costing system" (5 marks)

| Factor | The line that earns the mark |
|---|---|
| (a) Objective | Fixing prices or establishing cost control |
| (b) Nature of business or industry | Methods follow the industry's needs (refinery: process-wise accounts) |
| (c) Organisational hierarchy | Top: corporate strategy; strategic: pricing, diversification; operational: standard quantities, idle time |
| (d) Knowing the product | By-products; perishables: marginal costing |
| (e) Knowing the production process | Scientific apportionment needs knowledge of the process |
| (f) Information synchronisation | Other departments' needs (stock statement to the bank, tax returns) |
| (g) Method of maintenance of cost records | Inter-locking or reconciliation through control accounts |
| (h) Statutory compliances and audit | Statutory records, cost accounting standards |
| (i) Information attributes | Complete, accurate, timely, relevant |

In a case ("as a consultant"), apply each factor to the facts in one more line.

### Three answers to "practical difficulties and how to overcome them"

**[cols] **Loses marks****

"Staff do not like change, so train them."

One vague difficulty, one remedy.
---
**Passes**

The four difficulties named, with no remedies, or remedies with no difficulties.

Half the question answered.
---
**Full marks**

The four difficulties, each with its reason, then the four remedies: sell the idea, behavioural approach, training, regular meetings.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: "Install it first, study the business later"** **Fix:** factors (nine) come before installation: objective, nature of business, hierarchy, product, process, synchronisation, cost records, statutory compliances and audit, information attributes. Essential features (six) are qualities of the finished system.
- **Marginal costing for everything** **Fix:** ICAI's point is specific: products with by-products need by-product accounting; for perishable or short shelf-life products, marginal costing is appropriate, to know the contribution and minimum selling price.
- **Limitations that are not ICAI's** **Fix:** the Study Material's three are expensive, requirement of reconciliation, and duplication of work (SM 1.24).

### Recap

- Nine factors to study before installing: objective, nature of business or industry, organisational hierarchy, knowing the product, knowing the production process, information synchronisation, method of maintenance of cost records, statutory compliances and audit, and information attributes.
- Six essential features of a good system: informative and simple, accurate and authentic, uniformity and consistency, integrated and inclusive, flexible and adaptive, and trust on the system.
- Three limitations of cost accounting: it is expensive, it requires reconciliation with the financial accounts, and it duplicates work. So the first question is whether the system will be profitable to have.
- Five functions: collect cost by element; assign to cost objects; set budgets and standards, compare, report; information for decisions (MIS, the main function); evaluate responsibility centres.
- Perishables: marginal costing (contribution, minimum price). By-products: a system that accounts for them.
- Practical difficulties (ICAI's RTP and MTP answers): no top management support, staff resistance, user departments' non-co-operation, shortage of trained staff; remedies: sell the idea, behavioural approach, training, regular meetings.
- A system nobody trusts is a 46-page report nobody reads.

## Lesson 1.3: Cost, financial and management accounting, and financial management

*How cost accounting differs from financial accounting (seven bases) and from management accounting (six bases), and how all three relate to financial management.*

### Key idea

Financial accounting reports the financial performance of the entity, as a whole or by segment, to outsiders, from historical data, usually yearly, in a set format. Cost accounting ascertains the cost of each cost object for cost control and decisions, from historical and predetermined costs, as and when required. Management accounting is wider and forward-looking; all three serve financial management's decisions.

### Exam angle

**[exam] How ICAI examines these differences**
In the ICAI documents read for this lesson (new-scheme papers, RTPs and MTPs, May 2024 to September 2026, and old-scheme papers back to 2019):

- **Cost accounting vs management accounting** was asked in six: RTP September 2025 Q18(a) ("Though Cost Accounting and Management Accounting are used synonymously, but there are a few differences. Enumerate any five differences"); and in the old scheme, November 2020 Q6(a) (5 marks), RTP May 2020 Q13(a), MTP December 2021 Series I Q6(b), RTP November 2022 Q15(c) and MTP May 2023 Series I Q6(b). The suggested answers give the Study Material's six-row table, in the same order and nearly word for word.
- **Financial accounting vs cost accounting** and the link with **financial management** were not asked directly in the documents read; financial and cost accounts appear together in the reconciliation numericals instead. The tables in this lesson are the Study Material's own, so they are examinable as they stand.

**[trick] Two sentences, two tables**
Cost vs management accounting, six bases in ICAI's order: "**N**o **O**ther **A**ccountant **R**eads **D**ev's **R**ules": Nature, Objective, Area, Recording of data, Development, Rules and regulation.

Financial vs cost accounting, seven bases in ICAI's order: "**O**nly **N**ew **R**ecruits **U**se **A**ny **T**emplate **P**ossible": Objective, Nature, Recording of data, Users of information, Analysis of cost and profit, Time period, Presentation of information. (Cost reports follow no set format: any template is possible.)

### Answer frame

### "Distinguish between cost accounting and management accounting" (4–5 marks)

A three-column table, Basis | Cost Accounting | Management Accounting, with the six bases in ICAI's order: nature, objective, area, recording of data, development, rules and regulation. "Any five" means the first five done well; a sixth row costs little and covers a weak one.

### "Distinguish between financial accounting and cost accounting"

The same table with seven bases: objective, nature, recording of data, users of information, analysis of cost and profit, time period, presentation of information. Write the key words examiners look for: "financial performance of an entity" against "cost control and decision making"; "historical" against "historical and pre-determined"; "segment-wise or as a whole" against "each cost object"; "usually for a year" against "as and when required"; "set format" against "no set formats".

### "Explain the relationship of cost and management accounting with other disciplines"

1. Cost accounting provides inputs to financial accounting: cost of goods sold and inventories.
2. Management accounting takes inputs from cost accounts, financial accounts, statistical and operation management tools.
3. Cost and management accounting is an application of financial management for decision making purposes.
4. A labelled sketch: three overlapping circles (financial, management and cost accounting) on financial management.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: "Management accounting is cost accounting with a bigger salary"** **Fix:** management accounting records qualitative as well as quantitative information, is wider in area (financial accounting, budgeting, taxation, planning), projects the future and follows no specific rules. Six bases, and none of them is "users" or "time period".
- **Predetermined costs in the financial accounts** **Fix:** financial accounting records historical data; cost accounting uses both historical and pre-determined costs (standards and budgets are part of it).
- **The yearly cost report in a statutory format** **Fix:** cost reports and statements are prepared as and when required, in general with no set format, generally for internal management (sometimes for regulatory authorities also).

### Recap

- Cost accounting provides inputs to financial accounting (costs of goods sold and inventories); management accounting takes inputs from cost accounts, financial accounts, statistical and operation management tools.
- Cost vs management accounting, six bases: nature (quantitative / both), objective (cost of product or service / planning and co-ordination), area (cost ascertainment / wider), recording (past and present / future), development (industrial revolution / modern business), rules (follows / none).
- Financial vs cost accounting, seven bases: objective, nature, recording of data (historical / historical and pre-determined), users (outsiders / internal management), analysis (segment or whole / each cost object), time period (usually a year / as and when required), presentation (set format / no set format).
- Cost and management accounting is an application of financial management for decision making purposes.
- A segment profit can hide a loss-making cost object: only the cost records show it. (The cafés' ₹26,40,000 hid Bhopal's loss of ₹8,40,000.)

## Lesson 1.4: Cost units, cost centres, cost objects and responsibility centres

*The cost object (what is costed), the cost unit (per what), the cost driver (what causes the cost), cost centres, and the four responsibility centres with how each is judged.*

### Key idea

The cost object is what you cost, the cost unit is what you cost it per, and the cost driver is what makes the cost rise. Responsibility centres say who answers for what: costs (cost centre), revenue (revenue centre), both (profit centre), or profit plus capital investment (investment centre).

### Exam angle

**[exam] Where these terms appeared, May 2024 to September 2026**
In the ICAI documents read for this lesson:

- **Responsibility centres, 5 marks each**: "Define Responsibility Centre and discuss the types" (September 2025 Q6(a)); "Explain the types of responsibility centres" (MTP Series I for September 2024 Q6(a), with standard and discretionary cost centres in the answer); "Explain responsibility centre and its types in brief" (MTP Series I for September 2025 Q6(a)).
- **Responsibility centres as a case, 5 marks**: identify the type for four departments, with reasons (MTP Series II for January 2026 Q3(b): standard cost centre, discretionary cost centre, revenue centre, investment centre); specify the type for five units (MTP Series I for May 2026 Q6(a): decentralised branches, a Central Government PSU, an e-commerce customer support department, a hotel's restaurant, an airline's reservation department).
- **Standard and discretionary cost centres**: RTP September 2024 Q19(d); in the old scheme, MTP July 2021 Series I Q6(d) and MTP November 2023 Series II Q6(b).
- **Cost units, 4–5 marks**: "State cost unit of the following industry sector" for ten sectors (January 2025 Q6(b); the same list in MTP Series II for May 2026 Q6(a)); eight industries (MTP Series I for January 2026 Q6(c), an OR option); "Describe cost unit and give examples" (RTP September 2025 Q18(d)); in the old scheme, RTP November 2022 Q15(d) and MTP May 2023 Series I Q6(d).
- **Cost objects, 5 marks**: "Define cost objects and give examples of any four cost objects" (May 2023 Q6(a), old scheme), answered with the definition and ICAI's examples table.

**[exam] What the answers show**
- The suggested answers on responsibility centres open with the Study Material's paragraph ("As the organization grows …") before the four types. Write the definition first.
- In the case versions, each answer names the type and then gives the reason in the Study Material's words: "output is measurable", "input-output ratio cannot be defined", "authority to make capital investment decisions", "evaluated on ROI".
- The cost unit answers accept more than the Study Material's tables (January 2025: "per course, per student, per batch, per lecture" for education; "guest days or room days, per item, per meal" for hotels), but the tables are the safe core.
- The chapter's own question "Discuss the different cost centres that an organization can have" is answered by "Please refer paragraph 11": the responsibility centres (SM 1.45–1.46).

**[trick] C, R, P, I: what the manager answers for**
**C**ost centre: costs. **R**evenue centre: revenue. **P**rofit centre: both, so profit. **I**nvestment centre: profit plus capital investment decisions, so ROI. Classify on what the manager is accountable for and has authority over, not on what the department happens to touch: a sales desk that pays commission is still a revenue centre.

### Answer frame

### "Define responsibility centre and discuss its types" (5 marks)

1. The definition: management delegates responsibility and authority to departments or persons, who are held responsible for performance in terms of expenditure, revenue, profitability and return on investment, measured against set standards.
2. **Cost centres**: accountable for costs under their control, measured against standards or budgets; (a) standard cost centre (measurable output, identifiable input-output ratio, responsible for adverse variances); (b) discretionary cost centre (output not measurable in financial terms; inputs compared with the allocated budget; R&D, advertisement).
3. **Revenue centres**: accountable for revenue (sales department); may incur selling expenditure such as commission.
4. **Profit centres**: revenue and expenditure; judged on profitability (decentralised branches).
5. **Investment centres**: profitability plus authority over capital investment; judged on ROI besides profit (Maharatna, Navratna, Miniratna PSUs).

### "Identify the responsibility centre, with reasons" (case)

| Step | What to write |
|---|---|
| 1 | The type, in ICAI's name (standard cost centre, not "a production centre") |
| 2 | The fact in the case that decides it ("can buy trucks within its own capital budget") |
| 3 | The Study Material's test it meets ("authority to make capital investment decisions; measured on ROI") |
| 4 | Where a near-miss is likely, why not the next type ("not an investment centre: capital spending needs head-office approval") |

### "State the cost unit"

A two-column table, industry and cost unit, in the order the question lists them, using the Study Material's units; for a service, a composite unit where the service depends on two measures (tonne-km, passenger-km, patient day).

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the cost unit that was a building** **Fix:** the cost unit is the unit of product, service or time in which cost is expressed: for a hotel, room or meal (the CIMA list in the Study Material; ICAI's January 2025 answer also accepts guest days or room days). Ask "per what?" and the answer is the cost unit.
- **Every branch an investment centre** **Fix:** a branch whose manager answers for revenue and costs is a profit centre ("decentralised branches", SM 1.23). Only the authority to make capital investment decisions, with performance measured on ROI, makes it an investment centre.
- **The R&D lab on a standard cost** **Fix:** where output cannot be measured in financial terms, no input-output ratio exists: it is a discretionary cost centre, judged against its allocated budget.
- **Cost driver or cost unit?** **Fix:** the cost driver is the factor that causes the cost (number of purchase orders for procurement cost); the cost unit is the measure the cost is expressed in (tonne-km for transport).

### Recap

- Cost object: anything for which a separate measurement of cost is required (product, service, project, customer, brand category, activity, department, programme); the nucleus of cost classification.
- Cost unit: a unit of product, service or time (or a combination) in which costs are ascertained or expressed; usually a physical unit (number, weight, area, volume, length, time, value); an order, contract or batch can be one.
- Responsibility centres answer for expenditure, revenue, profitability or return on investment: cost centres (costs under their control), revenue centres (revenue), profit centres (revenue and expenditure, judged on profitability) and investment centres (profit plus capital investment decisions, judged on ROI besides profit).
- Standard cost centre: measurable output, identifiable input-output ratio. Discretionary cost centre: output not measurable in financial terms, inputs compared with the allocated budget (R&D, advertisement).
- Cost driver: a factor influencing the level of cost (number of set-ups, purchase orders, inspection hours, tests).
- Cost units to know: power and electricity kWh; steel ton; transport passenger-km (tonne-km for goods); brick-making 1,000 bricks; brewing barrel; hospitals patient day; education course, enrolled or successful student; credit control accounts maintained.
- Profit centre example: decentralised branches. Investment centre example: Maharatna, Navratna and Miniratna PSUs. (Papa buys the banquet hall; Mama-ji only stays within budget.)

## Lesson 1.5: Elements and classification of cost

*Six ways ICAI sorts the same rupee of cost (element, function, behaviour, controllability, normality and managerial decision making), with the elements of cost and eighteen decision-making cost terms.*

### Key idea

Classification is grouping costs by a common characteristic. ICAI uses six bases, and every cost sits in all six at once: the one you use depends on the question being asked and on the cost object (a box, a café, a department) you are costing.

### Formats and formulas

### From a list of costs to a cost statement

ICAI questions hand you a jumbled list and ask you to classify it, then total it. File each item first by element (direct or indirect), then by function. The statement below follows the order of ICAI's specimen cost sheet (SM 6.8) for a month with no opening or closing stocks. *Reproduce this layout from memory.*

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---:|
| Direct material | xxx |
| Direct employee (labour) cost | xxx |
| Direct expenses | xxx |
| **Prime cost** | **xxx** |
| Add: Works or factory overheads | xxx |
| **Works or factory cost** | **xxx** |
| Add: Administration overheads related to production | xxx |
| **Cost of production (= cost of goods sold)** | **xxx** |
| Add: General administration overheads | xxx |
| Add: Selling overheads | xxx |
| Add: Distribution overheads | xxx |
| **Cost of sales** | **xxx** |

With stocks, quality control, research and development, recoveries and primary packing, the full format is in [[cma.sheet.mfg|the cost sheet for a manufacturer]]. The Chapter 1 chart (SM 1.27) adds all administration overheads before cost of goods sold; cost of sales comes out the same, but in a cost sheet answer keep general administration after cost of goods sold, as SM 6.7–6.8 does.

**[formula] The element identities**
$$\text{Prime cost} = \text{Direct material} \qquad + \text{Direct employee cost} \qquad + \text{Direct expenses}$$
$$\text{Overheads} = \text{Indirect material} \qquad + \text{Indirect employee cost} \qquad + \text{Indirect expenses}$$
Total cost = prime cost + overheads, whichever way the overheads are grouped (SM 1.25–1.26).

**[formula] The functional build-up (SM 1.27)**
$$\text{Works cost} = \text{Prime cost} \qquad + \text{Factory overheads} \qquad \text{Cost of sales} = \text{Works cost} \qquad + \text{Administration} \qquad + \text{S\&D overheads}$$
S&D = selling and distribution.

**[definition] Direct materials**
Materials which are present in the finished product (cost object) or can be economically identified in the product. A direct material used in small quantities, where identifying the quantity is not economically feasible, is treated as indirect; materials used for purposes ancillary to the business are indirect materials. (SM 1.25)

**[definition] Direct labour**
Labour which can be economically identified or attributed wholly to a cost object: employees engaged on the actual production of the product or in carrying out the operations converting raw materials into finished product. (SM 1.26)

**[definition] Overheads**
The aggregate of indirect material costs, indirect labour costs and indirect expenses, subdivided into production or works, administration, selling and distribution overheads. (SM 1.26)

### Exam angle

**[exam] Where classification appeared, May 2024 to September 2026 (and old-scheme papers)**
In the ICAI documents read for this lesson, classification of cost was examined in 12 questions in 12 documents.

- **Classify a list, 5 marks.** January 2026 Q6(a): five items by function and variability (consumable stores, General Manager's salary, delivery van expenses, a salesman's fixed salary plus commission, rent of finished goods warehouse). September 2026 Q6(a): ten items "under appropriate functions of cost" (blueprint cost, hire charges of factory machinery for a product, one-time software licence for a particular job, overtime at a customer's request, specialised packing, royalty); suggested answers not yet published.
- **Decision-making costs.** RTP May 2025 Q1: five MCQs on one case (avoidable cost, sunk and shut-down costs, out-of-pocket cost, unexpired cost). RTP January 2026 Q19(a): "DISCUSS any five types of costs categorised based on its use in Managerial Decision Making". RTP May 2026 Q17(a): how seven of them (pre-determined, differential, product, out-of-pocket, shut-down, sunk, implicit) influence decisions in a scenario.
- **Controllability, 4 marks.** MTP May 2024 Series I Q6(c): "DIFFERENTIATE" controllable and uncontrollable costs. Old scheme: "EXPLAIN the difference between controllable & uncontrollable costs" in MTP May 2023 Series II Q6(a), MTP November 2023 Series I Q6(a) and RTP May 2022 Q14(a) (5 marks each in the MTPs); MTP December 2021 Series II Q6(b) and RTP July 2021 Q13(c), "DISCUSS cost classification based on variability and controllability".
- **The examiner's complaint.** July 2021 (old scheme) Q2(a): examinees "were confused about the treatment of some items like cost of special drawing, hire charges paid for plant, carriage on return and general overheads" in a cost sheet.

**[exam] The traps ICAI sets**
- **Direct costs hide in plain sight**: royalty for production, hire charges of equipment for a specific product or job, product-specific design or software are direct expenses (SM 6.4); overtime premium paid because a customer wants the job urgently is charged to that job directly (SM 3.18). They go in prime cost, not overheads.
- **Two answers can both be right on behaviour.** For delivery van expenses ICAI's January 2026 answer accepts "Semi-variable/Variable", and for a salesman paid a fixed salary plus commission, "Semi-variable". State your reading in a word.
- **Function words**: "Selling & Distribution/Distribution" was accepted for the warehouse rent. Use ICAI's five functions, not "miscellaneous".

**[trick] Element first, then function**
For each item ask two questions in order: (1) "Can it be traced economically to the cost object?" Yes: direct material, labour or expense, part of prime cost. No: overhead. (2) "For which function was it spent?" Factory, office, selling or delivery. The answer to (1) is about the cost object, so read the question for what the cost object is.

### Answer frame

### "Discuss any five types of costs used in managerial decision making"

Write each as the term, ICAI's meaning, and one example. The five most useful, in ICAI's order: **differential cost** (change in total cost due to a change in activity level, technology, process or method), **opportunity cost** (value of the sacrifice or benefit of the opportunity forgone), **out-of-pocket cost** (portion of total cost involving cash outflow; avoidable if the proposal is not accepted), **shut down cost** (costs that continue while a plant is temporarily shut down, such as rent, rates and depreciation) and **sunk cost** (historical cost that plays no role in current decisions, such as the written-down value of a machine being replaced) (SM 1.34–1.35).

### "Differentiate controllable and uncontrollable costs"

| Point | Controllable cost | Uncontrollable cost |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Can be influenced by the manager heading the responsibility centre | Cannot be influenced by the action of a specified member |
| Usual examples | Direct material, direct labour, direct expenses, some overheads | Share of a service department's cost apportioned to another department |
| Responsibility | The centre's manager is held accountable for it, as a cost centre is for costs under its control (SM 1.22) | The specified member cannot influence it, so it is not used to judge that member |
| Key point | No cost is uncontrollable for everyone; the classification is in relation to a particular individual (SM 1.33) | |

### "Discuss cost classification based on variability and controllability"

1. Variability: fixed, variable and semi-variable costs, each with ICAI's meaning and an example (SM 1.27–1.28).
2. Controllability: controllable and uncontrollable costs, with ICAI's meaning and the tool-room example (SM 1.33).
3. A closing line: the distinction is sometimes left to judgment; no cost is uncontrollable, only in relation to a particular individual.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the royalty that ended up in overheads** **Fix:** expenses that can be traced to the cost object in an economically feasible manner are direct expenses and belong in prime cost (SM 6.4).
- **The written-down value that sneaked into the decision** **Fix:** it is a sunk cost, already incurred, and plays no role in the decision; only future costs that differ between the choices count, here the ₹1,50,000 scrap value.
- **"Uncontrollable" stamped on a cost for everyone** **Fix:** controllability is always in relation to a particular individual; the decision to buy the machine was controllable by someone (SM 1.33).

### Recap

- Classification = grouping costs by common characteristics; six bases in ICAI's order: element, function, behaviour, controllability, normality, managerial decision making (SM 1.24).
- Elements: material, labour, other expenses (each direct or indirect) and overheads; overheads = indirect material + indirect labour + indirect expenses.
- Direct means economically traceable to the cost object; tiny quantities and ancillary materials are indirect even if physically present.
- Prime cost = direct material + direct labour + direct expenses; + factory overheads = works cost; with no stocks, works cost = cost of goods sold; + general administration, selling and distribution overheads = cost of sales (SM 6.8).
- Functions: production, administration, selling, distribution, research and development.
- Controllability is always in relation to a particular individual; apportioned costs are uncontrollable for the receiving manager.
- Abnormal cost is charged to Costing Profit and Loss Account.
- Decisions: sunk costs are ignored; opportunity cost = benefit forgone; out-of-pocket = cash outflow, avoidable; shut-down costs continue while closed. (Same rupee, different list.)

## Lesson 1.6: Cost behaviour: fixed, variable, semi-variable and step costs

*How each kind of cost moves when output moves, why fixed cost per unit falls, and ICAI's five methods of splitting a semi-variable cost into its fixed and variable parts.*

### Key idea

Behaviour describes **totals** against activity within a range: fixed totals stay put, variable totals move in proportion, semi-variable totals do both, step totals stay flat and then jump. **Per unit**, the picture flips: fixed cost per unit falls as output rises, variable cost per unit stays the same.

### Formats and formulas

*Reproduce the high-low layout and these formulas from memory.*

| High-low working | Activity | Total cost (₹) |
|---|---:|---:|
| At the highest volume | xxx | xxx |
| At the lowest volume | xxx | xxx |
| **Difference** | **xxx** | **xxx** |

Variable cost rate = difference in cost ÷ difference in activity; fixed cost = total cost at either level − variable rate × that level's activity. Check that both levels give the same fixed cost.

**[formula] Total cost within the relevant range**
$$\text{Total cost} = \text{Fixed cost} \qquad + \text{Variable cost per unit} \times \text{Output}$$
Valid only "within certain output and turnover limits"; add any step or additional fixed cost separately.

**[formula] High-low method**
$$\text{Variable cost rate} \qquad = \frac{\text{Cost at highest} - \text{cost at lowest}}{\text{Highest volume} - \text{lowest volume}}$$
Cost at highest and cost at lowest = total cost at the highest and at the lowest volume. The Study Material states it with sales value as the volume, so the rate is a percentage of sales (SM 1.30); with units or hours as the volume, the rate is per unit or per hour.

**[formula] Comparison by period or level of activity**
$$\text{Variable cost per unit} \qquad = \frac{\text{Change in amount of expense}}{\text{Change in quantity of output}}$$
Fixed cost = expense at either level − variable cost per unit × output at that level (SM 1.31–1.32).

**[formula] Least squares method**
$$y = mx + c$$
y = total cost, x = volume of output, m = variable element of cost per unit, c = total fixed cost (SM 1.32).

**[formula] Analytical method**
$$\text{Variable part} \qquad = \text{Degree of variability} \qquad \times \text{Semi-variable expense} \qquad \text{New total} = \text{Fixed part} \qquad + \text{Variable part} \times \frac{\text{New output}}{\text{Old output}}$$

**[formula] Step cost**
$$\text{Number of steps} \qquad = \frac{\text{Activity}}{\text{Activity per step}}, \text{ rounded up} \qquad \text{Step cost} = \text{Number of steps} \qquad \times \text{Cost of one step}$$

**[definition] Fixed costs**
Costs which are incurred for a period, and which, within certain output and turnover limits, tend to be unaffected by fluctuations in the levels of activity (output or turnover). They do not tend to increase or decrease with the changes in output. (SM 1.27)

**[definition] Variable costs**
Costs which tend to vary with the volume of activity. Any increase in the activity results in an increase in the variable cost and vice-versa. (SM 1.28)

**[definition] Semi-variable costs**
Costs which contain both fixed and variable components and are thus partly affected by fluctuations in the level of activity. (SM 1.28)

### Exam angle

**[exam] Where cost behaviour appeared, May 2024 to September 2026 (and old-scheme papers)**
In the ICAI documents read for this lesson, cost behaviour and the split of semi-variable costs came up in 8 questions in 8 documents, almost always inside a bigger numerical.

- **Classify by variability, 5 marks.** January 2026 Q6(a): five items by function and variability.
- **Split from two periods.** MTP May 2025 Series II MCQs 6–7 (2 marks each): repairs of ₹1,40,000 for 18 trips per bus and ₹1,60,000 for 22 trips; the answer writes x + 18y = 1,40,000 and x + 22y = 1,60,000. Old scheme MTP November 2022 Series I Q2(b): a flexible budget at 75% and 100% capacity, each item to be split.
- **A given variable component.** May 2026 Q2(b) (7 marks): a semi-variable cost of ₹8,20,000 with a variable component of ₹10 a unit, plus an additional fixed cost beyond 80% capacity.
- **Steps.** May 2025 Q5(c) (7 marks): buses for a college trip and block entrance fees by group size.
- **Pro rata, not steps.** MTP May 2026 Series II Q5(a): a semi-variable overhead of ₹1,50,000 a year up to 60% capacity plus ₹60,000 "for every 20% increase in capacity or proportionately", and direct labour subject to a monthly minimum.
- **Theory.** Old scheme MTP December 2021 Series II Q6(b) and RTP July 2021 Q13(c): "DISCUSS cost classification based on variability and controllability".

**[exam] The traps**
- A cost that changes between two levels is not automatically semi-variable. Test it: if the rate from the change also explains the total (₹1,80,000 ÷ 60,000 = ₹3, the same as the rate), it is purely variable.
- Supervisors, buses and batches are hired whole: round **up**, then cost the steps. But "or proportionately" means pro rata, not a step.
- "Fixed cost up to 80% capacity": use the higher fixed cost once the plan crosses 80%.
- The Study Material's high-low method uses **sales value**, not units: the variable cost comes out as a percentage of sales (SM 1.30). Read which measure of volume the question gives.

**[trick] Fixed per unit is variable, variable per unit is fixed**
Fixed cost per unit changes with every change in output; variable cost per unit stays the same. Condition: within the relevant range, with no change in prices or rates.

### Answer frame

### "Discuss cost classification based on variability"

1. Fixed costs: ICAI's definition ("incurred for a period ... within certain output and turnover limits ... unaffected by fluctuations in the levels of activity") and examples: rent, insurance of factory building.
2. Variable costs: definition ("tend to vary with the volume of activity") and examples: direct material, direct labour.
3. Semi-variable costs: definition ("contain both fixed and variable components") and examples: telephone bills, gas and electricity.
4. A sketch of each, cost on the Y-axis and output on the X-axis.

### "Explain the methods of segregating semi-variable costs"

Name all five in ICAI's order (graphical, high-low, analytical, comparison by period or level of activity, least squares), then two or three lines on each with its formula, and close with ICAI's verdict that least squares is the best method (SM 1.29–1.32). A one-line numerical illustration for high-low earns the marks for "explain".

### The numerical parts

**[cols] **Loses marks****

"Power at 90% = ₹2,10,000 × 90 ÷ 60 = ₹3,15,000."

Treats a semi-variable cost as variable: the fixed ₹60,000 has been multiplied too.
---
**Passes**

"Variable rate ₹2.50 a kg, so power at 90% = ₹2,85,000."

Right figure, but the rate and the fixed part appear from nowhere.
---
**Full marks**

A working note for each item: change in cost ÷ change in output = rate; fixed = total − rate × output; check at the other level; then the estimate. Step costs shown as number of steps × cost per step, with the rounding stated.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the rent that doubled with the chai** **Fix:** split first. Only variable costs and the variable parts of semi-variable costs move with output; fixed costs stay, and step costs move only when a band is crossed.
- **The supervisor who became 2.25 rupees a kilogram** **Fix:** when the question says "one for every ..." or "for every 20% increase ... or part thereof", count the steps and round up ("or proportionately" is pro rata instead).
- **High-low with the wrong two months** **Fix:** high-low uses the highest and lowest volumes; comparison by period uses the two periods the question names.

### Recap

- Fixed: incurred for a period, unaffected by activity within limits (rent, insurance). Variable: varies with activity (direct material, direct labour). Semi-variable: both parts (telephone, gas, electricity) (SM 1.27–1.28).
- Step cost: flat within a band of activity, jumps at the next band; count steps and round up.
- Per unit: fixed cost per unit falls as output rises; variable cost per unit stays the same.
- Five methods in ICAI's order: graphical, high-low, analytical, comparison by period or level of activity, least squares (the best).
- High-low: variable rate = change in cost ÷ change in volume between the highest and lowest volumes; fixed = total − rate × volume; check at the other level.
- Comparison by period: change in expense ÷ change in output for the two periods given.
- Least squares: y = mx + c (m variable cost per unit, c total fixed cost).
- Split before you flex: never scale a whole semi-variable cost in proportion to output. (The rent does not double with the chai.)

## Lesson 1.7: Cost control and cost reduction

*Cost control keeps actual costs to the standard; cost reduction lowers the standard itself, permanently and without hurting quality. ICAI's steps, three-fold assumptions and five differences.*

### Key idea

**Cost control** keeps costs to the standard (it accepts the standard). **Cost reduction** challenges the standard and lowers it, and counts only if the saving is in unit cost, permanent, and leaves utility and quality unaffected.

### Exam angle

**[exam] Where cost control and cost reduction appeared (new and old scheme)**
In the ICAI documents read for this lesson, the distinction was asked 10 times in 10 documents, always as a descriptive question.

- **New scheme, 4–5 marks.** May 2024 Q6(a), "Distinguish between cost control and cost reduction" (5 marks); May 2026 Q6(b), the same words (5 marks); MTP May 2025 Series I Q6(d) and RTP January 2026 Q19(d): "Cost control emphasis is on past and present, while cost reduction emphasis is on present and future. DISCUSS some more differences" (4 marks in the MTP).
- **Old scheme.** December 2021 Q6(e) (5 marks); MTP July 2021 Series II Q6(a); MTP November 2023 Series II Q6(a); RTP December 2021 Q15(a); RTP May 2023 Q15(d); RTP November 2022 Q15(b).
- Every suggested answer read is ICAI's table from SM 1.6, in the same order. Where the question already gives one point, the suggested answer gives the other four (MTP May 2025).
- The chapter's own MCQs test the same table: "It ends when targets achieved" is true of cost control, and "lowest possible cost under best conditions" is the false statement, because ICAI's words are "under existing conditions" (SM 1.43–1.44).

**[trick] Control looks back, reduction looks forward**
Control: standard accepted, past and present, preventive, ends at the target. Reduction: standard challenged, present and future, corrective, never ends. Condition: these are ICAI's words; write them as given, including "preventive" for control and "corrective" for reduction, which are easy to swap.

### Answer frame

### "Distinguish between cost control and cost reduction" (5 marks)

A two-column table with ICAI's five points in ICAI's order: aim (maintain standards / challenge standards), conditions (lowest cost under existing conditions / no condition permanent), emphasis (past and present / present and future), function (preventive / corrective, operates even with efficient cost control), end (ends when targets achieved / no visible end, continuous). If time allows, add one line each: the definition of cost reduction and the four steps of cost control.

### "DISCUSS some more differences" (when the question gives one)

Leave out the point already given (for example the emphasis) and write the other four. Repeating the given point earns nothing.

### "Explain cost reduction" or "What are the assumptions of cost reduction?"

1. ICAI's definition, word for word.
2. The approach: no cost is termed as lowest.
3. The two actions: value analysis (value added and non-value added activities; Value Chain Analysis by Michael Porter) and continuous research and study.
4. The three-fold assumptions: saving in unit cost; permanent nature; utility and quality unaffected, if not improved.

**[cols] **Loses marks****

"Cost control controls cost; cost reduction reduces cost."

Restates the names; no point of difference.
---
**Passes**

Three or four correct points in a list, in any order, some in the student's own words.

Correct, but misses points and ICAI's phrases.
---
**Full marks**

All five points in a two-column table, in ICAI's order, in ICAI's words ("existing conditions", "preventive", "corrective", "no visible end").

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: the preventive and the corrective swapped** **Fix:** ICAI's table says cost control is a **preventive** function and cost reduction a **corrective** function that operates even when an efficient cost control system exists (SM 1.6), and the suggested answers of May 2024 and May 2026 print exactly these words. Write them as ICAI gives them, whatever your intuition says.
- **Calling every saving a cost reduction** **Fix:** test all three assumptions: a saving in unit cost, of a permanent nature, with utility and quality unaffected. Fail one, and it is not cost reduction.
- **"Under best conditions"** **Fix:** ICAI's words are "under **existing** conditions"; it is cost reduction that refuses to accept existing conditions as permanent.

### Recap

- Cost control: maintaining discipline in expenditure, so that expenditures are in consonance with predetermined standards and variations are noted and reported continuously.
- Four steps of cost control, in order: set the standard, measure actual on the same basis, compare and report the variance, analyse and act (amend the standard if necessary).
- Cost reduction: real and permanent reduction in unit cost without impairing suitability for the use intended or diminution in quality.
- Three-fold assumptions: saving in unit cost; permanent; utility and quality unaffected, if not improved.
- Two actions: value analysis (eliminate non-value added activities; Porter's Value Chain Analysis) and continuous research and study.
- Five differences: maintain vs challenge standards; existing conditions vs no condition permanent; past and present vs present and future; preventive vs corrective; ends at target vs no visible end.
- Reduction lowers the standard; control then guards the new standard. (The ₹6.50 cup became the ₹6.10 cup.)

## Lesson 1.8: Methods and techniques of costing, and digital costing

*Which method of costing fits which kind of output, the techniques that work with any method, how IT and ERP changed cost accounting, and what a digital costing system collects and delivers.*

### Key idea

The **method** follows the nature of the output (job, batch, contract, process, single output, service, or a mix). A **technique** (uniform, marginal, standard, historical, absorption) is used with any method. **Digital costing** changes how cost data is collected, stored, analysed and reported, and links the entity with its suppliers, customers and market.

### Common mistakes (title and fix)

- **Hall of Shame: marginal costing listed as a method** **Fix:** methods are single or output, batch, job, contract, process, operating and multiple costing (SM 1.36–1.37); uniform, marginal, standard, historical and absorption costing are techniques (SM 1.38–1.39).

- **Digital costing answered as "computerised accounts"** **Fix:** use ICAI's points: links with suppliers, customers and the market; AI and machine learning on Big Data; real-time standards and variances; Marked to Market standards; demand prediction; elimination of non-value-added activities (SM 1.18–1.19).

### Recap

- Methods (SM 1.36–1.37): single or output, batch, job, contract, process (operation), operating, multiple. The method follows the nature of the output.
- Batch cost per unit = cost of the batch ÷ units in the batch; operation costing = process costing for mechanical operations.
- Techniques (SM 1.38–1.39): uniform, marginal, standard costing and variance analysis, historical (post, continuous), absorption. Standard costing can be used with any method.
- Uniform costing's two advantages: inter-firm comparison; an industry cost for tax relief or protection.
- IT impact (SM 1.17–1.18): ERP integration, paperless, extranet procurement and JIT, codified timely cost, uniformity, real-time variances, NVA elimination.
- Digital costing (SM 1.18): same functions, different collection, storage, analysis and reporting; links production, procurement and inventory with suppliers, customers and the market.
- Its benefits (SM 1.19): cost with certainty, time data for incentives, MRP and JIT, NVA elimination, real-time standards, M2M standards, demand prediction, cost behaviour analysis. (Containers, lenses, and now sensors.)
