## In plain words

### The road that looks like the moon

Tapri's samosa factory stands in Kulhadpur, a small town with a Municipal Council, a lot of potholes and a new musical fountain that has never played a note.

> **Kesar:** The council takes property tax from me, hoarding tax from me, and the road outside my factory looks like the moon.
> **Lakshmi:** Then you will like this, Kesar-ji. The State's Local Fund Audit Wing has appointed us to audit the council this year.
> **Bunty:** So we check its profit, Ma'am?
> **Lakshmi:** A municipality has no profit, Bunty. We check that its money was raised and spent by the rules, and whether the town got value for it.

A **municipality** is local self-government for a town or city: a body representing the local inhabitants, raising part of its money through local taxes (property tax above all), fees, rents and grants from the State, and spending it on local services such as roads, sanitation, public health and schools.

Here is the catch. In the State and Central governments the legislature that votes the money and the executive that spends it are separate. In a municipal council the same body does both, and there is no independent finance officer answerable to it. So the only real check on how a municipality spends comes from outside: the **external audit** arranged by the State government, usually through its **Local Fund Audit Wing**. That auditor reports on whether the accounts are fair, whether financial controls work, whether the law and the administrative rules were followed, whether the town got value for its money, and on error, fraud and misuse.

::: analogy The housing society committee
In many housing societies the same committee fixes the maintenance charge, spends it and keeps the books. Nobody inside can really check the committee, because the committee is everyone inside. So members insist on an outside auditor, who asks not only "do the books add up?" but "why did the lift repair cost three times the quote, and why does the lift still not work?" A municipality is that committee, for a whole town.
:::

::: key
A municipal council combines legislative and executive powers, so external audit by the State government is the only instrument of controlling municipal expenditure. The audit reports on fair accounts, financial controls, compliance, value for money, and detects and prevents error, fraud and misuse of resources.
:::

## How it works

### 1. What a local body is

::: definition Municipality and local self-government
A **municipality** can be defined as a unit of local self-government in an urban area. By **local self-government** is ordinarily understood the administration of a locality (a village, a town, a city or any other area smaller than a state) by a body representing the local inhabitants, possessing fairly large autonomy, raising at least a part of its revenue through local taxation and spending its income on services which are regarded as local and, therefore, distinct from state and central services (SM 9.22).
:::

The Study Material's discussion is based on the article "Audit of Municipal Administration" by R. Chandra Sekharan (SM 9.22). Its main facts (SM 9.23):

- **Five types of urban local authorities**: the municipal corporations, the municipal councils, the notified area committees, the town area committees and the cantonment committees.
- **Three kinds of functions**: (a) regulatory, (b) maintenance and (c) development activities.
- **Six heads of expenditure**: (a) general administration and revenue collection, (b) public health, (c) public safety, (d) education, (e) public works, and (f) others such as interest payments.

### 2. Where the money comes from

Local bodies derive their revenues from taxes on property, taxes on trade and taxes on persons; fees and licences; non-tax resources such as rent of land and houses and income from commercial undertakings; and government grants (SM 9.23).

**Property taxes and octroi are the major sources of revenue** of municipal authorities. Other municipal taxes are profession tax, non-mechanised vehicles tax, taxes on advertisements, taxes on animals and boats, tolls, show-tax and so on. The taxation powers of **corporations** are confined to a few items and are generally compulsive in nature; those of the **other urban local authorities** cover a wider range, are optional, and need the final sanction of the State government before they are imposed (SM 9.23–9.24).

Local bodies may also receive grants from the State administration. Broadly, the **revenue grants** are of three categories (SM 9.24):

- (a) **General purpose grants**: primarily intended to substantially bridge the gap between the needs and resources of the local bodies.
- (b) **Specific purpose grants**: tied to the provision of certain services or the performance of certain tasks.
- (c) **Statutory and compensatory grants**: given under various enactments to local bodies as compensation for loss of any revenue when the State government takes over a tax from the local government.

### 3. Financial administration: what the auditor must understand first

The auditor should understand the financial administration of local bodies before embarking on the audit (SM 9.24–9.25):

1. **Budgetary procedure.** It serves the twin considerations of **financial accountability and control of expenditure**: funds are to be raised and money spent by the executive departments according to the rules and within the limits of sanction and authorisation by the legislature or council. Budget preparation is usually when the levels of taxation and rates and the ceilings on expenditure are fixed. Budget formats and heads of account vary from State to State, and between corporations and municipalities. **There is no strict separation between revenue and capital items**: a head called **"extraordinary items"** usually covers most capital transactions. There are special funds (for example roads), and in some cases separate budgets for specific functions (for example education) or enterprise activities (water supply and sanitation, transport, electricity).
2. **Expenditure control.** In the State and Central governments there is a clear demarcation between legislature and executive. In a municipal council, legislative and executive powers are integrated, which makes it difficult for its executive to act as its inquisitorial body as well, and the separation of executive powers cannot accommodate an independent finance officer responsible to the council or its executive committee. ==This leaves the system of external audit by state government as the only instrument of controlling municipal expenditure==.
3. **Accounting system.** Municipal accounting and budget formats have been criticised as neither simple nor comprehensible, sometimes giving inadequate information and at other times a surfeit of it; neither helps a proper management information system.

### 4. Who audits, and what for

The **external control of municipal expenditure is exercised by the State governments** through the appointment of auditors to examine municipal accounts. However, the municipal corporations of Delhi, Mumbai and a few others have powers to appoint their own auditors for regular external audit (SM 9.25). The **Local Fund Audit Wing** of the State government is generally in charge of the audit of municipal accounts (SM 9.26).

**The important objectives of audit** (SM 9.25):

- (a) reporting on the fairness of the content and presentation of financial statements;
- (b) reporting upon the strengths and weaknesses of systems of financial control;
- (c) reporting on the adherence to legal and/or administrative requirements;
- (d) reporting upon whether value is being fully received on money spent; and
- (e) detection and prevention of error, fraud and misuse of resources.

**Audit and the privilege of ultra vires.** Audit is another method of financial control over local governments, coupled with the privilege of ultra vires: an action of the local authority that is **beyond its legal authority can result in "surcharge" by audit**. The procedure is a legacy of colonial days, and even in England it is resorted to less and less, perhaps because local authorities have become more competent (SM 9.25).

**Internal audit.** The author also opines that every municipal institution should have an internal audit, by its own staff, performed continuously to a well-defined programme. The external auditor should be able to rely on it as part of a complete system of internal financial control. **Where there is no internal audit**, as in small or poorly staffed municipalities, **the external auditor has to do detailed checking himself** (SM 9.26).

**Value for money.** As in government audit, increasing attention goes to "value for money" audit: whether urban institutions fulfil their responsibilities with **efficiency, economy and effectiveness**, sometimes called "the three Es" (SM 9.26). It is the municipal cousin of the performance audit in [[aud.entities.govt|government audit]].

### 5. The audit programme for local bodies

::: steps
1. **Appointment.** The Local Fund Audit Wing of the State government is generally in charge of the audit of municipal accounts; bigger corporations such as Delhi and Mumbai may appoint their own auditors. So the auditor should ensure his appointment.
2. **Auditor's concerns.** Report on the fairness of the contents and presentation of the financial statements, the strengths and weaknesses of the system of financial control, the adherence to legal and/or administrative requirements, and whether value is being fully received on money spent. The objective should be to detect errors and fraud and misuse of resources.
3. **Rules and regulations.** Ensure that the expenditure conforms to the relevant provisions of the law and is in accordance with the financial rules and regulations framed by the competent authority.
4. **Authorisations.** Ensure that all types of sanctions, special or general, are accorded by the competent authority.
5. **Provisioning.** Ensure that there is a provision of funds, that the expenditure is incurred from the provision, and that it has been authorised by the competent authority.
6. **Performance.** Check that the different schemes, programmes and projects where large financial expenditure has been incurred are running economically and getting the expected results.
:::

Steps 3 to 6 are the expenditure-audit standards of [[aud.entities.govt|government audit]] (rules and orders, sanctions, provision of funds, performance) applied to a town (SM 9.26–9.27).

## Worked examples

:::: example Kulhadpur's money, sorted
Kulhadpur Municipal Council's draft accounts for 2025-26 show all its grants from the State, ₹5.20 crore, under one line "General purpose grants". The grant letters show:

| Grant | Amount (₹ lakh) | What the letter says |
|---|---|---|
| A | 320 | To bridge the gap between the council's needs and its own resources |
| B | 85 | Only for running six urban health centres |
| C | 115 | Compensation for the entertainment tax, whose levy the State has taken over from the council |
| **Total** | **520** | |

Its expenditure includes salaries of tax-collection staff ₹1.40 crore, anti-malaria fogging ₹35 lakh, the fire brigade ₹60 lakh, municipal schools ₹1.10 crore, road resurfacing ₹2.25 crore and interest on municipal bonds ₹48 lakh.

Required:

(i) Classify the three grants into the categories of revenue grants, and state why the single line matters to the auditor.

(ii) Classify the six items of expenditure under the heads into which municipal expenditure is broadly classified.

::: answer Model answer
**(i) Revenue grants.** Local bodies may receive three categories of revenue grants from the State (SM 9.24):

| Grant | Category | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| A, ₹320 lakh | General purpose grant | Intended to bridge the gap between needs and resources |
| B, ₹85 lakh | Specific purpose grant | Tied to the provision of a service (health centres) |
| C, ₹115 lakh | Statutory and compensatory grant | Compensation for loss of revenue on the State taking over a tax |

The single line hides grant B, which is tied to a service. The auditor must ensure that there is a provision of funds and that expenditure is incurred from that provision under proper authority (audit programme, provisioning), so he must see that the ₹85 lakh was spent on the six health centres. The draft accounts should show the three categories separately; the total stays ₹520 lakh.

**(ii) Heads of expenditure** (SM 9.23):

| Item | Amount (₹ lakh) | Head |
|---|---|---|
| Tax-collection staff salaries | 140 | General administration and revenue collection |
| Anti-malaria fogging | 35 | Public health |
| Fire brigade | 60 | Public safety |
| Municipal schools | 110 | Education |
| Road resurfacing | 225 | Public works |
| Interest on municipal bonds | 48 | Others (interest payments) |
| **Total** | **618** | |

::::: example The fountain that never danced
The Local Fund Audit Wing of the State government has appointed Iyer & Associates to audit Kulhadpur Municipal Council for 2025-26. Bunty's working papers note:

(i) A ₹2.80 crore musical fountain was installed in the town square with every approval in place; it has not worked since the day it was inaugurated.

(ii) LED streetlights worth ₹45 lakh were bought on the oral instructions of the council's chairperson; no sanction is on file.

(iii) ₹30 lakh was taken from the Roads Special Fund to pay for the councillors' study tour.

(iv) The council resolved to waive property tax on buildings owned by its own councillors, though the Municipalities Act under which it works gives it no power to grant such a waiver.

(v) Advertisement tax on hoardings is collected in cash; nobody reconciles the hoardings standing in the town with the tax collected.

(vi) The council has no internal audit; two accountants keep all the books.

> **Bunty:** Ma'am, shall I just write "noted" against each?
> **Lakshmi:** Write what the audit is for, Bunty. Then decide.

Advise Iyer & Associates how each observation should be dealt with, with reference to the objectives of the audit of a local body and the audit programme.

:::: answer Model answer
**Relevant provision.** The external control of municipal expenditure is exercised by the State governments through the appointment of auditors to examine municipal accounts; the Local Fund Audit Wing is generally in charge (SM 9.25–9.26). The important objectives of audit are: (a) reporting on the fairness of the content and presentation of financial statements; (b) reporting upon the strengths and weaknesses of systems of financial control; (c) reporting on the adherence to legal and/or administrative requirements; (d) reporting upon whether value is being fully received on money spent; and (e) detection and prevention of error, fraud and misuse of resources. An action beyond the legal authority of the local authority can result in surcharge by audit, and where there is no internal audit the external auditor has to do detailed checking (SM 9.25–9.26).

**Facts, analysis and treatment.**

| Observation | Objective and audit programme step | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| (i) Idle fountain | (d) value for money; performance | Report that value is not being received on ₹2.80 crore |
| (ii) No sanction | (c) adherence; authorisations | Report expenditure without sanction of the competent authority |
| (iii) Roads Fund used for a tour | (c), (e); provisioning | Report use of a special fund outside its purpose |
| (iv) Tax waiver without power | (c); rules and regulations | Ultra vires: may result in surcharge by audit |
| (v) Cash hoarding tax | (b), (e) | Report the control weakness; extend checks for loss |
| (vi) No internal audit | (b) | Do detailed checking himself |

**Conclusion.** The auditor first ensures his appointment by the Local Fund Audit Wing, and then reports on all five objectives: the fountain under value for money, the streetlights and the Roads Fund under adherence to legal and administrative requirements, the waiver as an ultra vires act liable to surcharge, and the cash collections and the missing internal audit under the weaknesses of financial control, with detailed checking to detect and prevent error, fraud and misuse of resources.

## Under the hood

### Why municipalities started small

By 1947 most urban and semi-urban areas had been constituted into municipalities. The premier corporations of Mumbai, Chennai and Kolkata had a special constitutional structure, better financial resources and less State control than other municipal bodies. Local bodies' services were then restricted to education, public health, sanitation, medical relief, roads and a few public works, because these bodies were considered inefficient and lacked adequate financial resources (SM 9.22–9.23).

### Local bodies and government audit, side by side

| Point | Government audit | Local bodies |
|---|---|---|
| External auditor | C&AG, through the Indian Audit and Accounts Department | State government auditors (Local Fund Audit Wing); a few big corporations their own |
| Why audit is the key control | Accountability of the executive to the legislature | Council combines legislative and executive powers |
| Results test | Performance audit | Value for money, "the three Es" |
| Distinctive feature | Article 151 report laid before the legislature | Surcharge by audit for ultra vires actions |

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: the Centre audits the town
Bunty writes that the Central Government controls municipal spending through the C&AG. **Fix:** external control of municipal expenditure is exercised by the **State governments**, generally through the Local Fund Audit Wing; Delhi, Mumbai and a few other corporations appoint their own auditors.
:::

::: mistake Four objectives instead of five
Listing only the four "reporting" objectives. **Fix:** the fifth, **detection and prevention of error, fraud and misuse of resources**, is the one the audit programme calls "his objective"; write all five.
:::

::: mistake Compensatory grant or general grant?
Calling every untied grant "general purpose". **Fix:** a grant that makes good the revenue lost because the State took over a tax is a **statutory and compensatory grant**; a general purpose grant bridges the gap between needs and resources.
:::

::: mistake Ignoring the missing internal audit
**Fix:** where a small or poorly staffed municipality has no internal audit, the external auditor cannot rely on one and has to do **detailed checking** himself (SM 9.26).
:::

## Recap

- A municipality is a unit of local self-government in an urban area: representing local inhabitants, fairly autonomous, raising part of its revenue by local taxation, spending on local services.
- Five urban local authorities: municipal corporations, municipal councils, notified area committees, town area committees, cantonment committees. Functions: regulatory, maintenance, development.
- Expenditure heads: general administration and revenue collection, public health, public safety, education, public works, others (interest).
- Property taxes and octroi are the major revenues. Revenue grants: general purpose, specific purpose, statutory and compensatory.
- No strict revenue-capital separation ("extraordinary items"); a council combines legislative and executive powers, so external audit by the State is the only instrument of controlling expenditure.
- Auditors are appointed by the State (Local Fund Audit Wing); Delhi, Mumbai and a few others appoint their own.
- Objectives: fairness of financial statements, financial control systems, adherence to legal and administrative requirements, value for money, error, fraud and misuse. Ultra vires acts can lead to surcharge; no internal audit means detailed checking.
- Programme: appointment, concerns, rules and regulations, authorisations, provisioning, performance. (The fountain still does not dance; now it is in the report.)
