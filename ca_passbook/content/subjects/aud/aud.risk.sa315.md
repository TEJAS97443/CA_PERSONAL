## In plain words

### Bunty wants to tick vouchers on day one

Iyer & Associates have taken on the audit of Tapri Cold Chain Pvt Ltd, Tapri's refrigerated-delivery company. Bunty arrives with a fresh pen.

> **Bunty:** Ma'am, where are the purchase vouchers? I will start ticking.
> **Lakshmi:** Ticking what, Bunty? Do you know what this company sells, who owes it money, what its trucks are worth, or why its profit jumped?
> **Bunty:** ...Not yet.
> **Lakshmi:** Then your tick means nothing yet.

Before testing anything, the auditor must **understand the entity**: its industry and regulation, how it is owned, run and financed, its accounting policies, its goals and the business risks behind them, how its performance is measured, and its internal control. The auditor learns this by **risk assessment procedures**: asking people (inquiry), comparing numbers and ratios (analytical procedures), and watching and reading (observation and inspection).

From that understanding the auditor **identifies and assesses the risks of material misstatement**: what could go wrong, how likely, how big, and whether it spreads across the whole set of accounts or sits in one assertion. Some risks are **significant risks** (fraud, unusual related party deals, wildly uncertain estimates) and get special attention. All of this is the map; the digging comes next, under [[aud.risk.sa330|SA 330]].

::: analogy The doctor before the prescription
A good doctor asks about your history (inquiry), checks your blood report against last year's (analytical procedures) and examines you (observation and inspection) before writing a prescription. The consultation itself cures nothing, but no prescription is safe without it. Risk assessment procedures are the consultation; the audit tests that follow are the prescription.
:::

::: key
SA 315: understand the entity and its environment, including internal control, through risk assessment procedures (inquiry, analytical procedures, observation and inspection), so as to identify and assess the risks of material misstatement at the financial statement and assertion levels. These procedures are the basis for the responses, but by themselves they never give sufficient appropriate audit evidence for the opinion.
:::

## How it works

### 1. The objective

As per SA 315, "Identifying and Assessing the Risks of Material Misstatement through Understanding the Entity and its Environment", the objective of the auditor is ==to identify and assess the risks of material misstatement==, whether due to fraud or error, at the financial statement and assertion levels, through understanding the entity and its environment, including the entity's internal control, thereby providing a basis for designing and implementing responses to the assessed risks of material misstatement (SM 3.12).

### 2. Identifying and assessing the risks

(i) The auditor shall identify and assess the risks of material misstatement at (a) the financial statement level and (b) the assertion level for classes of transactions, account balances and disclosures, to provide a basis for designing and performing further audit procedures (SM 3.12).

(ii) For this purpose the auditor shall (SM 3.12–3.13):

- (a) **identify** risks throughout the process of obtaining an understanding of the entity and its environment, including relevant controls that relate to the risks, and by considering the classes of transactions, account balances and disclosures in the financial statements;
- (b) **assess** the identified risks, and evaluate whether they relate more pervasively to the financial statements as a whole and potentially affect many assertions;
- (c) **relate** the identified risks to what can go wrong at the assertion level, taking account of relevant controls that the auditor intends to test; and
- (d) **consider the likelihood** of misstatement, including the possibility of multiple misstatements, and whether the potential misstatement is of a magnitude that could result in a material misstatement.

### 3. Risk assessment procedures

::: definition Risk assessment procedures
The audit procedures performed to obtain an understanding of the entity and its environment, including the entity's internal control, to identify and assess the risks of material misstatement, whether due to fraud or error, at the financial statement and assertion levels (SM 3.13).
:::

The auditor shall perform them to provide a basis for identifying and assessing the risks. They **do not by themselves provide sufficient appropriate audit evidence** on which to base the opinion, and the risks they look for include both error and fraud (SM 3.13). The risk assessment procedures **shall include** (SM 3.13):

- (a) **inquiries** of management and of others within the entity who in the auditor's judgment may have information likely to assist in identifying risks of material misstatement due to fraud or error;
- (b) **analytical procedures**; and
- (c) **observation and inspection**.

**(a) Inquiries.** Much of the information comes from management and those responsible for financial reporting, but others give a different perspective (SM 3.14):

| Inquiry directed to | May provide information about |
|---|---|
| Internal audit personnel | Internal audit procedures on the design and effectiveness of internal control, and whether management has responded to the findings |
| Employees initiating, processing or recording complex or unusual transactions | The appropriateness of the selection and application of accounting policies |
| In-house legal counsel | Litigation, compliance with laws, known or suspected fraud, warranties, post-sales obligations, arrangements with business partners, the meaning of contracts |
| Marketing or sales personnel | Changes in marketing strategies, sales trends, contractual arrangements with customers |
| Risk management function | Operational and regulatory risks that may affect financial reporting |
| Information systems personnel | System changes, system or control failures, other information system-related risks |

**(b) Analytical procedures** performed as risk assessment procedures may identify aspects of the entity of which the auditor was unaware, and may use both financial and non-financial information (for example, the relationship between sales and square footage of selling space or volume of goods sold). They may identify unusual transactions or events, amounts, ratios and trends with audit implications, especially risks of misstatement due to fraud. On data aggregated at a high level they give only a broad initial indication, to be read with the other information gathered (SM 3.14–3.15).

**(c) Observation and inspection** support inquiries and give information about the entity: observing or inspecting the entity's operations; documents (business plans and strategies), records and internal control manuals; reports by management (quarterly management reports, interim financial statements) and by those charged with governance (minutes of board meetings); and the entity's premises and plant facilities (SM 3.15).

Information from these procedures may be used as **audit evidence** to support the risk assessment, and may also give evidence about assertions and the operating effectiveness of controls; the auditor may perform substantive procedures or tests of controls concurrently because it is efficient (SM 3.15–3.16).

### 4. What the auditor must understand

SA 315 states that the auditor shall obtain an understanding of the following (SM 3.24–3.27):

::: diagram The five areas of understanding (SM 3.24–3.27)
{"type": "layers", "layers": [{"label": "(a) Industry, regulatory, external factors", "note": "competition, regulation, the framework, economy"}, {"label": "(b) Nature of the entity", "note": "operations, ownership, investments, financing"}, {"label": "(c) Accounting policies", "note": "appropriate? consistent? why changed?"}, {"label": "(d) Objectives, strategies, business risks", "note": "those that may cause material misstatement"}, {"label": "(e) Measuring financial performance", "note": "KPIs, budgets, ratings: pressure"}]}
:::

- **(a)** Industry conditions (the competitive environment, supplier and customer relationships, technological developments) can create risks from the nature of the business or the degree of regulation (SM 3.24–3.25).
- **(b)** The nature of the entity shows whether it has a complex structure, such as subsidiaries, which often introduces risks, and its relationships with owners, which helps in judging whether **related party transactions** have been identified and accounted for (SM 3.25). Matters to consider include business operations (revenue sources, products or services, location of production facilities, key customers and suppliers), investment activities (capital investment, planned or recent acquisitions), financing (major subsidiaries, debt structure) and financial reporting (accounting principles, revenue recognition practices) (SM 3.26).
- **(c)** The auditor shall evaluate whether the accounting policies are appropriate for the business and consistent with the framework and the policies used in the industry (SM 3.26).
- **(d)** **Business risk** is broader than the risk of material misstatement, though it includes it, and may arise from change or complexity. Most business risks eventually have financial consequences, but the auditor has no responsibility to identify or assess all of them (SM 3.26). Examples: industry developments (no expertise to deal with them); new products (increased product liability); expansion (demand not accurately estimated) (SM 3.27).
- **(e)** Performance measures create pressures that may motivate management to improve the business or **to misstate the financial statements**, including through fraud. Examples the auditor may use: key performance indicators (financial and non-financial), key ratios, trends and operating statistics; period-on-period analyses; budgets, forecasts, variance analyses and departmental reports; credit rating agency reports (SM 3.27).

The understanding also includes the entity's **internal control** relevant to the audit, through its five components: the control environment, the entity's risk assessment process, the information system and communication, control activities, and monitoring of controls (SM 3.32). They are covered in [[aud.risk.ic|internal control: components and limitations]].

### 5. Why it matters, and why it never stops

Understanding the entity helps the auditor plan the audit and identify areas requiring special attention; without adequate knowledge of the client's business, a proper audit is not possible (SM 3.27–3.28). It is **a continuous, dynamic process** of gathering, updating and analysing information throughout the audit, and a frame of reference for judgments such as (SM 3.28): assessing risks of material misstatement; determining materiality under [[aud.risk.sa320|SA 320]]; considering accounting policies; identifying areas needing special consideration (related party transactions, the going concern assumption, the business purpose of transactions); developing expectations for analytical procedures; and evaluating the sufficiency and appropriateness of evidence, including management's representations.

### 6. Significant risks

As part of the risk assessment, the auditor shall determine whether any identified risk is, in the auditor's judgment, a **significant risk**. The auditor shall consider at least (SM 3.41–3.42):

- (a) whether the risk is a risk of fraud;
- (b) whether it relates to recent significant economic, accounting or other developments (such as changes in the regulatory environment) and so needs specific attention;
- (c) the complexity of transactions;
- (d) whether it involves significant transactions with related parties;
- (e) the degree of subjectivity in measuring the financial information, especially measurements with a wide range of measurement uncertainty; and
- (f) whether it involves significant transactions outside the normal course of business, or that otherwise appear unusual.

Significant risks often relate to **significant non-routine transactions** (unusual in size or nature, so infrequent) or **judgmental matters** (estimates with significant measurement uncertainty). They are inherent risks ([[aud.risk.model|audit risk]]) with both a higher likelihood and a higher magnitude of potential misstatement. ==Always significant: risks of material misstatement due to fraud, and significant related party transactions outside the normal course of business== (SM 3.42).

Risks of material misstatement may be **greater for significant non-routine transactions** because of (SM 3.42):

- greater management intervention to specify the accounting treatment;
- greater manual intervention for data collection and processing;
- complex calculations or accounting principles;
- the nature of non-routine transactions, which may make it difficult for the entity to implement effective controls over the risks.

They may be **greater for significant judgmental matters** that require accounting estimates, because (SM 3.42–3.43):

- accounting principles for accounting estimates or revenue recognition may be subject to differing interpretation;
- required judgment may be subjective or complex, or require assumptions about the effects of future events (for example, judgment about fair value).

### 7. Documenting the risks

The auditor shall document (SM 3.64):

- (a) the discussion among the engagement team and the significant decisions reached;
- (b) key elements of the understanding of each aspect of the entity and its environment and of each internal control component, the sources of information, and the risk assessment procedures performed;
- (c) the identified and assessed risks of material misstatement at the financial statement level and at the assertion level; and
- (d) the risks identified, and related controls about which the auditor has obtained an understanding.

## Worked examples

:::: example The kulhads that did not add up
Every cup of chai at a Tapri café is served in one kulhad, issued to the café from Tapri's central store; Tapri's own norm allows 4 extra kulhads per 100 cups for breakage. While planning the 2025-26 audit, Lakshmi's team puts the year's point-of-sale data for three cafés next to the store's issue records:

| Café | Sales per POS (₹ lakh) | Cups sold per POS (lakh) | Kulhads issued (lakh) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bhopal | 96.00 | 4.80 | 5.00 |
| Ujjain | 88.00 | 4.40 | 5.72 |
| Raipur | 102.00 | 5.10 | 4.59 |

Required:

(i) Compute the kulhads issued per 100 cups sold at each café and compare them with Tapri's norm.

(ii) Identify the procedure the team is performing, and state the risks of material misstatement that the Ujjain and Raipur results may indicate.

(iii) State the other risk assessment procedures the team should now perform at Ujjain and Raipur, and how the results will be used.

::: answer Model answer
**(i) Working.**

| Café | Kulhads ÷ cups × 100 | Against the norm of 104 |
|---|---|---|
| Bhopal | 5.00 ÷ 4.80 × 100 = 104.2 | In line |
| Ujjain | 5.72 ÷ 4.40 × 100 = 130.0 | 26 more kulhads per 100 cups than the norm allows |
| Raipur | 4.59 ÷ 5.10 × 100 = 90.0 | Fewer kulhads than cups sold, which cannot happen if every cup needs one |

All three cafés sell at ₹20 a cup (96.00 ÷ 4.80, 88.00 ÷ 4.40, 102.00 ÷ 5.10), so the price is not the explanation.

**(ii) The procedure and the risks.** The team is performing **analytical procedures as risk assessment procedures**. As per SA 315, these may include both financial and non-financial information (for example, the relationship between sales and the volume of goods sold), may identify unusual transactions, amounts, ratios and trends that might indicate matters with audit implications, and may assist in identifying risks of material misstatement, especially due to fraud. Where they use data aggregated at a high level, they give only a broad initial indication.

- *Ujjain*: more cups served than cups billed may mean sales not rung up and cash kept back (**completeness** of revenue, with a risk of fraud), or kulhads issued to the café but stolen or wasted (**accuracy** of inventory records).
- *Raipur*: more cups billed than kulhads received may mean sales recorded that did not take place, for example to meet a target (**occurrence** of revenue, again a possible fraud risk), or kulhads obtained outside the central store and not recorded (**completeness** of purchases and liabilities), or issues from the store not recorded (**existence** of the store's inventory).

**(iii) Further risk assessment procedures, and their use.** Risk assessment procedures also include (a) **inquiries** of management and of others within the entity, such as the café managers and counter staff about how sales are rung up and whether kulhads are bought locally, and information systems personnel about any change or failure in the POS system; and (b) **observation and inspection**, such as observing a café's counter during a busy evening and inspecting the store's issue notes and internal audit's café reports. Risk assessment procedures by themselves do not provide sufficient appropriate audit evidence on which to base the opinion. The results, read together with the other information gathered, are the basis for assessing the risks at Ujjain and Raipur and for designing further audit procedures under SA 330.
:::

:::: example Four items on Lakshmi's risk list
During the 2025-26 audit of Tapri Tea Co. Ltd, the team lists:

(a) Tapri sold its old Indore depot land to Kesar's brother-in-law's company at well below the market price. Tapri does not normally sell land.

(b) Monthly payroll for 3,400 employees, processed by the same system as last year.

(c) A provision for returns of unsold Diwali gift hampers, estimated by management over a wide range of possible outcomes.

(d) The senior management bonus doubles if the year's profit crosses ₹40 crore, and the draft profit is ₹40.3 crore.

Advise which of these are significant risks under SA 315, giving reasons.

::: answer Model answer
**Relevant provision.** As per SA 315, the auditor shall determine whether any identified risk is, in the auditor's judgment, a significant risk, considering at least: whether it is a risk of fraud; whether it relates to recent significant developments; the complexity of transactions; whether it involves significant transactions with related parties; the degree of subjectivity in measurement, especially with a wide range of measurement uncertainty; and whether it involves significant transactions outside the normal course of business or that otherwise appear unusual. Risks of material misstatement due to fraud, and significant related party transactions outside the normal course of business, are always significant risks.

**(a)** A significant transaction with a related party, outside the normal course of business and at an unusual price: **always a significant risk**.

**(b)** Routine, recurring and processed by an unchanged system: none of the six factors applies. In the auditor's judgment, **not a significant risk**, though the auditor still assesses it and designs further audit procedures for it.

**(c)** A judgmental matter: an accounting estimate with a high degree of subjectivity and a wide range of measurement uncertainty. In the auditor's judgment it is **likely to be a significant risk**.

**(d)** The bonus threshold creates pressure to reach the profit target, and profit is just above it. Performance measures that create such pressure may motivate management to misstate the financial statements, including through fraud (SM 3.27). The auditor should consider whether this is a **risk of material misstatement due to fraud**; if it is, it is **always a significant risk**. Such pressure on senior management may also be a risk at the financial statement level.

The significant risks so identified require special audit consideration, and the assessed risks are documented (SM 3.41, 3.64).
:::

::: answer Model answer
Both views are incorrect. A foreign operation opened during the year changes the nature of the entity, its structure and its business risks, and may give rise to new risks of material misstatement. Bunty must update the understanding and the risk assessment, and the planned further audit procedures with it.

## Under the hood

### What risk assessment is not

Gathering evidence about the valuation of trade receivables is **not** part of understanding the entity; it is a further audit procedure (RTP May 2024 MCQ 5). Gaining knowledge of the debt structure, evaluating accounting policies and understanding business risks are.

### Inquiry is never enough on its own

When the auditor evaluates the design and implementation of relevant controls, the risk assessment procedures may include inquiring of personnel, observing specific controls, inspecting documents and reports, and tracing transactions through the information system; **inquiry alone is not sufficient** (SM 3.40–3.41). The same idea appears when responses conflict: in MTP September 2026 Series II, the CFO's comfort contradicted the internal audit head and the ageing report, and the answer was to corroborate, not to accept. Testing controls is in [[aud.risk.testing|testing internal control]].

### When the auditor revises the assessment

If analytical procedures near the end of the audit identify a previously unrecognised risk of material misstatement, SA 315 requires the auditor to revise the assessment and modify the planned procedures (SM 4.114–4.115).

## Answer frame

### "How will the auditor identify and assess the risks?"

1. Objective of SA 315, in its words.
2. Identify and assess at two levels: financial statement and assertion.
3. The four steps: identify, assess (pervasive?), relate to assertions, consider likelihood and magnitude.
4. Through risk assessment procedures (inquiry, analytical procedures, observation and inspection) and the understanding of the entity.
5. Conclusion: this provides the basis for further audit procedures under SA 330.

### "Is this a significant risk?"

Provision (the six factors and the two "always" cases) → facts → the factor(s) the facts trigger → conclusion, with "special audit consideration" as the consequence.

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: the opinion built on ratios
Writing that analytical procedures at planning "confirm the revenue". **Fix:** risk assessment procedures by themselves do not provide sufficient appropriate audit evidence for the opinion; they are the basis for further procedures.
:::

::: mistake Understanding the entity "done" at planning
**Fix:** it is a continuous, dynamic process throughout the audit (SM 3.28).
:::

::: mistake Listing every business risk
**Fix:** the auditor considers business risks that may result in risks of material misstatement; there is no responsibility to identify or assess all business risks (SM 3.26).
:::

::: mistake Calling every large item a significant risk
**Fix:** size alone is not the test; apply the six factors. Significant risks often relate to significant non-routine transactions or judgmental matters (SM 3.42), and fraud risks and significant related party transactions outside the normal course always are significant.
:::

## Recap

- SA 315 objective: identify and assess the risks of material misstatement (fraud or error) at the financial statement and assertion levels, through understanding the entity and its environment, including internal control, as a basis for responses.
- Steps: identify; assess (pervasive?); relate to what can go wrong at the assertion level; consider likelihood and magnitude.
- Risk assessment procedures: inquiries, analytical procedures, observation and inspection. Never sufficient evidence on their own.
- Understand: (a) industry, regulatory, external factors; (b) nature of the entity; (c) accounting policies; (d) objectives, strategies, related business risks; (e) measurement and review of performance; plus internal control.
- Understanding is continuous and dynamic, a frame of reference for the whole audit.
- Significant risk factors: fraud, recent developments, complexity, related parties, subjectivity, unusual or outside the normal course. Always significant: fraud risks; significant related party transactions outside the normal course.
- Document: team discussion, key elements of the understanding and sources, assessed risks at both levels, risks and related controls. (No prescription before the consultation.)

## Flashcards

Q:: SA 315: what is the objective of the auditor?
A:: To identify and assess the risks of material misstatement, whether due to fraud or error, at the financial statement and assertion levels, through understanding the entity and its environment, including internal control, providing a basis for designing and implementing responses.

Q:: SA 315: what three procedures shall risk assessment procedures include?
A:: Inquiries of management and others within the entity; analytical procedures; observation and inspection.

Q:: Do risk assessment procedures provide sufficient appropriate audit evidence for the opinion?
A:: No. They are the basis for identifying and assessing risks, but by themselves do not provide sufficient appropriate audit evidence on which to base the opinion.

Q:: SA 315: four things the auditor shall do to identify and assess risks?
A:: Identify risks while understanding the entity; assess them and evaluate whether they are pervasive; relate them to what can go wrong at the assertion level; consider the likelihood and magnitude of misstatement.

Q:: SA 315: five areas of the entity the auditor shall understand?
A:: Industry, regulatory and other external factors (incl. the framework); the nature of the entity; its accounting policies; its objectives, strategies and related business risks; the measurement and review of its financial performance.

Q:: What can inquiries of in-house legal counsel reveal?
A:: Litigation, compliance with laws and regulations, known or suspected fraud, warranties, post-sales obligations, arrangements with business partners, and the meaning of contracts.

Q:: SA 315: six factors for judging whether a risk is significant?
A:: Risk of fraud; recent significant developments; complexity of transactions; significant related party transactions; subjectivity and measurement uncertainty; significant transactions outside the normal course or unusual.

Q:: Which risks are always significant risks?
A:: Risks of material misstatement due to fraud, and significant related party transactions outside the normal course of business.

Q:: Why are risks greater for significant non-routine transactions?
A:: Greater management intervention in the accounting treatment; greater manual intervention in data collection and processing; complex calculations or principles; and their nature makes effective controls difficult.

Q:: SA 315: what shall the auditor document?
A:: The engagement team discussion and significant decisions; key elements of the understanding, its sources and the risk assessment procedures; the assessed risks at both levels; the risks identified and related controls understood.

Q:: Is understanding the entity a one-time planning task?
A:: No. It is a continuous, dynamic process of gathering, updating and analysing information throughout the audit.
