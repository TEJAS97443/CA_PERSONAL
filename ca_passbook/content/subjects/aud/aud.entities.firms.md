## In plain words

### Four suppliers, four rulebooks

Kesar's cousin Dolly runs Dolly Packaging as a **sole trader**. The kulhads come from Mehra Brothers, a **partnership firm** of Kesar's uncles. The clay-cup moulds come from Kulhad Kraft LLP, a **limited liability partnership**. The milk comes from Malwa Dugdh Utpadak Sahakari Sanstha, a dairy **co-operative society** of 3,000 farmers. All four want Iyer & Associates.

> **Bunty:** Ma'am, Dolly ji says audit only the bank account and skip the stock.
> **Lakshmi:** She may, Bunty: the law does not make her get an audit. Get it in writing.
> **Bunty:** And can the dairy tell us what to skip?
> **Lakshmi:** No. The Registrar appoints its auditor, and the auditor reports to him.

As you move from the sole trader to the co-operative, **the client decides less and the law decides more**:

- A **sole trader** need not be audited at all; if she wants an audit, she sets its scope, so the scope must be written down.
- A **partnership firm** is audited because the partners or the deed say so. The deed is the rulebook, and the auditor protects every partner, especially the one who does not run the business.
- An **LLP** must be audited under the LLP law unless it is below the size limits; the designated partners appoint the auditor by set dates, and two returns go to the Registrar of Companies each year.
- A **co-operative society**'s auditor is appointed by the **Registrar of Co-operative Societies**, reports to him, tests obedience to the Co-operative Act (shares, loans, borrowings, investments, profits), classifies overdue debts and awards an audit class. Only a chartered accountant may audit a **multi-state** co-operative.

::: analogy Who sends the umpire
Gully cricket: you pick the umpire and decide which rules count. A club match: the club rulebook binds both sides, and the umpire checks nobody's prize share was cheated. A league match: above a certain level an umpire must be appointed in time, and the scorecard filed by a deadline. A State-association match: the association sends the umpire, and he reports to it, not to the captain.
:::

::: key
The further from a sole trader, the less the client decides: a proprietor sets the scope (so write it down), a firm's deed governs, the LLP Act and Rules fix an LLP's audit and dates, and the Registrar appoints a co-operative's auditor, who reports to him.
:::

### 1. Sole trader

A sole trader is under **no legal obligation** to have the accounts audited. Many get them audited because of regulatory requirements (for example inventory brokers) or on the specific instructions of a bank for approval of loans (SM 9.32).

- **Appointment**: the auditor is appointed by the sole proprietor; on a change of auditor, the incoming auditor must communicate with the previous auditor.
- **Scope**: the proprietor determines the scope and conditions: for example, only a **partial audit**, certain parts of the accounts left unchecked, the auditor also to **prepare the final statements**, or a **continuous or year-end** audit.
- So the appointment should be **in writing** and clearly define the scope, to prevent misunderstanding. If it is not, the auditor should **write to the client** explaining the scope of duties and stating the limitations on the work, to obtain the client's confirmation (SM 9.33; see [[aud.ethics.sa210|terms of audit engagements]]).

A partnership audit's advantages and procedure apply equally to a proprietorship (SM 9.33).

### 2. Partnership firm

**Appointment.** Usually by the partners, by their decision or under a condition in the partnership agreement; they fix the remuneration. The letter of appointment should clearly state the nature and scope of the audit and any limitations; an incoming auditor communicates with the previous auditor (SM 9.33).

**Accounting standards.** The auditor may particularly ensure the application of the accounting standards prescribed by ICAI. Where the firm must get its accounts audited under a statute, the auditor will have to **qualify the report** for non-compliance with the accounting standards; alternatively, the Study Material says, disclosure of the non-compliance alone would be sufficient, without a qualification (SM 9.33–9.34).

**Before starting, examine the partnership agreement** for these matters (SM 9.34–9.35):

| | Matter in the partnership agreement |
|---|---|
| 1 | Name and style of the business |
| 2 | Duration of the partnership, if agreed |
| 3 | Capital of each partner: fixed or variable |
| 4 | Accounting period; profit-sharing and loss-bearing ratios; any partner exempt from losses |
| 5 | Books of account; what is deducted before divisible profit (reserves, depreciation); period for reopening accounts for a manifest error |
| 6 | Borrowing capacity (when not implied, as in non-trading firms) |
| 7 | Interest on capitals and loans; interest on drawings and current accounts |
| 8 | Partners' salaries; drawings against shares of profit, and their extent |
| 9 | Partners' management duties; the managing partners |
| 10 | Who operates the bank account; investment of surplus funds |
| 11 | Agreed limits on partners' rights and powers, and on their implied authority to pledge the firm's credit or render it liable |

**Advantages of audit of a partnership firm** (SM 9.35):

1. **Disputes**: a reliable means of settling accounts between partners; usually binding on them unless a manifest error comes to light within a specified period.
2. **Dissolution**: on retirement or death, reliable evidence for the amount due for capital, profits and goodwill.
3. **Reliable**: relied on by banks when lending and by prospective purchasers.
4. **Admission**: help negotiations to admit a partner, especially for several past years.
5. **Control**: a safeguard against undue advantage taken by a working partner, especially for partners not active in the firm.

**Matters specially considered in the audit of a partnership** (SM 9.35–9.36):

1. **Letter of Appointment**: signed by a partner duly authorised, clearly stating the nature and scope of audit and any limitation.
2. **Partnership Documents**: the minute book, especially minutes authorising extraordinary and capital expenditure, raising of loans, purchase of assets and extraordinary contracts.
3. **Objects of Partnership**: the business carried on is authorised by the partnership agreement, or by an extension or modification agreed later.
4. **Books of Account**: reasonable and adequate for the nature of the business.
5. **Mutual Interest**: ==the interest of no partner has suffered prejudicially== by an activity the firm was not authorised to do under the deed, or by any violation of the agreement.
6. **Provision for Taxes**: the firm's tax is provided for before arriving at the profit divisible among partners.
7. **Division of Profits**: profits and losses are divided in the agreed profit-sharing ratio.

The object of examining the agreement is that the auditor may **report to the partners** if any partner's interest has been prejudicially affected (SM 9.36). When partners change during the year, profits up to the retirement date follow the old terms and later profits the new deed (SM 9.92).

### 3. Limited liability partnership (LLP)

An LLP is governed by the **Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008**. It combines the advantages of the company and partnership forms. At least **2 partners** can form an LLP, and at least two must be **designated partners**, who take a **DPIN** (Designated Partner Identification Number); partners' rights and duties are governed by agreement between them (SM 9.37).

::: definition Small Limited Liability Partnership
An LLP (a) whose contribution does not exceed twenty-five lakh rupees (₹25,00,000) or such higher amount, not exceeding five crore rupees, as may be prescribed; and (b) whose turnover, as per the Statement of Accounts and Solvency for the immediately preceding financial year, does not exceed forty lakh rupees (₹40,00,000) or such higher amount, not exceeding fifty crore rupees, as may be prescribed (SM 9.37). The audit provisions do not apply to a small LLP, whose financial statements can be signed by the designated partners alone (SM 9.90).
:::

**Books of account.** An LLP must maintain annual accounts reflecting a true and fair view, with books containing (SM 9.37): (1) particulars of all sums received and expended and the matters to which they relate; (2) a record of assets and liabilities; (3) statements of cost of goods purchased, inventories, work-in-progress, finished goods and cost of goods sold; (4) any other particulars the partners decide.

**Audit.** The accounts of every LLP shall be audited in accordance with **Rule 24 of the LLP Rules, 2009**. The rule provides that any LLP whose turnover does not exceed, in any financial year, **forty lakh rupees**, or whose contribution does not exceed **twenty-five lakh rupees**, is not required to get its accounts audited; but if its partners decide to get them audited, the audit shall be only in accordance with the rule (SM 9.37).

::: warning One rule, two readings
As the Study Material words Rule 24 (SM 9.37, 9.89–9.90), an LLP is exempt when its turnover **or** its contribution is within the limit. ICAI's MTP January 2025 Series II MCQ 5 keyed the other reading: an LLP "is always required to conduct an audit if either the turnover exceeds ₹40 lakhs or the capital contribution exceeds ₹25 lakhs", so it is exempt only when **both** are within (as for a small LLP). No other ICAI question read decides between the two. With one figure above its limit and one within, quote the rule and state your reading. Verify the limits against the Study Material and Statutory Update for your attempt.
:::

**Advantages of an LLP audit** (SM 9.38): (1) **detection of errors** and frauds and verification of the financial statements; (2) settling **disputes** between partners; (3) **reliability**: banks and financial institutions lend on audited accounts; (4) **better compliance and management** from the auditor's visits and suggestions; (5) **reconstitution**: settling accounts on admission, death, retirement, insolvency, insanity, etc.

**Returns** (SM 9.38): the **annual return in Form 11** is filed with the ROC **within 60 days of the close of the financial year** (open to public inspection on payment of fees); the **Statement of Account and Solvency in Form 8** is filed **within thirty days from the end of six months** of the financial year to which it relates.

**Appointment of auditor** by the **designated partners** (SM 9.38): (1) at any time for the first financial year, but before the end of that year; (2) at least **thirty days before the end** of each later financial year; (3) to fill a casual vacancy; (4) to fill a casual vacancy caused by removal. The **partners** may appoint the auditor if the designated partners have failed to.

**Auditor's duty regarding audit of an LLP** (SM 9.39):

1. **Engagement Letter**: definite instructions in writing as to the work to be performed.
2. **Minutes Book**: refer to any resolution on the accounts, if partners maintain one.
3. **LLP Agreement**: note (a) nature of the business; (b) capital contributed by each partner; (c) interest on additional capital contributed; (d) duration of partnership; (e) drawings allowed; (f) salaries, commission etc. payable to partners; (g) borrowing powers; (h) rights and duties of partners; (i) method of settling accounts on admission, retirement, etc.; (j) loans advanced by partners; (k) profit-sharing ratio.
4. **Reporting**: mention (a) whether the records appear correct and reliable; (b) whether all information and explanations necessary were obtained; (c) whether any restriction was imposed.

### 4. Co-operative societies

A co-operative is "an autonomous association of persons united voluntarily to meet their common economic, social, and cultural needs and aspirations through a jointly-owned enterprise". The **Co-operative Societies Act, 1912**, a Central Act, contains the fundamental law and applies in many States with or without amendments; States such as Maharashtra, West Bengal and Orissa have their own Acts, so the auditor must know the Act governing the society. A chartered accountant can also offer (1) guidance in accounts writing, (2) installation of the accounting system, (3) internal audit, (4) management accounting services and (5) taxation (SM 9.59).

**Section 17 of the 1912 Act** (SM 9.60): (1) the **Registrar shall audit or cause to be audited**, by a person authorised by him by general or special order in writing, the accounts of every registered society **at least once every year**; (2) the audit shall include an **examination of overdue debts**, if any, and a **valuation of the assets and liabilities**; (3) the Registrar, the Collector or a person authorised by the Registrar shall at all times have access to all books, accounts, papers and securities, and every officer shall furnish the information required.

**Points to keep in mind** (SM 9.60–9.64):

1. **Qualifications**: besides a chartered accountant, some State Acts permit holders of a government diploma in co-operative accounts (or co-operation and accountancy) and persons who served as auditors in the government's co-operative department.
2. **Appointment**: by the **Registrar**; the auditor audits on the Registrar's behalf and reports to him and to the society. The society pays fees on the **statutory scale prescribed by the Registrar** for its category.
3. **Books**: under **section 43(h)**, a **State Government** can frame rules prescribing the books. In Maharashtra the Registrar's instructions require records of (i) all sums received and expended and the matters to which they relate, (ii) all sales and purchases of goods, (iii) assets and liabilities. These are not conclusive: the society may keep more. A large society keeps subsidiary books whose daily totals go to the main Cash Book, for example (a) a daily cash sales summary register, (b) a register of collections from debtors where bye-laws allow credit sales, (c) a register of recovery of loans from salaries and directly from members (credit society), (d) a loan disbursement register (credit society), (e) other columnar subsidiaries.
4. **Shareholding (section 5)**: where members' liability is limited, no member other than a registered society may hold more than **20% of the total shares**, or shares of a value over **₹1,000**. The auditor watches for breaches, and for bye-laws contrary to this; State Acts may set other limits.
5. **Loans (section 29)**: a society shall **not lend to a non-member**; with the **special sanction of the Registrar**, it may lend to another registered society. The State Government may add restrictions.
6. **Borrowings (section 30)**: loans and deposits from **non-members** only subject to the restrictions and limits of the **bye-laws**.
7. **Investment of funds (section 32)**: only in (a) the Central or State Co-operative Bank; (b) securities specified in section 20 of the Indian Trusts Act, 1882; (c) shares, securities, bonds or debentures of another society with limited liability; (d) any other co-operative bank approved by the Registrar on specified terms; (e) any other mode permitted by the Central or State Government.
8. **Appropriation of profits (section 33)**: a **prescribed percentage** of profits goes to the **Reserve Fund** before any dividend or bonus.
9. **Charitable contributions (section 34)**: with the **Registrar's sanction**, up to **10% of the net profits remaining after the compulsory transfer to the reserve fund**, for a charitable purpose as defined in section 2 of the Charitable Endowments Act, 1890.
10. **Reserve Fund**: some State Acts allow it to be used (a) as working capital in the business, (b) invested as per the Act, (c) for public purposes likely to promote the society's objects; the auditor ensures strict compliance.
11. **Education Fund**: some State Acts require an annual contribution to the Education Fund of the State Federal Society; it is ==a charge on profits and not an appropriation==.

The **General Body**, the supreme authority, must approve appropriations (other reserves, dividends), and the **entries are passed after the date of its approval**: a departure from corporate practice, where proposed appropriations are booked subject to the AGM. Where a State Act calls transfers to a Dividend Equalisation Reserve or Share Capital Redemption Fund "charges", the auditor points out that under generally accepted accounting principles they are appropriations (SM 9.63–9.64).

**Special features of co-operative audit** (SM 9.64–9.67):

1. **Examination of overdue debts**: classify overdue debts for **6 months to 5 years** and **more than 5 years** and report them; analyse their chances of recovery as good or bad; see that provision for doubtful debts is adequate.
2. **Overdue interest**: exclude it from interest outstanding and accrued while calculating profit; in practice an **overdue interest reserve** is created.
3. **Certification of bad debts**: under the Maharashtra State Co-operative Rules, 1961, bad debts can be written off only when **certified as bad by the auditor**; where no such requirement exists, the **managing committee** must authorise the write-off.
4. **Valuation of assets and liabilities**: no specific provisions, so general principles apply: existence, ownership and valuation; fixed assets at cost less adequate depreciation (incidental and installation costs capitalised; a note of present market value where the difference is significant); current assets at the lower of cost and market price; all known liabilities brought in; contingent liabilities by note.
5. **Adherence to co-operative principles**: how far the objects have been achieved, judged by benefits to members rather than profit; expenses economically incurred, middlemen's commission avoided; the principles of [[aud.entities.govt|propriety audit]] are followed.
6. **Observation of the Act and Rules**: point out and report infringements of the Act, Rules and bye-laws, assessing their financial implications; note restrictions on dividends.
7. **Members' Register and pass books**: examine loan entries in members' pass books and confirm balances in person, on a test basis; a safeguard, especially in rural credit societies whose members may not be literate.
8. **Special report to the Registrar** on serious irregularities, for example: (i) personal profiteering by managing committee members detrimental to the society; (ii) fraud in expenses, purchases, property and stores; (iii) specific mismanagement, decisions against co-operative principles; (iv) in urban co-operative banks, disproportionate advances to vested interests such as relatives of management, negligent recovery, and reckless advancing without adequate security.
9. **Audit classification**: the auditor awards the society a class on criteria specified by the Registrar; the management may **appeal to the Registrar**, who may direct a review.
10. **Discussion of draft audit report**: the auditor asks the **Secretary** to convene a **managing committee** meeting; the report is never finalised without this discussion; minor irregularities are rectified, policy matters discussed.

### 5. Multi-State co-operative societies

The **Multi-State Co-operative Societies Act, 2002** (in force from August 2002) applies to co-operative societies whose objects are not confined to one State; their funds cannot be used for any political purpose. Under the Multi-State Co-operative Society Rules, 2002, books are kept of (a) all sums received and expended and the matters concerned; (b) all sales and purchases of goods; (c) assets and liabilities; (d) for a society in production, processing and manufacturing, the utilisation of materials, labour and other items of cost as specified in its bye-laws (SM 9.67–9.68).

- **Qualification (section 72)**: **only a chartered accountant** within the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949. Not eligible: (a) a body corporate; (b) an officer or employee of the society; (c) a person who is a member, or in the employment, of an officer or employee of the society; (d) a person indebted to the society, or who has given a guarantee or security for a third person's debt to it, for **more than one thousand rupees**. An auditor who becomes disqualified after appointment is **deemed to have vacated** office (SM 9.68–9.69).
- **Appointment (section 70)**: the **first auditor** by the **board within one month of registration**, holding office until the conclusion of the first AGM; if the board fails, the society in general meeting appoints. **Subsequent auditors** are appointed at each AGM, from its conclusion to the conclusion of the next (SM 9.69).
- **Powers and duties (section 73)**: access at all times to books, accounts and vouchers, at the head office or elsewhere, and the right to require information and explanations. The auditor inquires (a) whether loans and advances on security are properly secured and on terms not prejudicial to the society or its members; (b) whether transactions represented merely by book entries are not prejudicial; (c) whether personal expenses have been charged to revenue account; (d) where shares are stated to be allotted for cash, whether cash was actually received, and if not, whether the books and balance sheet are correct, regular and not misleading (SM 9.69–9.70).
- **Report (section 73(3)–(4))**: to the **members**, on the accounts and every balance sheet, profit and loss account and annexed document laid before the general meeting, stating whether they give the information required by the Act and a true and fair view of the state of affairs at the year end and of the profit or loss for the year. It also states whether (i) all information and explanations were obtained; (ii) proper books were kept and proper returns received from branches not visited; (iii) the report on any branch audited by another person was forwarded and how it was dealt with; (iv) the balance sheet and profit and loss account agree with the books and returns. Any negative or qualified answer must give reasons (SM 9.70–9.71).

## Worked examples

:::: example Mehra Brothers changes its partners
Mehra Brothers had partners A, B and C sharing profits 3:2:1. C, a sleeping partner, retired on 30 September 2025, and D joined on 1 October 2025; the new deed shares profits among A, B and D in the ratio 2:2:1. Both deeds forbid a partner to pledge the firm's credit without the consent of all partners. The profit for 2025-26, after providing for the firm's tax, is ₹36,00,000, earned evenly through the year. The accountant has divided the whole ₹36,00,000 in the new ratio. In January 2026, A signed a guarantee of ₹40,00,000 on the firm's behalf for a bank loan to his son's company, without asking B or D. The engagement letter was signed by A's son, the office manager, who is not a partner.

Required: compute the correct division of profit and comment on the matters the auditor should consider.

::: answer Model answer
**Division of profit for 2025-26**

| Partner | 1 April to 30 September (3:2:1) ₹ | 1 October to 31 March (2:2:1) ₹ | Correct share ₹ | As divided ₹ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 9,00,000 | 7,20,000 | 16,20,000 | 14,40,000 |
| B | 6,00,000 | 7,20,000 | 13,20,000 | 14,40,000 |
| C | 3,00,000 | — | 3,00,000 | — |
| D | — | 3,60,000 | 3,60,000 | 7,20,000 |
| **Total** | **18,00,000** | **18,00,000** | **36,00,000** | **36,00,000** |

**Change in constitution.** The auditor considers the retirement deed and partnership deed for the dates, and ensures that profits up to retirement are distributed under the old terms and profits after it as per the new deed. The new-ratio division denies C ₹3,00,000 and A ₹1,80,000 and overpays B ₹1,20,000 and D ₹3,60,000; it must be corrected.

**Guarantee by A.** The auditor must verify that the interest of no partner has suffered prejudicially by an activity the firm was not authorised to do or by any violation of a provision of the partnership agreement. A's guarantee breaches the deed's restriction on pledging the firm's credit and exposes B and D to a liability of up to ₹40,00,000. The auditor should see that it is disclosed as a contingent liability and report the violation to the partners.

**Letter of appointment.** The auditor should confirm that the letter of appointment is signed by a partner duly authorised and clearly states the nature and scope of the audit and any limitation. A letter signed by a non-partner does not meet this; the auditor should obtain one signed by an authorised partner before proceeding.
:::

:::: example Kulhad Kraft LLP and the locked minute book
Kulhad Kraft LLP, in its third financial year, makes Tapri's clay-cup moulds. For 2025-26 its contribution is ₹30,00,000 and its turnover ₹1.6 crore. On 20 February 2026 the designated partners appointed CA Neha Joshi as auditor; the engagement letter, signed by designated partner Arjun, says: "Audit sales and bank only. Partners' accounts are a private matter." The LLP agreement allows interest at 9% a year on additional capital contributed, drawings of up to ₹50,000 a month per partner, and interest at 12% a year on loans advanced by partners. Neha finds:

(i) Partner Bela contributed additional capital of ₹10,00,000 on 1 October 2025; no interest has been credited to her.

(ii) Arjun drew ₹9,00,000 during the year.

(iii) A loan of ₹5,00,000 advanced by partner Chetan on 1 April 2025 is shown as his capital, with no interest provided.

(iv) Arjun refuses to show her the partners' minute book.

Required: state whether the audit is compulsory and the appointment valid; quantify items (i) to (iii); advise CA Neha on her duties and her report.

::: answer Model answer
**Audit requirement.** As per Rule 24 of the LLP Rules, 2009, the accounts of every LLP shall be audited; an LLP whose turnover does not exceed ₹40 lakh in any financial year, or whose contribution does not exceed ₹25 lakh, is not required to get them audited. Turnover of ₹1.6 crore and contribution of ₹30 lakh both exceed the limits, so the audit is compulsory on either reading of the rule.

**Appointment.** For a year other than the first, the designated partners appoint the auditor at least thirty days before the end of the financial year: for 2025-26, by 1 March 2026. The appointment on 20 February 2026 is valid.

**Working: amounts under the LLP agreement**

| Item | Computation | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|---:|
| (i) Interest due on Bela's additional capital | 10,00,000 × 9% × 6/12 | 45,000 |
| (ii) Drawings allowed to Arjun | 50,000 × 12 | 6,00,000 |
| (ii) Drawings in excess of the agreement | 9,00,000 − 6,00,000 | 3,00,000 |
| (iii) Interest due on Chetan's loan | 5,00,000 × 12% | 60,000 |

**Engagement letter.** The auditor should get definite instructions in writing as to the work to be performed. This letter excludes the partners' accounts; Neha should ask for written instructions without that limitation, and report any restriction that remains.

**LLP agreement.** The auditor reads the agreement and notes, among other provisions, each partner's capital, interest on additional capital, drawings allowed and loans advanced by partners. Against it: (i) interest of ₹45,000 must be credited to Bela; (ii) Arjun has drawn ₹3,00,000 more than the agreement allows, a breach to be brought to the partners' notice; (iii) Chetan's ₹5,00,000 is a loan, not capital, and must be shown as a loan with interest of ₹60,000 provided.

**Minutes book.** If partners maintain a minute book, the auditor refers to it for any resolution passed regarding the accounts; withholding it denies her that evidence.

**Reporting.** Her report should mention whether the records appear correct and reliable (not, until items (i) and (iii) are corrected), whether she obtained all the information and explanations necessary (no: the minute book was withheld) and whether any restriction was imposed on her (yes: the scope limitation and the refusal).

::::: example The dairy's profit and its five problems
Malwa Dugdh Utpadak Sahakari Sanstha is a dairy co-operative governed by the Co-operative Societies Act, 1912; its State's rules prescribe a transfer of 25% of profits to the Reserve Fund. The auditor appointed by the Registrar finds, for 2025-26:

(i) Net profit per the accounts is ₹48,00,000, after crediting ₹2,40,000 of interest on members' loans whose principal is overdue, and before a contribution of ₹1,20,000 to the Education Fund of the State Federal Society, which is shown as an appropriation.

(ii) The managing committee proposes ₹4,00,000 for flood relief, and the Registrar's sanction has been applied for.

(iii) ₹8,00,000 was lent to a dairy-equipment dealer who is not a member, without any sanction.

(iv) A dividend of 8% on share capital was entered as a liability on 31 March 2026; the General Body meets in September 2026.

(v) Members' loans of ₹30,80,000 are overdue: ₹18,50,000 for up to 6 months, ₹9,20,000 for 6 months to 5 years, ₹3,10,000 for more than 5 years.

Required: compute the correct profit, the transfer to the Reserve Fund and the maximum charitable contribution; comment on each item.

:::: answer Model answer
**Working: profit and appropriations**

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---:|
| Net profit per accounts | 48,00,000 |
| Less: overdue interest wrongly credited | (2,40,000) |
| Less: Education Fund contribution (a charge) | (1,20,000) |
| **Correct net profit** | **44,40,000** |
| Transfer to Reserve Fund at 25% | 11,10,000 |
| Net profit remaining after the compulsory transfer | 33,30,000 |
| **Maximum charitable contribution: 10% of ₹33,30,000** | **3,33,000** |

**(i) Overdue interest and Education Fund.** Overdue interest must be excluded from interest outstanding and accrued while calculating profit, and the Education Fund contribution is a charge on profits, not an appropriation. The section 33 Reserve Fund transfer, made before any dividend, is therefore ₹11,10,000.

**(ii) Charitable contribution.** Under section 34, a society may, with the sanction of the Registrar, contribute not more than 10% of the net profits remaining after the compulsory transfer to the reserve fund. ₹4,00,000 exceeds the maximum of ₹3,33,000 by ₹67,000, which even the Registrar's sanction cannot cover.

**(iii) Loan to a non-member.** Under section 29, a society shall not lend to any person other than a member (only to another registered society, with the Registrar's special sanction). The ₹8,00,000 loan infringes the Act; the auditor assesses its recoverability and reports the infringement and its financial implications, if serious in a special report to the Registrar.

**(iv) Dividend.** Appropriations of profit must be approved by the General Body, and the entries are passed after the date of its approval, unlike corporate practice. The 31 March 2026 entry, made before the September meeting, should be reversed and passed after approval.

**(v) Overdue debts.** ₹9,20,000 (6 months to 5 years) and ₹3,10,000 (more than 5 years) must be classified and reported, analysed as good or bad and adequately provided for; bad debts are written off only when certified by the auditor where the law so requires, otherwise with the managing committee's authority.

## Answer frame

### A case on a co-operative society or LLP

1. **Relevant provision**: the section and its words ("As per section 29 of the Co-operative Societies Act, 1912, …").
2. **Facts of the case**: the figures or dates that decide it.
3. **Analysis**: compute where needed (the 10% cap, the deadline date); compare.
4. **Conclusion**: permissible or not; what the auditor does (report, special report to the Registrar, ask for correction, qualify).

### "Auditor's duty regarding audit of an LLP" (4 marks)

The four heads in order, each with its content: engagement letter; minutes book; LLP agreement (a)–(k); reporting (records correct and reliable, information obtained, restrictions). ICAI's September 2025 answer is exactly these; an answer that stops at the agreement loses the reporting mark.

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: the sole trader must be audited
Bunty writes that a proprietor "needs an audit under law". **Fix:** a sole trader is under no legal obligation; the audit serves regulators or banks, and the proprietor sets the scope, so put it in writing (SM 9.32–9.33).
:::

::: mistake Charity before the reserve
Computing a co-operative's 10% charitable limit on the whole net profit. **Fix:** 10% of the net profits **remaining after the compulsory transfer to the reserve fund**, and only with the Registrar's sanction (SM 9.63).
:::

::: mistake AGM appoints the first multi-state auditor
**Fix:** the **board** appoints the first auditor within one month of registration; the general meeting does so only if the board fails, and the AGM appoints the subsequent auditors (SM 9.69, 9.90).
:::

## Recap

- Sole trader: no legal obligation to audit; the proprietor appoints and sets the scope, so the scope and limitations go in writing (or a letter to the client for confirmation).
- Partnership: partners appoint (decision or deed); study the deed's 11 matters; seven special matters; report if any partner's interest is prejudiced.
- LLP: audit under Rule 24 unless turnover ≤ ₹40 lakh or contribution ≤ ₹25 lakh (SM wording, though ICAI's MTP key reads it as audit if either is exceeded); designated partners appoint (first year before its end, later years 30 days before year end), partners if they fail.
- LLP returns: Form 11 within 60 days of year end; Form 8 within 30 days after six months; auditor's duty: engagement letter, minutes, agreement (a)–(k), reporting.
- Co-operative: Registrar audits or appoints (section 17); shares 20% or ₹1,000; loans only to members; section 32 investments only; reserve before dividend; charity ≤ 10% after reserve; Education Fund a charge.
- Co-operative audit: overdue debts (6 months–5 years, over 5 years), overdue interest out of profit, bad debts certified, special report to the Registrar, audit class, draft discussed with the managing committee.
- Multi-State: CA only; board appoints first auditor within a month; over ₹1,000 indebtedness disqualifies; section 77 special audit needs 51% government capital; inquiry and inspection on 15 days' notice. (The umpire is sent by the association, not chosen by the captain.)

## Flashcards

Q:: Is a sole trader legally required to get the accounts audited? Why do many do so?
A:: No. Many do because of regulatory requirements (e.g. inventory brokers) or a bank's instructions for approval of loans.

Q:: Sole trader audit: what should the auditor do if the appointment is not in writing?
A:: Write to the client explaining the scope of duties and stating the limitations placed on the work, to obtain the client's confirmation.

Q:: Partnership audit: state the seven matters specially considered.
A:: Letter of appointment; partnership documents (minute book); objects of partnership; books of account; mutual interest; provision for taxes; division of profits.

Q:: Partnership audit: name the five advantages of audit.
A:: Disputes, dissolution, reliable (banks, purchasers), admission of partners, control over working partners.

Q:: LLP: when is an LLP not required to get its accounts audited under Rule 24?
A:: As the Study Material words it: when its turnover does not exceed ₹40 lakh in any financial year, or its contribution does not exceed ₹25 lakh. (ICAI's MTP January 2025 keyed audit as compulsory if either limit is exceeded.)

Q:: LLP: deadlines for Form 11 and Form 8?
A:: Form 11 (annual return) within 60 days of the close of the financial year; Form 8 (Statement of Account and Solvency) within 30 days from the end of six months of the financial year.

Q:: LLP: when must the designated partners appoint the auditor?
A:: For the first year, at any time before its end; for later years, at least 30 days before the end of the year; also to fill casual vacancies (including by removal). Partners may appoint if the designated partners fail.

Q:: Co-operative Societies Act, 1912, section 17: what must the audit include?
A:: An examination of overdue debts, if any, and a valuation of the assets and liabilities of the society.

Q:: Co-operative society: limit on charitable contributions under section 34?
A:: With the Registrar's sanction, not more than 10% of the net profits remaining after the compulsory transfer to the reserve fund.

Q:: Co-operative audit: how are overdue debts classified for reporting?
A:: From 6 months to 5 years, and more than 5 years; then analysed as good or bad for recovery.

Q:: Multi-State co-operative society: who appoints the first auditor, and when?
A:: The board, within one month of the date of registration (holding office until the conclusion of the first AGM); if the board fails, the society in general meeting.

Q:: Multi-State co-operative society: when can the Central Government order a special audit under section 77?
A:: When it is of the opinion that the society is not managed per co-operative or sound business principles, is managed so as to seriously injure its trade or industry, or its financial position endangers solvency; and only if the Central and/or State Government hold 51% or more of the paid-up share capital.
