## In plain words

### The café where Raju does everything

At Tapri's busiest kulhad café in Bhopal, one man, Raju, takes the orders, collects the cash, writes the day's sales book and deposits the money when he finds time. Kesar trusts him completely. Lakshmi does not trust the arrangement.

> **Kesar:** Raju has been with me since the first tapri. Nobody checks Raju.
> **Lakshmi:** That is exactly what worries me, Kesar-ji. If nobody checks Raju, my team has to check everything Raju touched.
> **Bunty:** So the café's own checks decide how much work we do?
> **Lakshmi:** Now you are thinking like an auditor.

**Internal control** is the set of guardrails a business builds for itself: who may approve, who records, who holds the cash, who checks, and whether anyone notices when a check stops working. Tapri's board, managers and staff design and run it. The auditor does not build it; the auditor **understands** it, because it shows where the accounts could go wrong and how much testing each area needs.

Good guardrails have five parts: the culture at the top, a way of spotting risks, a system that carries transactions to the accounts, the day-to-day checks, and someone watching whether those checks still work. Even the best set gives only **reasonable** assurance: people make mistakes, two people can collude, and the boss can walk round any rule. To understand the system, the auditor records it as a **narrative**, a **check list**, an **internal control questionnaire** or a **flow chart**.

::: analogy The exam hall
A board exam hall runs on internal control. The principal's "no cheating, ever" attitude is the control environment; deciding which centres are most at risk of leaks is risk assessment; roll lists and the notice board are the information system and communication; invigilators checking admit cards, and keeping the paper-setter apart from the evaluator, are control activities; the flying squad checking whether invigilators invigilate is monitoring. It still fails when two invigilators collude, or the principal opens the sealed packet early.
:::

::: key
Internal control is designed, implemented and maintained by those charged with governance, management and other personnel, and gives only reasonable assurance. The auditor understands and evaluates the internal control relevant to the audit, to design the nature, timing and extent of further audit procedures. Maintaining it is never the auditor's job.
:::

## How it works

### 1. What internal control is

::: definition Internal control (SA 315)
"The process designed, implemented and maintained by those charged with governance, management and other personnel to provide ==reasonable assurance about the achievement of an entity's objectives== with regard to reliability of financial reporting, effectiveness and efficiency of operations, safeguarding of assets, and compliance with applicable laws and regulations. The term "controls" refers to any aspects of one or more of the components of internal control." (SM 3.29–3.30)
:::

So internal control addresses business risks that threaten the entity's objectives concerning (SM 3.30): the reliability of financial reporting; the effectiveness and efficiency of operations; compliance with applicable laws and regulations; and safeguarding of assets. How it is designed and maintained **varies with the entity's size and complexity**.

The auditor shall obtain an understanding of internal control relevant to the audit (SM 3.32). It assists the auditor in (SM 3.30): (i) identifying types of potential misstatements; (ii) identifying factors that affect the risks of material misstatement; and (iii) designing the nature, timing and extent of further audit procedures. Weak controls mean higher **control risk**, one of the two components of the [[aud.risk.model|risk of material misstatement]].

### 2. The five components

The division of internal control into five components gives auditors a useful framework to consider how its different aspects may affect the audit (SM 3.32):

#### (A) The control environment

The auditor shall obtain an understanding of the control environment and, as part of it, shall **evaluate whether** (SM 3.33): (i) management has created and maintained a **culture of honesty and ethical behaviour**; and (ii) the strengths in the control environment elements collectively provide an **appropriate foundation** for the other components. It includes the governance and management functions and the attitudes, awareness and actions of those charged with governance and management; it **sets the tone of an organisation**, influencing the control consciousness of its people.

Elements that may be relevant (SM 3.33–3.35):

| Element | What the auditor looks at |
|---|---|
| (a) Communication and enforcement of integrity and ethical values | Codes of conduct; removing temptations to act dishonestly |
| (b) Commitment to competence | Competence levels for jobs and the skills they need |
| (c) Participation by those charged with governance | Independence from management, experience, involvement, scrutiny |
| (d) Management's philosophy and operating style | Attitude to financial reporting, policies, estimates, business risks |
| (e) Organisational structure | Framework to plan, execute, control and review activities |
| (f) Assignment of authority and responsibility | Authorisation hierarchies, reporting relationships |
| (g) Human resource policies and practices | Recruitment, training, appraisal, promotion, remedial action |

The effectiveness of controls cannot rise above the integrity and ethical values of the people who create, administer and monitor them (SM 3.33).

**Not an absolute deterrent to fraud** (SM 3.35). A satisfactory control environment is a positive factor when the auditor assesses the risks of material misstatement and may help reduce the risk of fraud, but it is ==not an absolute deterrent to fraud==. Conversely, deficiencies in it may undermine other controls, in particular against fraud: management's failure to commit resources to IT security may allow improper changes to programs or data, or unauthorised transactions. The control environment in itself does not prevent, or detect and correct, a material misstatement; it influences the auditor's evaluation of other controls, such as monitoring and specific control activities.

#### (B) The entity's risk assessment process

The auditor shall obtain an understanding of whether the entity has a process for (SM 3.36): (a) **identifying** business risks relevant to financial reporting objectives; (b) **estimating** the significance of the risks; (c) **assessing** the likelihood of their occurrence; and (d) **deciding** about actions to address those risks. If the process is appropriate, it assists the auditor in identifying risks of material misstatement. Risks arise or change through new technology, new business models, products or activities, or changes in the operating environment; whether the process suits the circumstances is a matter of judgment.

#### (C) The information system and communication

The auditor shall obtain an understanding of the information system, including the related business processes, relevant to financial reporting, including (SM 3.36–3.37):

- (a) the classes of transactions significant to the financial statements;
- (b) the procedures by which they are initiated, recorded, processed, corrected as necessary, transferred to the general ledger and reported;
- (c) the related accounting records, supporting information and specific accounts;
- (d) how the system captures events and conditions significant to the financial statements;
- (e) the financial reporting process used to prepare the financial statements; and
- (f) controls surrounding journal entries.

An information system consists of infrastructure (physical and hardware components), software, people, procedures and data. The auditor shall also understand how the entity **communicates** financial reporting roles and responsibilities: policy manuals, accounting and financial reporting manuals, memoranda, and electronically, orally and through the actions of management (SM 3.37).

#### (D) Control activities

::: definition Control activities
The policies and procedures that help ensure that management directives are carried out (SM 3.37).
:::

The auditor understands only those control activities relevant to the audit: those over significant classes of transactions, account balances and disclosures and the relevant assertions. They generally include (SM 3.37) **performance reviews** (actual against budgets), **information processing** (arithmetical accuracy checks, program change controls), **physical controls** (security of assets) and **segregation of duties** (different people authorise, record and hold custody). Raju, in the Bhopal café, records the sales and holds the cash: two of the three jobs segregation keeps apart.

#### (E) Monitoring of controls

The auditor shall obtain an understanding of the major activities the entity uses to monitor internal control over financial reporting (SM 3.37). Monitoring assesses the effectiveness of internal control performance over time and takes remedial action; it considers whether controls are **operating as intended** and **modified as appropriate for changes in conditions**. Management monitors through ongoing activities built into regular supervision, separate evaluations, or both, and may use communications from external parties such as customer complaints and regulator comments (SM 3.38).

### 3. Limitations: why the guardrails can still fail

The Study Material lists six inherent limitations (SM 3.30–3.31):

| Limitation | What it means |
|---|---|
| (i) Only reasonable assurance | However effective, controls give only reasonable assurance about financial reporting objectives |
| (ii) Human judgment in decision-making | Judgment can be faulty; human error, e.g. in designing or changing a control |
| (iii) Lack of understanding the purpose | An exception report is not used because the reviewer does not understand it or fails to act |
| (iv) Collusion among people | Two or more people collude, or management overrides: side agreements with customers; credit-limit edit checks overridden or disabled |
| (v) Judgments by management | Management chooses the nature and extent of controls and of the risks it assumes |
| (vi) Small entities | Segregation of duties impracticable; owner-manager oversight may compensate, but the owner can override more easily |

The auditor takes the small-entity override risk into account when identifying risks of material misstatement due to fraud (SM 3.31). See also [[aud.nature.limits|inherent limitations of an audit]].

### 4. Which controls matter to the audit

Not all of an entity's controls are relevant to the auditor's risk assessment; whether a control, alone or with others, is relevant is a matter of **professional judgment** (SM 3.32, 3.38). Factors include (SM 3.39): materiality; the significance of the related risk; the size of the entity; the nature of its business, including organisation and ownership; the diversity and complexity of its operations; legal and regulatory requirements; the circumstances and the component of internal control; the nature and complexity of its systems, including service organisations; and whether, and how, the control prevents, or detects and corrects, material misstatement. The Study Material's examples (SM 3.39–3.40):

| Control | Relevant? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Passwords over cash disbursement programs and data | May be | Safeguarding, relevant to reliable financial reporting |
| Price list and sales volume data used to recompute revenue | May be | Completeness and accuracy of information, if the auditor uses it |
| Preventing excessive use of materials in production | Generally no | Operations objective only |
| An airline's automated flight-schedule controls | Ordinarily no | Efficiency of operations |

Where a statute requires the auditor to report on specific aspects of internal control, the review is broader and more detailed (SM 3.40); for companies, see [[aud.evid.ifc|internal financial controls]].

**Design, then implementation** (SM 3.40–3.41). Evaluating **design** asks whether the control is capable of effectively preventing, or detecting and correcting, material misstatements; **implementation** means the control exists and the entity is using it. Design comes first, since there is little point in assessing the implementation of an ineffective control; an improperly designed control may be a significant deficiency. The auditor inquires, observes, inspects documents and traces transactions; inquiry alone is not sufficient. Whether the control worked all year is [[aud.risk.testing|testing internal control]].

### 5. Evaluating the internal control system

The examination and evaluation of the internal control system is **an indispensable part of the overall audit programme** (SM 3.43). The review of internal controls will enable the auditor to know (SM 3.43–3.44):

1. whether errors and frauds are likely to be located in the ordinary course of operations;
2. whether an adequate internal control system is in use and operating as planned by management;
3. whether an effective internal auditing department is operating;
4. whether any administrative control bears on the audit (weak control over worker recruitment may let dummy names into the wages sheet);
5. whether the controls adequately safeguard the assets;
6. how far management is discharging its function of correct recording of transactions;
7. how reliable the reports, records and certificates to management can be;
8. the extent and depth of examination needed in different areas of accounting;
9. the appropriate audit technique and procedure in the circumstances;
10. the areas where control is weak and where it is excessive; and
11. whether worthwhile suggestions can be given to improve the system.

Without this, the programme becomes "unwieldy and unnecessarily heavy"; the worst case is a system installed but not actually in operation (SM 3.44–3.45). Where controls are weak, the auditor may add a procedure or extend tests: observing wages distribution when dummy workers may be paid, or confirming trade receivables exactly at the year-end with larger coverage (SM 3.45).

### 6. Four ways to record and evaluate the system

The auditor first determines the controls laid down by management, by reading manuals, studying organisation charts and flow charts and making enquiries, and makes written notes (SM 3.46). Then one of these is used (SM 3.46–3.51):

| Method | What it is | Remember |
|---|---|---|
| (A) Narrative record | A complete and exhaustive description of the system as found in operation, after actual testing and observation | Where no formal control system exists; suits small business |
| (B) Check list | Instructions or questions that audit staff follow or answer, initialling each | Yes / No / N.A.; studied by the Principal, Manager or Senior |
| (C) Internal control questionnaire | A comprehensive series of questions on internal control | The most widely used method |
| (D) Flow chart | A graphic presentation of each part of the system | The most concise way of recording the review |

- **Narrative record, disadvantages** (SM 3.47): it is difficult (i) to comprehend the system in operation, (ii) to identify weaknesses or gaps, and (iii) to incorporate changes from reshuffling of manpower.
- **Check list** (SM 3.47): an on-the-job requirement, framed having regard to the desirable elements of control.
- **Questionnaire, advantages** (SM 3.47): oversight or omission of significant review procedures is less likely; evaluation can be done at one time or in sections; the review is easier on an interim basis; it gives an orderly means of disclosing control defects.
- **Flow chart** (SM 3.51): minimises narrative explanation; gives a bird's eye view of the system, the flow of transactions and their integration, so weaknesses can be spotted and improvements suggested.

The questionnaire is answered by the client's staff; the check list is followed and initialled by the auditor's own staff (SM 3.47–3.48). The Study Material's check list examples: tenders called before orders; purchases on written orders; standardised and pre-numbered purchase order forms; inventory control accounts kept by persons with nothing to do with custody, receipt, inspection or purchase of inventory (SM 3.47).

### The Study Material's questionnaire extracts, in brief

ICAI has asked students to match questions to their area, so know what each area covers (SM 3.48–3.50):

- **Purchases**: centralised purchase department; approved suppliers, with more than one source for important materials; requisitions signed by authorised persons; competitive quotations; pre-numbered orders signed by authorised staff; materials received only in the Receiving Department, by people who cannot issue orders or approve invoices; materials inspected and counted, weighed or measured on receipt; pre-numbered Goods Received Notes.
- **Creditors**: invoices routed direct to Accounts and entered in a bill register; all invoices processed; quantities and values per invoices reconciled with receipts into stock records; invoices matched with GRNs and purchase orders and checked for prices, freight and terms; advance payments authorised; duplicate invoices marked; suppliers' statements compared with ledgers and differences followed up; unpaid creditors listed and reconciled.
- **Inventories**: stocks in assigned areas; comprehensive insurance (an uninsured risk only by a senior official's decision); a record of policies, reviewed periodically; an official deciding the value insured and reviewing adequacy; perpetual records for raw materials, work-in-progress, finished goods and stores, reconciled with accounts; shortages and excesses reported, investigated and adjusted with written approval; norms for stock levels.
- **Fixed assets**: capital budgets approved; authority to spend restricted to specified officials; the same controls as other purchases; spending kept within the amount authorised; periodic verification under a written procedure, with reports showing damaged or obsolete items; discrepancies investigated and records corrected with appropriate authority.

## Worked examples

:::: example Bunty's misfiled working papers
Bunty has filed five working papers from Tapri's 2025-26 internal control review, each under a component heading. Lakshmi returns all five.

| Paper | Bunty filed it under |
|---|---|
| (a) Minutes in which the audit committee, all independent directors, question the CFO on why the café breakage estimate was changed, and ask for the evidence behind it | Monitoring of controls |
| (b) Tapri's accounting manual and a monthly memo telling café managers what they must record before the day-end close, and who signs off | Control environment |
| (c) The ERP check that rejects any café sales entry whose total does not equal the sum of its lines | Information system |
| (d) Management's review of 1,800 customer complaints about wrong cashback amounts on the app, used to find where the cashback controls had stopped working, and the fixes made | Risk assessment process |
| (e) A finance committee paper on how the new e-invoicing rules could cause errors in recording revenue, estimating how significant and how likely each is, and deciding to add a daily reconciliation | Control activities |

Identify the correct component for each paper, and explain why Bunty's heading is wrong.

::: answer Model answer
**Relevant provision.** As per [[aud.risk.sa315|SA 315]], the auditor shall obtain an understanding of internal control relevant to the audit, which has five components: (A) the control environment; (B) the entity's risk assessment process; (C) the information system, including related business processes, relevant to financial reporting, and communication; (D) control activities relevant to the audit; and (E) monitoring of controls.

**(a) Control environment.** Participation by those charged with governance (their independence from management, their experience and stature, the extent of their involvement and scrutiny of activities) is an element of the control environment. Monitoring assesses whether controls operate effectively over time; the audit committee questioning the CFO is governance oversight, part of the tone of the organisation.

**(b) Information system and communication.** The auditor shall understand how the entity communicates financial reporting roles and responsibilities, which may take forms such as policy manuals, accounting and financial reporting manuals and memoranda. The control environment concerns attitudes, awareness and actions, not the channels through which reporting responsibilities are communicated.

**(c) Control activities.** Information processing controls, such as checks of arithmetical accuracy, are control activities: policies and procedures that help ensure management directives are carried out. The information system is the set of procedures by which transactions are initiated, recorded, processed and reported; a check built into it that stops an incorrect entry is a control activity, not the system itself.

**(d) Monitoring of controls.** Monitoring is a process to assess the effectiveness of internal control performance over time, including whether controls are operating as intended, and taking necessary remedial actions; management may use communications from external parties, such as customer complaints, which may indicate problems. The risk assessment process looks forward at business risks; this paper looks back at controls that failed.

**(e) The entity's risk assessment process.** The auditor understands whether the entity has a process for identifying business risks relevant to financial reporting objectives, estimating their significance, assessing the likelihood of their occurrence and deciding about actions to address them. The paper does all four. The reconciliation it decides on will be a control activity once in place, but the paper itself is the risk assessment process at work.

**Conclusion.** Each component must be identified from what the activity does, not from the words it uses. The understanding helps the auditor identify types of potential misstatements and design the nature, timing and extent of further audit procedures.
:::

:::: example Limitation, or no control at all?
Six lapses came to light at Tapri and its circle during 2025-26:

(a) At the Nagpur delivery hub, the supervisor who approves fuel claims and the clerk who records them agreed between themselves to pass fake claims worth ₹9.6 lakh.

(b) Scrap from the Dewas samosa factory (empty oil tins, used sacks) is sold for cash with no weighbridge slip, no scrap register, and nobody comparing scrap sales with production.

(c) After a cost study, the board decided that café petty-cash vouchers below ₹2,000 need no second review.

(d) The CFO had the IT team reopen March, a closed period, so that he could post a ₹1.2 crore expense into April "just this once".

(e) At Mehra Masala Traders, the four-person firm run by Kesar's cousin Pinky, Pinky collects the cash, records it and banks it herself.

(f) At the Indore flagship café, a trained cashier who reads the cash-shortage report every day mis-keyed a refund of ₹4,500 as ₹45,000 in the Diwali rush.

> **Kesar:** Six failures, Lakshmi-ji. Controls fail anyway, so why have them? And your audit should have caught them all.
> **Lakshmi:** Let us take them one at a time, Kesar-ji.

For each lapse, state whether it illustrates an inherent limitation of internal control (naming it) or is simply a control that does not exist, and what it means for the auditor.

::: answer Model answer
**Relevant provision.** As per SA 315, internal control, no matter how effective, can provide an entity with only reasonable assurance about achieving its financial reporting objectives, because of its inherent limitations: human judgment in decision-making can be faulty, and breakdowns can occur because of human error; information produced for control may not be used because the reviewer does not understand its purpose; controls can be circumvented by the collusion of two or more people or by inappropriate management override; management makes judgments on the nature and extent of the controls it implements and the risks it assumes; and in smaller entities segregation of duties is often not practicable, although owner-manager oversight may compensate, while the owner-manager may be more able to override controls. A deficiency in internal control, on the other hand, exists if a control is designed, implemented or operated in such a way that it is unable to prevent, or detect and correct, misstatements on a timely basis, or if the control is missing.

**Facts and analysis.**

| Lapse | Limitation or missing control | For the auditor |
|---|---|---|
| (a) Fuel claims | Collusion among people | Controls can be circumvented; consider the fraud risk in claims |
| (b) Scrap sales | No limitation: the control is missing | A deficiency in internal control; evaluate it and respond in the scrap sales area |
| (c) Petty cash under ₹2,000 | Judgments by management | A choice of the extent of control and risk; considered in risk assessment |
| (d) Reopened March | Inappropriate management override | Taken into account in identifying risks of material misstatement due to fraud |
| (e) Pinky does it all | Small entity: segregation not practicable | Owner oversight may compensate, but she can override more easily |
| (f) ₹45,000 refund | Human judgment: breakdown through human error | Even a well-run control gives only reasonable assurance |

Lapse (b) is different in kind from the other five. There, a control existed and was defeated or had its limits chosen; at Dewas there was never a control to defeat. Calling it an inherent limitation would hide a deficiency that the auditor must evaluate and respond to.

**Conclusion.** Five of the lapses show that internal control gives only reasonable assurance, not that it is useless; the sixth is a missing control. Maintaining internal control is the responsibility of management and those charged with governance, not of the auditor. Because internal control has inherent limitations, including management override, the auditor never relies on controls alone: substantive procedures are performed for each material class of transactions, account balance and disclosure (SM 3.70; [[aud.risk.sa330|SA 330]]).
:::

## Under the hood

### The classic four objectives

In a Test Your Knowledge answer, the Study Material also gives the objectives of internal control (SM 3.83): (1) transactions are executed in accordance with management's general or specific authorisation; (2) all transactions are promptly recorded in the correct amount, in the appropriate accounts and in the accounting period in which executed, so as to permit preparation of financial information within recognised accounting policies and statutory requirements, and to maintain accountability for assets; (3) assets are safeguarded from unauthorised access, use or disposition; and (4) recorded assets are compared with existing assets at reasonable intervals, with appropriate action on differences.

## Answer frame

### "Identify the component of internal control"

1. **Name it**: "CA X is gaining an understanding of the [component] component of internal control."
2. **Describe it** in the Study Material's words: what the auditor shall understand or evaluate for that component.
3. **Tie it to the facts**: which fact shows it.
4. **Why it helps**: identifying potential misstatements and designing the nature, timing and extent of further procedures.

### "Can a satisfactory control environment prevent fraud?"

The points, from SM 3.35:

| Point | What to write |
|---|---|
| Positive factor | In assessing the risks of material misstatement |
| But | May reduce the risk of fraud; not an absolute deterrent |
| Conversely | Deficiencies undermine other controls (IT security example) |
| By itself | Does not prevent, or detect and correct, a misstatement |
| Conclusion | "Fraud cannot happen" is not a correct assurance |

### "Explain the limitations of internal control"

Name and explain each limitation in the Study Material's words, match each lapse to one, and conclude that internal control gives only reasonable assurance. A bare list of names does not get full marks.

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: the auditor who maintains internal control
Bunty writes "the statutory auditor is responsible for maintaining the internal control system". **Fix:** management is, because internal control is designed, implemented and maintained by those charged with governance and management (SM 3.82). The auditor understands and evaluates it.
:::

::: mistake "Strong culture, so no fraud"
**Fix:** a satisfactory control environment is "not an absolute deterrent to fraud", and by itself it does not prevent, or detect and correct, a misstatement (SM 3.35).
:::

::: mistake Mixing up control activities and monitoring
Segregation of duties labelled "monitoring". **Fix:** control activities are the checks themselves; monitoring asks whether those checks are operating as intended over time and are modified for changes (SM 3.37–3.38).
:::

::: mistake A narrative record for a listed giant
**Fix:** the narrative record suits small businesses and cases with no formal control system, and makes weaknesses hard to identify (SM 3.46–3.47). A large entity's formal systems call for the questionnaire (the most widely used method) or a flow chart (the most concise record).
:::

::: mistake The questionnaire where "Yes" means trouble
"Do staff ever buy from unapproved suppliers?" **Fix:** frame each question so that "Yes" denotes a satisfactory position and "No" suggests a weakness, with space to explain every "No" (SM 3.48).
:::

## Recap

- Internal control (SA 315): a process by those charged with governance, management and others; reasonable assurance on financial reporting, operations, safeguarding of assets and compliance.
- Understanding it helps identify potential misstatements and risk factors, and design further audit procedures.
- Five components, A to E: control environment; risk assessment process; information system and communication; control activities; monitoring of controls.
- Control environment: culture of honesty and a foundation for the rest; seven elements; not an absolute deterrent to fraud.
- Six limitations: reasonable assurance only, human judgment, not understanding the purpose, collusion and override, management's judgments, small entities.
- Relevance of a control is the auditor's judgment; design is evaluated before implementation; inquiry alone is not enough.
- Evaluation is an indispensable part of the audit programme: narrative record, check list, questionnaire (most widely used), flow chart (most concise).
- Management maintains internal control; the auditor evaluates it. (Raju may keep the café, but not the cash, the book and the bank slip all at once.)

## Flashcards

Q:: SA 315: define internal control.
A:: The process designed, implemented and maintained by those charged with governance, management and other personnel to provide reasonable assurance about the achievement of an entity's objectives with regard to reliability of financial reporting, effectiveness and efficiency of operations, safeguarding of assets, and compliance with applicable laws and regulations.

Q:: Name the five components of internal control, in ICAI's order.
A:: (A) Control environment; (B) the entity's risk assessment process; (C) the information system, including related business processes, relevant to financial reporting, and communication; (D) control activities; (E) monitoring of controls.

Q:: Control environment: what two things shall the auditor evaluate?
A:: Whether (i) management has created and maintained a culture of honesty and ethical behaviour; and (ii) the strengths in the control environment elements collectively provide an appropriate foundation for the other components of internal control.

Q:: List the seven elements of the control environment.
A:: Communication and enforcement of integrity and ethical values; commitment to competence; participation by those charged with governance; management's philosophy and operating style; organisational structure; assignment of authority and responsibility; human resource policies and practices.

Q:: Entity's risk assessment process: what process does the auditor look for?
A:: A process for identifying business risks relevant to financial reporting objectives, estimating their significance, assessing the likelihood of their occurrence, and deciding about actions to address them.

Q:: Which control activities are generally relevant to the audit?
A:: Policies and procedures relating to performance reviews, information processing, physical controls and segregation of duties.

Q:: State the six inherent limitations of internal control.
A:: Only reasonable assurance; faulty human judgment and human error; lack of understanding of a control's purpose; collusion among people or management override; management's judgments on the nature and extent of controls and risks; limited segregation of duties in small entities.

Q:: Is a satisfactory control environment an absolute deterrent to fraud?
A:: No. It may help reduce the risk of fraud and is a positive factor in assessing risks, but it is not an absolute deterrent, and by itself it does not prevent, or detect and correct, a material misstatement.

Q:: Name the four methods of recording and evaluating internal control.
A:: Narrative record, check list, internal control questionnaire and flow chart.

Q:: State the three disadvantages of a narrative record.
A:: It is difficult to comprehend the system in operation, to identify weaknesses or gaps in the system, and to incorporate changes arising from reshuffling of manpower.

Q:: Internal control questionnaire: what do "Yes" and "No" answers indicate?
A:: Questions are framed so that "Yes" denotes a satisfactory position and "No" suggests a weakness; explanations are given for "No" answers and "Not Applicable" for irrelevant questions.

Q:: Which method gives a bird's eye view of the system and is the most concise way of recording the review?
A:: The flow chart.
