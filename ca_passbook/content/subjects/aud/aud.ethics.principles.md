## In plain words

### The rule Bunty could not find

It is Bunty's first audit kick-off at Tapri Tea Co. Ltd. Kesar is launching "Tapri Gold", a card for unlimited free chai for life, and he hands one to every member of the Iyer & Associates team. That night Bunty searches the whole Code of Ethics for the word "chai".

> **Bunty:** Ma'am, I searched the entire Code. Nothing about chai cards. So it is allowed, no?
> **Lakshmi:** Did you also search for the word "judgment"?
> **Bunty:** *(scrolling)* There are hundreds of those.
> **Lakshmi:** No rule book can list every card a client will ever hand you, Bunty. That is why ours is built on principles.

Bunty has met the first idea of this lesson: **ethics** is not a list of banned objects but a **state of mind**, the habit of acting on moral principles even when nobody has written the rule down. (Whether he may keep that card is a question about threats to independence, answered in [[aud.ethics.independence|the next lesson]].)

Why does a Chartered Accountant need it more than most people? Because the whole profession sells **trust**. Society, governments, clients, tax authorities, employees, investors and lenders rely on what a CA signs. An audit report is worth something only if readers believe the person who signed it.

So ICAI has written a **Code of Ethics** for every CA, in practice or in service, and it rests on five **fundamental principles**: be honest (**integrity**), be unbiased (**objectivity**), know your job and do it carefully and on time (**professional competence and due care**), keep what you learn to yourself (**confidentiality**), and do nothing that breaks the law or embarrasses the profession (**professional behaviour**). Break them and ICAI's disciplinary machinery can fine, suspend or remove you.

::: analogy The umpire
A cricket umpire calls what he sees, not what the crowd wants (integrity). He does not umpire his brother's match (objectivity). He knows this season's rule changes and watches every ball (competence and due care). He does not tell a bookie what he overheard in the dressing room (confidentiality). And he does not dance with the fans after the match in a way that makes umpires look silly (professional behaviour). Nobody gives him a rule for every ball; he is trusted to apply the spirit of the game.
:::

::: key
A Chartered Accountant shall comply with **each** of the five fundamental principles: integrity, objectivity, professional competence and due care, confidentiality and professional behaviour. Where no specific rule fits, the principles still apply: ICAI expects the spirit of the Code to be followed, not just its letter.
:::

## How it works

### 1. Ethics: a state of mind

::: definition Ethics
The term "Ethics" means moral principles which govern a person's behaviour or his conducting of an activity. It is the branch of knowledge that deals with moral principles. Ethics comes from an individual intrinsically: it is a state of mind to act and perform in accordance with moral principles, and it must be strong enough to withstand any selfish motive or temptation (SM 11.4).
:::

### 2. Why the profession needs ethics

The Study Material gives these reasons (SM 11.4–11.5):

- **Trust is manifold.** Society in general, governments, clients, taxing authorities, employees, investors, and the business and financial community in particular have reposed tremendous trust in the services rendered by Chartered Accountants.
- **Assurance depends on it.** The purpose of assurance engagements is to enhance the confidence of the intended users, so users need to trust the person providing the service.
- **Public interest.** A distinguishing feature of the accountancy profession is its acceptance of the responsibility to act in the public interest. The respect a profession enjoys depends on the strictness and scrupulousness with which its ethics are adhered to by self-discipline; professional ethics protect the interests of the profession as a whole and act as a shield.
- **Everyone, everywhere.** A Chartered Accountant, in practice or in service, has to follow the fundamental principles; ICAI has codified them in a Code of Ethics.
- **Consequences.** Any deviation brings the disciplinary mechanism into action, which may result in fines, suspension of membership, removal from membership or other disciplinary actions.

### 3. Principles-based versus rules-based approach

Ethical guidance can be written in two ways (SM 11.5):

::: compare
| Point | Principles-based approach | Rules-based approach |
|---|---|---|
| Essence | Compliance with the **spirit** of ethics | Strict compliance with clearly established rules |
| How a case is decided | Professional judgment in every situation, using professional knowledge, skill and expertise | By looking up the rule that applies |
| Outlook | Every situation evaluated on its facts | May lead to a narrow outlook; the spirit may be overlooked |
| New situations | Can deal with them | Rigid: rules cannot cover every practical situation |
:::

The Study Material's conclusion: ==it is necessary that the spirit of the Code is followed== (SM 11.5). Bunty's search for "chai" was the rules-based approach; Lakshmi's "judgment" is the principles-based one.

### 4. The five fundamental principles

The fundamental principles establish the standard of behaviour expected of a professional accountant, and a professional accountant shall comply with each of them (SM 11.5–11.6). Learn the wording: ICAI's answers quote it.

#### (1) Integrity

::: definition Integrity
A professional accountant shall comply with the principle of integrity, which requires an accountant to be straightforward and honest in all professional and business relationships. Integrity implies fair dealing and truthfulness. A professional accountant shall not knowingly be associated with reports, returns, communications or other information where the accountant believes that the information (a) contains a materially false or misleading statement; (b) contains statements or information provided negligently; or (c) omits or obscures required information where such omission or obscurity would be misleading (SM 11.6).
:::

Notice limb (c): a report in which every number is true can still fail integrity, if it hides something the reader needed.

#### (2) Objectivity

::: definition Objectivity
The principle of objectivity requires an auditor not to compromise professional judgment because of bias, conflict of interest or undue influence of others. A professional accountant shall not undertake a professional activity if a circumstance or relationship unduly influences the accountant's professional judgment regarding that activity (SM 11.6–11.7).
:::

#### (3) Professional competence and due care

::: definition Professional competence and due care
This principle requires an accountant to (a) attain and maintain professional knowledge and skill at the level required to ensure that a client or employing organization receives competent professional service, based on current technical and professional standards and relevant legislation; and (b) act diligently and in accordance with applicable technical and professional standards. Diligence includes the responsibility to act carefully, thoroughly and on a timely basis in accordance with the requirements of an assignment (SM 11.7).
:::

Two halves, two kinds of failure: not *knowing* (an outdated rule applied) and not *doing* (careless, incomplete or late work).

#### (4) Confidentiality

::: definition Confidentiality
The confidentiality principle requires a professional accountant to respect the confidentiality of information acquired as a result of professional or business relationships. Confidentiality serves the public interest because it facilitates the free flow of information from the client or employing organization to the accountant, with the understanding that the information will not be disclosed to a third party (SM 11.7).
:::

It is not absolute. Confidential information **may be disclosed**, for example (SM 11.7):

#### (5) Professional behaviour

::: definition Professional behaviour
This principle requires an accountant to comply with relevant laws and regulations and avoid any conduct that the accountant knows or should know might discredit the profession. A professional accountant shall not knowingly engage in any employment, occupation or activity that impairs or might impair the integrity, objectivity or good reputation of the profession, and as a result would be incompatible with the fundamental principles (SM 11.7).
:::

## Worked examples

:::: example Four files on Bunty's desk
Identify and explain the fundamental principle of professional ethics involved in each of the following situations.

(a) CA Nirmala Rao, a Chartered Accountant in practice, accepted a client's application for a GST refund in January. Busy with audits, she left it untouched until the time limit for filing had expired, and the refund was lost.

(b) CA Harsh Vora, a Chartered Accountant in service as CFO of Ganga Cold Storage Ltd, prepared a loan proposal for a bank. The bank's format asks for all pending tax demands. Harsh listed two small demands and left out a demand of ₹14 crore, knowing that the bank would treat it as decisive.

(c) CA Faisal Khan, a Chartered Accountant in practice, posted in a public social-media group: "Any income-tax notice: we make it disappear. We know the right people."

(d) CA Mehul Shah has been offered the internal audit of Kulhad Crafts Ltd. Its managing director is Mehul's partner in a separate real-estate venture, and Mehul's share of that venture's profit depends on the managing director's goodwill.

::: answer Model answer
**(a) Professional competence and due care.** This principle requires an accountant to attain and maintain professional knowledge and skill and to act diligently and in accordance with applicable technical and professional standards. Diligence includes the responsibility to act carefully, thoroughly and on a timely basis in accordance with the requirements of an assignment. CA Nirmala did not act on a timely basis, and the client lost the refund: the principle of professional competence and due care is violated.

**(b) Integrity.** Integrity requires an accountant to be straightforward and honest in all professional and business relationships. A professional accountant shall not knowingly be associated with information that omits or obscures required information where such omission would be misleading. The bank's format requires all pending demands; Harsh knowingly omitted the ₹14 crore demand, which makes the proposal misleading. Being a Chartered Accountant in service does not exempt him: the principle of integrity is violated.

**(c) Professional behaviour.** This principle requires an accountant to comply with relevant laws and regulations and avoid any conduct that the accountant knows or should know might discredit the profession. A public claim to make tax notices "disappear" through contacts suggests improper influence over a tax authority and discredits the profession: the principle of professional behaviour is violated.

**(d) Objectivity.** Objectivity requires an accountant not to compromise professional judgment because of bias, conflict of interest or undue influence of others, and not to undertake a professional activity if a circumstance or relationship unduly influences his professional judgment regarding that activity. Mehul's income from the venture depends on the managing director whose company he would audit internally: a conflict of interest that would unduly influence his judgment. He should not undertake the assignment; accepting it would violate the principle of objectivity.
:::
::::

:::: example Three requests for Tapri's secrets
In one week Iyer & Associates, statutory auditors of Tapri Tea Co. Ltd, receive three requests for information about Tapri that they hold from the audit:

(i) A summons issued by the GST department under the GST law, requiring the firm to produce copies of Tapri's records relating to input tax credit.

(ii) BrewBucks, which is negotiating to buy Tapri's samosa factory, asks for the firm's working papers on the factory. Tapri's board has given the firm written authority to share them.

(iii) A loan officer of Tapri's bank phones Bunty and asks him to email Tapri's ageing of trade receivables "just to save time"; Tapri has not been asked and has authorised nothing.

Comment whether Iyer & Associates may disclose the information in each case, with reference to the fundamental principles of professional ethics.

::: answer Model answer
**Relevant principle.** The principle of confidentiality requires a professional accountant to respect the confidentiality of information acquired as a result of professional or business relationships. Confidentiality serves the public interest because it facilitates the free flow of information from the client to the accountant, with the understanding that the information will not be disclosed to a third party. However, confidential information may be disclosed, for example, when it is required by law, when it is permitted by law and is authorised by the client or employer, or when there is a professional duty or right to disclose that is not prohibited by law.

**(i) GST summons.** The disclosure is **required by law**. The firm may produce the records the summons requires, limited to what it requires; doing so does not violate confidentiality.

**(ii) BrewBucks.** Sharing information with a prospective buyer is permitted by law, and the client has **authorised** it in writing. Disclosure is therefore permitted, within the scope of the authority given. The authority permits the firm to share; it does not take the file out of the firm's hands: unless otherwise specified by law or regulation, engagement documentation is the property of the firm ([[aud.ethics.qc|SQC 1]], SM 11.30), so the firm itself decides which portions or extracts to share.

**(iii) The bank's phone call.** No law requires the disclosure and Tapri has not authorised it. Bunty must not send the ageing; the bank should obtain it from Tapri, or Tapri may authorise the firm. Sending it would violate the principle of confidentiality.

**Conclusion.** Iyer & Associates may disclose in cases (i) and (ii), within the limits stated, but not in case (iii).
:::

## Under the hood

### Where the five principles turn up again

- **SQC 1, ethical requirements.** A firm's system of quality control must give it reasonable assurance that the firm and its personnel comply with the Code of Ethics issued by ICAI, which establishes these five principles (SM 11.26–11.27; [[aud.ethics.qc|SQC 1 and SA 220]]).
- **Independence is not a sixth principle.** The Study Material treats independence, its threats and safeguards separately, and defines independence of mind as the state of mind that allows an individual to act with integrity and exercise objectivity and professional skepticism (SM 11.8; [[aud.ethics.independence|independence, threats and safeguards]]). The chapter's own MCQ lists "safeguards to independence" as the option that is *not* a fundamental principle (SM 11.40).
- **Objectivity and independence overlap.** A relationship that "unduly influences" judgment fails objectivity; the same relationship, seen from the audit side, is a threat to independence. A question asking for the *principle* wants objectivity; one asking for the *threat* wants the threat's name.

### Patterns ICAI's own answers use

- An auditor who does not reply to a letter from ICAI, despite reminders: **professional behaviour** (SM 11.42, Test Your Understanding 1).
- An outgoing auditor who does not reply to the incoming auditor's communication: **professional behaviour** (SM 11.36–11.37, case study; also January 2026 MCQ 1).
- A certificate knowingly showing a false figure: **integrity** (SM 11.42).
- An auditor who never sent an engagement letter, so the client misunderstood the audit's scope: **professional competence and due care**, in ICAI's suggested answer (MTP September 2024 Series I Q4(b); the letter itself is [[aud.ethics.sa210|SA 210]]).
- An auditor who, to keep a long-standing client and its future work, deliberately leaves identified non-compliances undocumented and uncommunicated: **professional behaviour** in ICAI's key (RTP September 2026, case MCQ 1).
- A professional accountant in service who discloses documents because the law requires it in legal proceedings: **no violation** of confidentiality (September 2024 Q6(b)).

## Answer frame

### "Identify and explain the principle" (1 to 2 marks per situation)

1. **Name the principle** as a heading: "(a) Integrity".
2. **Quote the definition**: only the limb that fits ("shall not knowingly be associated with information that omits required information where such omission would be misleading").
3. **Apply it to the facts**: "In the given case, Harsh knowingly omitted …".
4. **Conclude**: "Hence, the principle of integrity is violated." (Or "not violated", as in a disclosure required by law.)

### "Distinguish between the principles-based and the rules-based approach" (2 marks)

Write the comparison table of §3 as points: essence (spirit versus established rules); professional judgment in every situation versus following rules; narrow outlook and rigidity of rules; conclusion that the spirit of the Code must be followed.

### Three answers to file (b)

**Loses marks**

"Harsh is dishonest."

A judgment, not an answer: no principle named, no definition.
---
**Passes**

Right principle, but no definition and no link to "misleading".

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: independence as the sixth principle
Bunty lists six fundamental principles, the sixth being "independence". **Fix:** there are five: integrity, objectivity, professional competence and due care, confidentiality, professional behaviour. Independence, its threats and safeguards are a separate topic.
:::

::: mistake Integrity and objectivity swapped
A knowingly false certificate written up as "objectivity", or a relative's audit written up as "integrity". **Fix:** false or misleading information is integrity; bias, conflict of interest or undue influence is objectivity. When both are present, name both.
:::

::: mistake Confidentiality as a vow of silence
"The auditor can never disclose client information", even to a court. **Fix:** disclosure is allowed when required by law, when permitted by law and authorised by the client or employer, or under a professional duty or right not prohibited by law.
:::

::: mistake The client's say-so is not always enough
"The client authorised it, so it is fine." **Fix:** the second exception needs *both* permission of law and the client's authority; and the auditor's working papers remain the firm's, shared at its discretion.
:::

## Practice

::: quiz Check yourself
Q: Case scenario. CA Tanvi Bose is the finance controller, a Chartered Accountant in service, of Sevadham Hospital Trust. On 4 April a trustee asks her to issue a donation receipt dated 30 March for a donation received that day, so that the donor can claim it in the earlier year. Which fundamental principle would Tanvi violate if she agrees?
- [ ] Professional competence and due care
- [ ] Confidentiality
- [x] Integrity
- [ ] None, since she is not in practice
> A receipt carrying a false date is information Tanvi knows to be materially false: integrity. It is not a lack of skill or care, nothing confidential is disclosed, and the principles apply to Chartered Accountants in service as much as in practice.

Q: Which of the following are fundamental principles of professional ethics in the Study Material? Select all that apply.
- [x] Objectivity
- [ ] Professional skepticism
- [x] Professional behaviour
- [ ] Independence in appearance
- [x] Confidentiality
> The five are integrity, objectivity, professional competence and due care, confidentiality and professional behaviour. Professional skepticism and independence (of mind and in appearance) are covered elsewhere in the chapter.

Q: CA Vikram signs a stock audit report for a bank showing ₹40 lakh of goods that he knows the borrower has already sold, because the borrower is his school friend. Which principles are violated? Select all that apply.
- [x] Integrity
- [x] Objectivity
- [ ] Confidentiality
- [ ] None: the bank should have verified the stock itself
> He is knowingly associated with a materially false report (integrity), and he let a relationship compromise his judgment (objectivity). Naming only one of them is the commonest slip.

Q: Which statement about the principles-based approach to ethics is correct?
- [ ] An accountant who complies with every written rule has necessarily acted ethically
- [x] It requires professional judgment in every situation, using professional knowledge, skill and expertise
- [ ] It applies only where the law prescribes no penalty
- [ ] It is more rigid than the rules-based approach
> The principles-based approach looks to the spirit of ethics and needs judgment in every situation; the rules-based approach can be narrow and rigid, and complying with its letter does not guarantee ethical conduct.

Q: Under the confidentiality principle, in which of these cases may a professional accountant disclose a client's information? Select all that apply.
- [x] A court order requires its production
- [ ] A friend asks informally and promises to keep it secret
- [x] The law permits it and the client has authorised it
- [ ] The information is more than three years old
> Disclosure is allowed when required by law, when permitted by law and authorised by the client or employer, or under a professional duty or right to disclose not prohibited by law. Age of the information and a friend's promise change nothing.
:::

## Recap

- Ethics: moral principles governing a person's behaviour; a state of mind strong enough to resist selfish motives.
- Need: society, governments, clients, taxing authorities, employees, investors and the business and financial community trust Chartered Accountants; assurance exists to enhance users' confidence; the profession acts in the public interest.
- Deviation brings the disciplinary mechanism: fines, suspension or removal from membership, or other action.
- Principles-based approach: the spirit of ethics and professional judgment in every situation; rules-based: rigid, may be narrow. Follow the spirit of the Code.
- Five principles: integrity, objectivity, professional competence and due care, confidentiality, professional behaviour. Comply with each.
- Integrity covers false, negligent **and** misleadingly incomplete information; diligence means carefully, thoroughly and on a timely basis.
- Confidential information may be disclosed when required by law, permitted by law and authorised by the client or employer, or under a professional duty or right not prohibited by law. (The umpire may tell the court; he may not tell the bookie.)

## Flashcards

Q:: What does "ethics" mean, according to the Study Material?
A:: Moral principles which govern a person's behaviour or his conducting of an activity; a state of mind to act in accordance with moral principles.

Q:: Name the five fundamental principles of professional ethics, in order.
A:: Integrity; objectivity; professional competence and due care; confidentiality; professional behaviour.

Q:: Integrity: with what kind of information shall a professional accountant not knowingly be associated?
A:: Information that contains a materially false or misleading statement, contains statements or information provided negligently, or omits or obscures required information where the omission would be misleading.

Q:: What does the principle of objectivity require?
A:: Not to compromise professional judgment because of bias, conflict of interest or undue influence of others; not to undertake an activity if a circumstance or relationship unduly influences that judgment.

Q:: Professional competence and due care: what does "diligence" include?
A:: The responsibility to act carefully, thoroughly and on a timely basis in accordance with the requirements of an assignment.

Q:: Confidentiality: when may confidential information be disclosed (Study Material examples)?
A:: When required by law; when permitted by law and authorised by the client or employer; or when there is a professional duty or right to disclose, not prohibited by law.

Q:: What does the principle of professional behaviour require?
A:: Compliance with relevant laws and regulations and avoiding any conduct that the accountant knows or should know might discredit the profession.

Q:: Principles-based versus rules-based approach: the essence of each?
A:: Principles-based: compliance with the spirit of ethics through professional judgment in every situation. Rules-based: strict adherence to established rules; can be narrow and rigid.

Q:: What may follow a deviation from the ethical responsibilities?
A:: The disciplinary mechanism: fines, suspension of membership, removal from membership or other disciplinary actions.

Q:: Which principle do ICAI's answers apply to an outgoing auditor who ignores the incoming auditor's letter?
A:: Professional behaviour.
