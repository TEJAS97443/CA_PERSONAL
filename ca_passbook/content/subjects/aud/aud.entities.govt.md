## In plain words

### The museum with the locked door

Kesar is reading the newspaper aloud in Lakshmi's office, far too happily.

> **Kesar:** "C&AG report: tea heritage museum built for ₹14 crore, still locked after three years." Fourteen crore! And you question my ₹4,000 of miscellaneous expenses, Lakshmi-ji.
> **Lakshmi:** The C&AG asked the question I ask you, Kesar-ji. Only the shareholders are everyone who pays tax.
> **Bunty:** But Ma'am, if the department followed every rule, what is the objection?
> **Lakshmi:** Following the rules is only the first question, Bunty. There are five.

Parliament (or, for a State, the State Legislature) decides how much the government may spend and on what, by voting on the **demands for grants**; the money comes out of the **Consolidated Fund**. Someone must check afterwards that it was spent as Parliament allowed: the **Comptroller and Auditor General of India (C&AG)**, an independent authority whose safeguards are written into the Constitution, working through the Indian Audit and Accounts Department.

Five questions are asked of spending, in this order. Was it allowed by the law and the financial rules? Did someone with the power to approve it approve it? Was money provided for that purpose, and did spending stay within it? Was it sensible, or wasteful even though every rule was followed? And did the big programmes deliver? A locked museum can pass the first three and fail the fourth.

The C&AG reports to the President (Union) or the Governor (State), who has the report laid before the legislature; the report states facts, and the **Public Accounts Committee** takes it from there. For **government companies** the C&AG appoints the statutory auditors, tells them how to audit, and may audit after them.

::: analogy The family wedding budget
In a joint family the elders' council approves the wedding budget head by head. Chachu spends; afterwards Nani checks every bill. Allowed by family rules? Approved by an elder? Was there money in that head, or did the band's fee come out of the food budget? Sensible? (A gold-plated invitation card can be allowed, approved and within budget, and still be silly.) And did the wedding go well? Then Nani reads her findings to the council and lets them decide. She is the C&AG; the council is Parliament.
:::

::: key
The C&AG audits public money on behalf of Parliament and the State Legislatures. Expenditure audit applies five standards: rules and orders, sanctions, provision of funds, propriety and performance. The first three check that spending was allowed, approved and provided for; propriety and performance catch spending that is perfectly regular and still wasteful or useless.
:::

## How it works

### 1. What government audit is, and why it exists

Government audit aims to ensure the accountability of the executive in respect of public revenue and expenditure. Parliament, and in the States the State Legislatures, control all government expenditure by insisting on demands for grants: no expenditure can be incurred unless it has been voted on, and the funds for it must come out of the Consolidated Fund of India or of the State (SM 9.5).

::: definition Consolidated Fund of India
As per Article 266, the Consolidated Fund of India consists of all the revenue received from direct and indirect taxes, all the loans taken by the Government of India and all the amount of repayment of loans received by the Government of India (SM 9.5). All government expenditure is incurred from this fund, and no money can be appropriated out of it except in accordance with law and for the purposes and in the manner provided in the Constitution (SM 9.92).
:::

After the expenditure is incurred and the accounts are closed, the **Appropriation Accounts** are prepared and scrutinised by the **Public Accounts Committee (PAC)**, a committee of selected members of Parliament. So legislative control is exercised both before and after spending (SM 9.5).

::: definition Government auditing (U.N. Handbook on Government Auditing and Developing Countries)
Government auditing is the objective, systematic, professional and independent examination of financial, administrative and other operations of a public entity, made subsequently to their execution, for the purpose of evaluating and verifying them, presenting a report containing explanatory comments on audit findings together with conclusions and recommendations for future actions by the responsible officials and, in the case of examination of financial statements, expressing the appropriate professional opinion regarding the fairness of the presentation (SM 9.6).
:::

**Objectives of government audit** (SM 9.6–9.7):

- (a) **Accounting for public funds**: a mechanism for public accounting of government funds.
- (b) **Appraisal of government policies**: public accounting of the operational, management, programme and policy aspects of public administration, and accountability of the officials administering them.
- (c) **Base for corrective actions**: observations based on factual data highlight the lapses of the lower hierarchy, helping supervisory officers take corrective measures.

**Administrative accountability.** Government audit is neither equipped nor intended to function as an investigating agency pursuing every irregularity to its logical end. ==Its main objective is a combination of ensuring accountability of administration to legislature and functioning as an aid to administration== (SM 9.7).

### 2. Why the C&AG is independent: the legal framework

The Constitution contains specific provisions on the appointment, salary, duties and powers of the C&AG (SM 9.7–9.8):

1. **Appointment and removal.** Appointed by the President of India; not removed from office except on the ground of proven misbehaviour or incapacity. As for a Judge of the Supreme Court, removal needs each House of Parliament to decide so by a majority of not less than two-thirds of the members of the House present and voting.
2. **Salary and conditions.** Determined by laws of Parliament, and not variable to the C&AG's disadvantage after appointment. The service conditions of the Indian Audit and Accounts Department and the C&AG's administrative powers are determined by the President after consultation with the C&AG.
3. **Tenure.** The Comptroller and Auditor General's (Duties, Powers and Conditions of Service) Act, 1971 fixes the tenure and a salary equal to a Supreme Court Judge's.
4. **Article 149**: the C&AG performs such duties and exercises such powers in relation to the accounts of the Union, the States and any other authority or body as are prescribed by or under any law made by Parliament (the 1971 Act).
5. **Article 150**: the accounts of the Union and of the States shall be kept in such form as **the President** may, on the advice of the C&AG, prescribe.
6. **Article 151**: the C&AG's reports on the accounts of the Union or a State are submitted to **the President or the Governor**, who causes them to be laid before Parliament or the State Legislature.

### 3. Duties and powers of the C&AG under the 1971 Act

| Duty | What it covers (SM 9.8–9.11) |
|---|---|
| (i) Compile and submit accounts | Compile the accounts of the Union and each State from the accounts rendered by treasuries, offices and departments; prepare the annual accounts (including appropriation accounts, for accounts he compiles) and submit them to the President, Governor or Administrator |
| (ii) General audit | Audit and report on (a) all expenditure from the Consolidated Funds, ascertaining whether the moneys disbursed ==were legally available for and applicable to the service or purpose== to which they were applied, and whether the expenditure conforms to the authority which governs it; (b) all transactions of Contingency Funds and Public Accounts; (c) all trading, manufacturing and profit and loss accounts, balance sheets and subsidiary accounts kept in any department |
| (iii) Bodies substantially financed | Audit all receipts and expenditure of a body or authority substantially financed by grants or loans from a Consolidated Fund (subject to any law applicable to it), and report on them; the test is under Formats and formulas |
| (iv) Grants or loans for a specific purpose | Scrutinise the procedures by which the sanctioning authority satisfies itself that the conditions are fulfilled, with a right of access to the body's books after reasonable previous notice (not for a foreign State or international organisation) |
| (v) Receipts of the Union or States | Audit all receipts payable into the Consolidated Funds; see that the rules secure an effective check on assessment, collection and proper allocation of revenue and are duly observed |
| (vi) Stores and inventory | Audit and report on the accounts of stores and inventory kept in any office or department |
| (vii) Government companies | Perform these duties under the Companies Act, 2013: appoint the auditor under section 139(5) or (7) and direct the manner of audit (section 7 below) |

**Powers** (SM 9.11): (a) to inspect any office of accounts under the control of the Union or a State, including offices creating the initial or subsidiary accounts; (b) to require that accounts, books, papers and other documents relevant to the transactions under audit be sent to specified places; (c) to put questions or make observations to the person in charge of the office, and call for information needed for any account or report he must prepare; (d) to dispense with any part of the detailed audit of any accounts or class of transactions and apply such limited checks as he determines.

### 4. Expenditure audit: the five standards

The audit of government expenditure is one of the major components of government audit. Its basic standards ensure that there is provision of funds authorised by competent authority fixing the limits within which expenditure can be incurred (SM 9.11–9.12); their exact wording is in the table under Formats and formulas.

::: diagram Expenditure audit: five standards and the question each asks

**(1) Audit against rules and orders** ensures that the expenditure conforms to the Constitution, the laws and rules made under it, and the financial rules, regulations and orders of a competent authority. It is quasi-judicial work, interpreting the Constitution, statutes and rules, though the final power of interpretation does not vest with the C&AG (SM 9.12). The executive frames the rules and audit sees that subordinate authorities apply them properly; it is not the function of audit to prescribe them. But audit examines the executive's rules, regulations and orders to see that (SM 9.13):

- (a) they are not inconsistent with any provisions of the Constitution or any laws made thereunder;
- (b) they are consistent with the essential requirements of audit and accounts as determined by the C&AG;
- (c) they do not come in conflict with the orders of, or rules made by, any higher authority; and
- (d) in case they have not been separately approved by competent authority, the issuing authority possesses the necessary rule-making power.

**(2) Audit of sanctions** ensures that each item of expenditure is covered by a sanction, general or special, of the competent authority, and that the sanctioning authority is competent under the Constitution, the law, rules or orders, or the rules of delegation of financial powers (SM 9.13).

**(3) Audit against provision of funds** ascertains that the expenditure has been on the purpose for which the grant and appropriation had been provided, and that it does not exceed the appropriation made (SM 9.13).

**(4) Propriety audit** brings out cases of ==improper, avoidable, or ineffective expenditure even though the expenditure has been incurred in conformity with the existing rules and regulations==: a building constructed for a telephone exchange but not used for it (unproductive), or a school building used only five years after completion (avoidable). It looks into the wisdom, faithfulness and economy of transactions. The Audit Code's standards of financial propriety (SM 9.14–9.15):

- (a) The expenditure should not be prima facie more than the occasion demands. Every public officer is expected to exercise the same vigilance over public money as a person of ordinary prudence would over his own.
- (b) No authority should exercise its powers of sanctioning expenditure to pass an order which will be directly or indirectly to its own advantage.
- (c) Public moneys should not be utilised for the benefit of a particular person or section of the community unless:
  - (i) the amount of expenditure involved is insignificant; or
  - (ii) a claim for the amount could be enforced in a Court of law; or
  - (iii) the expenditure is in pursuance of a recognised policy or custom; and
  - (iv) the amount of allowances, such as travelling allowances, granted to meet expenditure of a particular type should be so regulated that the allowances are not, on the whole, sources of profit to the recipients.

Enforcing economy is the executive's responsibility; audit brings wasteful, improper, avoidable and unproductive expenditure to the notice of the proper authorities (SM 9.15).

**(5) Performance audit** checks that programmes, schemes and projects with large financial expenditure are run economically and yield the results expected. It covers efficiency, economy and effectiveness audit, also called full scope audit (SM 9.15–9.16):

- **Efficiency audit**: whether schemes are executed and operated economically and yield the expected results: the relationship between goods and services produced and the resources used.
- **Economy audit**: whether government acquired its financial, human and physical resources economically, and the sanctioning and spending authorities observed economy.
- **Effectiveness audit**: an appraisal of programmes, schemes and projects against the overall targeted objectives, and of the efficiency of the means adopted.

Its procedure covers **identification of topic, preliminary study, planning, execution of audit, and reporting** (SM 9.16).

### 5. Audit of receipts

Neither all pervasive nor as old as expenditure audit, it has come to stay in some countries. It checks (SM 9.16–9.17):

- (i) whether all revenues or other debts due to government have been correctly assessed, realised and credited to government account by the designated authorities;
- (ii) whether adequate regulations and procedures have been framed by the department or agency to secure an effective check on assessment, collection and proper allocation of cases;
- (iii) whether such regulations and procedures are actually being carried out;
- (iv) whether adequate checks ensure the prompt detection and investigation of irregularities, double refunds, fraudulent or forged refund vouchers or other loss of revenue through fraud, wilful omission or negligence to levy or collect taxes or to issue refunds;
- (v) a review of systems and procedures to see that they secure correct and regular accounting of demands, collections and refunds and pursuit of dues up to final settlement, and to suggest improvement. **It is more important to look at the general than at the particular**, though individual cases matter within the test check; judicial decisions of tax authorities are reviewed to judge the assessment procedure;
- (vi) the extent and quantum of audit are determined by the C&AG and are neither negotiable nor questioned; they follow test check, random sampling, general review and in-depth study of specified areas. Primary check is by the auditor, test check by the supervisor, control and direction by the group leader.

### 6. Audit of stores and inventories

Developed as a part of expenditure audit, it is conducted (SM 9.17–9.18):

- to ascertain whether the regulations governing purchase, receipt and issue, custody, sale and inventory taking of stores are well devised and properly carried out;
- to bring to the notice of the government any deficiencies in quantities of stores held or any defects in the system of control;
- to verify that the purchases are properly sanctioned, made economically and in accordance with the rules for purchase laid down by the competent authority;
- to ensure that the prices paid are reasonable and agree with the supply contract, and that certificates of quality and quantity are furnished by the inspecting and receiving units; uneconomical purchases and losses from defective or inferior stores are specifically brought out;
- to check the accounts of receipts, issues and balances for accuracy, correctness and reasonableness, against the specified norms for consumption and inventory holding. Excess or idle inventory is specifically reported, periodical verification ensures existence, and valuation is seen so that value accounts tally with physical accounts.

### 7. Commercial accounts and government companies

Public enterprises maintain commercial accounts and fall into three categories (SM 9.18–9.19). (For municipalities, see [[aud.entities.local|audit of local bodies]].)

| Category | How it is audited |
|---|---|
| (a) Departmental enterprises in commercial and trading operations | Like any department where commercial accounts are kept |
| (b) Statutory bodies and corporations created by specific statutes, mostly government-financed | Depends on the statute; financial audit by the C&AG (where the C&AG compiles the accounts, professional auditors' norms apply mutatis mutandis) |
| (c) Government companies under the Companies Act, 2013 | By their own auditors appointed by the C&AG, plus the C&AG's supplementary test audit, periodical financial audit and appraisal of performance |

For performance reviews the C&AG uses an **Audit Board** of audit representatives and government nominees, including functional specialists (SM 9.19). For a government company, or any company owned or controlled, directly or indirectly, by the Central Government, any State Government or Governments, or partly by both, section 143(5), (6) and (7) of the Companies Act, 2013 give the C&AG three roles (SM 9.19–9.20):

1. **Appoint and direct.** The C&AG appoints the auditor under section 139(5) or (7) and directs the manner of audit; the directions broadly cover the system of book-keeping and accounts, internal control and so on. The auditor submits a copy of the audit report to the C&AG, including the directions, the action taken on them and their impact on the accounts and financial statements.
2. **Supplementary audit and comments.** Within **60 days from the date of receipt of the audit report**, the C&AG has a right to (a) conduct a supplementary audit by persons he authorises, who may require information or additional information; and (b) comment upon or supplement the audit report. The company sends the comments to every person entitled to copies of the audited financial statements under section 136 (every member, every trustee for debenture-holders, and every other person so entitled), and **places them before the AGM at the same time and in the same manner as the audit report**.
3. **Test audit.** For any company covered by section 139(5) or (7), the C&AG may, if he considers it necessary, by an order cause a test audit; section 19A of the 1971 Act applies to its report.

**Does the board have to answer the C&AG?** Section 134(3) requires the board to explain or comment on every reservation, adverse remark or disclaimer in the **auditor's report** and the secretarial audit report. There is no similar provision for the C&AG's comments, so the board is not bound to give information or explanation on them (SM 9.21).

### 8. Reporting procedures

Reports laid before the legislature under **Article 151** should also be publicised, to build public opinion for remedial action. **In India the reporting is factual and the conclusions are left to the reader**, presumably to ensure total objectivity; nothing debars the C&AG from recommending, but traditionally that has been left to the Public Accounts Committee (SM 9.21–9.22).

## Formats and formulas

### When is a body "substantially financed"?

::: formula Substantially financed (C&AG's duty (iii))
A body or authority is deemed substantially financed by grants or loans from the Consolidated Fund of India, of a State or of a Union Territory having a Legislative Assembly when, **in a financial year**, both hold (SM 9.10):

- the grant or loan is **not less than ₹25 lakh**; and
- the grant or loan is **not less than 75% of the total expenditure** of that body or authority.
:::

Both tests must be met; "not less than" includes exactly ₹25 lakh or 75%.

## Worked examples

:::: example The Tourism Department's very busy year
The C&AG's audit party is examining the State Department of Tourism for 2025-26 and notes:

(i) A Deputy Director approved a ₹18 lakh contract for event lighting; under the State's delegation of financial powers, his limit is ₹10 lakh.

(ii) ₹42 lakh from the grant for "Development of Rural Homestays" was spent on new cars for the Secretariat.

(iii) A ₹6 crore tourist reception centre, built in full compliance with the rules and completed in 2022, was first used only in 2025, when the access road it needed was finally built.

(iv) A ₹60 crore scheme to train 20,000 youth as tourist guides has run for four years; nobody has examined how many trainees ever found work as guides.

(v) The department's own circular lets officers draw a daily allowance of ₹6,000 while the State's Financial Rules allow ₹3,500; the circular was never approved by the Finance Department, which alone has the rule-making power.

(vi) Under the State's own rules, field officers get a flat conveyance allowance of ₹9,000 a month; their actual conveyance costs average about ₹3,000.

Identify, with reasons, the standard of expenditure audit (and, where relevant, the principle of financial propriety) under which each observation falls.

::: answer Model answer
**Relevant provision.** The basic standards of government expenditure audit are audit against rules and orders, audit of sanctions, audit against provision of funds, propriety audit and performance audit (SM 9.12).

**(i) Audit of sanctions.** Each item of expenditure must be covered by a sanction of the competent authority, competent under the rules of delegation of financial powers. With a limit of ₹10 lakh, the Deputy Director is not competent to sanction ₹18 lakh.

**(ii) Audit against provision of funds.** Expenditure must be on the purpose for which the grant and appropriation were provided. Cars for the Secretariat are not the purpose of the rural homestays grant.

**(iii) Propriety audit.** It brings out improper, avoidable or ineffective expenditure even though incurred in conformity with the existing rules and regulations. A building used only years after completion is avoidable expenditure, like the Study Material's school building used five years after it was completed.

**(iv) Performance audit.** A scheme with large financial expenditure must be run economically and yield the results expected of it; effectiveness is judged against its targeted objective, employment as guides.

**(v) Audit against rules and orders.** The expenditure must conform to the financial rules. Examining the circular, audit sees that it does not conflict with rules of a higher authority and that, if not separately approved, its issuer has the rule-making power. Neither holds.

**(vi) Propriety audit, principle (c)(iv).** The payment is regular, but the amount of allowances, such as travelling allowances, should be so regulated that the allowances are not, on the whole, sources of profit to the recipients; ₹9,000 against costs of about ₹3,000 is a source of profit.

**Conclusion.** Each observation is reported under its standard; (iii) and (vi) are reported even though the payments were regular.
:::

:::: example Who gets the C&AG's full audit?
In 2025-26, three bodies received money from the Consolidated Fund of Madhya Pradesh:

| Body | Grant received (₹ lakh) | Total expenditure (₹ lakh) | Terms of the grant |
|---|---|---|---|
| Malwa Tea Research Institute | 162 | 210 | General grant |
| Indore Lok Kala Mandal | 21 | 24 | General grant |
| Kulhad Shilpi Co-operative | 45 | 90 | For building two kilns |

Required: State, with reasons, what the C&AG's duty is in respect of each body under the C&AG's (Duties, Powers and Conditions of Service) Act, 1971.

::: answer Show the working
**Provision.** A body is substantially financed when the grant or loan in a financial year is not less than ₹25 lakh and not less than 75% of its total expenditure; the C&AG then audits all its receipts and expenditure. For a specific-purpose grant, the C&AG scrutinises the sanctioning authority's procedures for checking the conditions (SM 9.10).

| Body | Grant ≥ ₹25 lakh? | Grant ÷ total expenditure | ≥ 75%? | Substantially financed? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Malwa Tea Research Institute | Yes (162) | 162 ÷ 210 = 77.14% | Yes | **Yes** |
| Indore Lok Kala Mandal | No (21) | 21 ÷ 24 = 87.50% | Yes | **No** |
| Kulhad Shilpi Co-operative | Yes (45) | 45 ÷ 90 = 50.00% | No | **No** |

**Malwa Tea Research Institute**: both tests are met, so the C&AG shall audit **all its receipts and expenditure** and report on them.

**Indore Lok Kala Mandal**: the grant is 87.5% of expenditure, but ₹21 lakh is below ₹25 lakh, so it is **not** substantially financed: one test is not enough. Its grant is general, not for a specific purpose, so neither duty arises.

**Kulhad Shilpi Co-operative**: only 50%, so not substantially financed. But the grant is for a specific purpose (kilns), so the C&AG shall **scrutinise the procedures by which the sanctioning authority satisfies itself** that its conditions are fulfilled, with access to the co-operative's books after reasonable previous notice.

::::: example Narmada Dairy's annual report
Narmada Dairy Vikas Nigam Ltd is wholly owned by the Government of Madhya Pradesh; it sells milk to Tapri Tea Co. Ltd. The C&AG appointed Iyer & Associates as its statutory auditors for 2025-26 and directed them to report, among other things, on the system of reconciling milk-procurement subsidies receivable from the State. Their report, which contains a qualification on the subsidy receivable, reached the C&AG on 12 July 2026. On 28 August 2026, after a supplementary audit, the C&AG sent comments supplementing the report on the same receivable.

> **Bunty:** Ma'am, the MD says the C&AG's letter is "just government paperwork".
> **Lakshmi:** Paperwork with section numbers in it, Bunty. Read them before you agree with him.

The board proposes (a) to send members the auditor's report but not the C&AG's comments, because "the C&AG is not our auditor", and (b) to give no explanation of the auditor's qualification in the Board's report, because "the C&AG's comments already deal with the same receivable". Advise the board, explaining the C&AG's role in the audit of a government company.

:::: answer Model answer
**Relevant provision.** Under section 143(5) of the Companies Act, 2013, the C&AG appoints the auditor of a government company and directs the manner of audit; the auditor submits a copy of the audit report to the C&AG, including the directions, the action taken on them and their impact on the accounts. Under section 143(6), within 60 days from the date of receipt of the audit report, the C&AG has a right to conduct a supplementary audit and to comment upon or supplement the audit report; such comments shall be sent by the company to every person entitled to copies of the audited financial statements under section 136 and placed before the annual general meeting at the same time and in the same manner as the audit report. Section 134(3) requires the board to explain every reservation, adverse remark or disclaimer in the auditor's report; there is no similar provision for the C&AG's comments (SM 9.19–9.21).

**Facts of the case.** The report reached the C&AG on 12 July 2026 and the comments came on 28 August 2026, 47 days later: within the 60-day window. Iyer & Associates' copy to the C&AG must cover the directions on subsidy reconciliation, the action taken and the impact on the financial statements.

**Analysis and conclusion.**

(a) **Not permissible.** The C&AG's comments must go to every member, every trustee for debenture-holders and every other person so entitled, and before the AGM with the audit report.

(b) **Not permissible.** Section 134(3) requires the board to explain or comment on every reservation in the auditor's report; the C&AG's comments do not replace that explanation. The board is, however, not bound to explain the C&AG's own comments.

## Under the hood

### Regularity audit's rulebook

Three main categories (SM 9.12–9.13): rules and orders regulating (i) the powers to incur and sanction expenditure from the Consolidated Fund (and Contingency Fund) of India or a State; (ii) presenting claims against government and withdrawing money from the Consolidated Fund, Contingency Fund and Public Accounts, with the detailed financial rules of procedure; (iii) conditions of service, pay and allowances, and pensions of government servants.

### The Study Material's case study, decoded

ICAI's chapter case study (SM 9.80–9.82) tests recognition: a metro station sited where ridership is low, and a government power company's low generation because of high variable cost, are both **performance audit**; a report tabled in Parliament on direct-tax administration is a C&AG report under **Article 151 to the President**; the **C&AG** conducts a government company's supplementary audit; and a statutory auditor's comments on reconciling inter-company balances respond to the **C&AG's directions**.

## Answer frame

### "Identify the type of audit" case

1. **Relevant provision**: the basic standards of expenditure audit, in one sentence.
2. For each observation: **name the audit**, quote its "that ..." clause, apply the fact ("the Deputy Director's limit is ₹10 lakh").
3. **Conclusion**: one line per observation.

### "Explain the duties (or powers) of the C&AG"

Start with Article 149 and the 1971 Act; then duties (i) to (vii) as headings with one sentence each (the ₹25 lakh and 75% test under (iii)); then, if asked, powers (a) to (d).

### "Distinguish propriety audit and performance audit"

| Point | Propriety audit | Performance audit |
|---|---|---|
| Question asked | Was the expenditure wise, faithful and economical? | Is the programme run economically and yielding the results expected? |
| Typical subject | A transaction or item of expenditure | Programmes, schemes, projects with large financial expenditure |
| What it brings out | Improper, avoidable or ineffective expenditure, even when regular | Efficiency, economy and effectiveness |
| Study Material example | Building for a telephone exchange not used for it | Metro station sited where ridership is low (case study) |

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: the report that went to the Prime Minister
Bunty writes that C&AG reports go to the Prime Minister and that the Finance Minister prescribes the form of accounts. **Fix:** Article 151: reports go to the **President** (Union) or **Governor** (State), who has them laid before the legislature. Article 150: the **President** prescribes the form, on the C&AG's advice.
:::

::: mistake Sanction or funds? Mixing up the two
"Paid without approval" and "paid without a budget" are different audits. **Fix:** no sanction by a competent authority, or a sanction beyond the officer's delegated powers: **audit of sanctions**. Spent on a purpose other than the grant's, or beyond the appropriation: **audit against provision of funds**.
:::

::: mistake Calling every waste "performance audit"
**Fix:** an avoidable or unproductive item of expenditure, regular but unwise (the locked museum, the unused building), is **propriety audit**. Whether a programme, scheme or project with large expenditure is economical and achieving its expected results is **performance audit**.
:::

::: mistake "The C&AG audits government companies himself, and the board must answer him"
**Fix:** the auditor the C&AG appoints and directs does the statutory audit; the C&AG may conduct a supplementary audit (within 60 days of receiving the report) or a test audit, and comment. The board explains the **auditor's** reservations under section 134(3), not the C&AG's comments.
:::

## Recap

- The C&AG, appointed by the President and removable only like a Supreme Court Judge (two-thirds of members present and voting in each House), audits on behalf of Parliament and the State Legislatures.
- Article 149: duties and powers by law of Parliament (the 1971 Act). Article 150: form of accounts by the President on the C&AG's advice. Article 151: reports to the President or Governor, laid before the legislature.
- Duties, in order: compile accounts, general audit (Consolidated Fund expenditure, Contingency Funds, Public Accounts, departmental trading accounts), bodies substantially financed (grant ≥ ₹25 lakh **and** ≥ 75% of expenditure), specific-purpose grants, receipts, stores, government companies.
- Expenditure audit, in order: rules and orders, sanctions, provision of funds, propriety, performance.
- Propriety: improper, avoidable or ineffective expenditure even when regular; performance: efficiency, economy and effectiveness of large programmes.
- Receipts audit looks at the general more than the particular; its extent is fixed by the C&AG and is not negotiable.
- Government companies: the C&AG appoints and directs the auditor, may do a supplementary audit and comment within 60 days, and may order a test audit; comments go to members and the AGM; the board need not reply to them.
- Reports are factual; conclusions are left to the reader, recommendations to the PAC. (Nani reads the bills out; the council decides.)

## Flashcards

Q:: What does the Consolidated Fund of India consist of (Article 266)?
A:: All revenue received from direct and indirect taxes, all loans taken by the Government of India and all repayments of loans received by it.

Q:: How is the independence of the C&AG guaranteed by the Constitution?
A:: Appointed by the President; removable only on proven misbehaviour or incapacity, like a Supreme Court Judge, by each House with a majority of not less than two-thirds of members present and voting; salary and conditions fixed by Parliament and not variable to his disadvantage after appointment.

Q:: Articles 149, 150 and 151 in one line each?
A:: 149: the C&AG's duties and powers as prescribed by law of Parliament. 150: form of Union and State accounts prescribed by the President on the C&AG's advice. 151: C&AG reports go to the President or Governor, who has them laid before the legislature.

Q:: When is a body "substantially financed" for the C&AG's audit?
A:: When the grant or loan from a Consolidated Fund in a financial year is not less than ₹25 lakh and not less than 75% of the body's total expenditure.

Q:: Name the five basic standards of government expenditure audit, in order.
A:: Audit against rules and orders; audit of sanctions; audit against provision of funds; propriety audit; performance audit.

Q:: Propriety audit: what does it bring out?
A:: Cases of improper, avoidable or ineffective expenditure even though the expenditure has been incurred in conformity with the existing rules and regulations.

Q:: State the principles of financial propriety (a) and (b).
A:: (a) Expenditure should not be prima facie more than the occasion demands (the vigilance of a person of ordinary prudence over his own money). (b) No authority should sanction expenditure that is directly or indirectly to its own advantage.

Q:: Performance audit: which three audits does it cover, and what are its procedure steps?
A:: Efficiency, economy and effectiveness audit. Steps: identification of topic, preliminary study, planning, execution of audit, reporting.

Q:: Audit against rules and orders: four things audit checks in the executive's rules, regulations and orders?
A:: Not inconsistent with the Constitution or laws; consistent with the essential requirements of audit and accounts as determined by the C&AG; not in conflict with orders or rules of a higher authority; if not separately approved, the issuing authority has the rule-making power.

Q:: Section 143(6): what may the C&AG do, and within what time?
A:: Within 60 days from the date of receipt of the audit report: conduct a supplementary audit, and comment upon or supplement the audit report.

Q:: Must the board of a government company explain the C&AG's comments?
A:: No. Section 134(3) requires explanations only on the auditor's (and secretarial auditor's) reservations, adverse remarks and disclaimers; there is no similar provision for the C&AG's comments.

Q:: How does India's C&AG report its findings?
A:: Factually, leaving conclusions to the reader; recommendations have traditionally been left to the Public Accounts Committee.
