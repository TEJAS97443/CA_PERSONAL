## In plain words

### Chai in a can

Tapri Tea Co. Ltd wants to launch "Tapri Cold", chilled chai in a can, across India, and Kesar also has his eye on the Gulf.

> **Kesar:** Ananya, the product is perfect. Book the factory, print the cans, and find me a shop in Dubai.
> **Ananya:** The product is inside our walls, Kesar-ji. Most of what decides its fate is outside them: the summer heat, what young people drink, labelling law, interest rates, taxes, new canning machines.
> **Kesar:** None of that is in my control.
> **Ananya:** Exactly. That is why we study it first.

Everything outside a firm that weighs on its managers' minds is its **business environment**. It comes in layers. Closest in is the **micro environment**: the customers, suppliers, marketing intermediaries and competitors the firm deals with directly and regularly. Further out is the **macro environment**: forces such as the economy, society, technology and government that act on many firms at once and that no single firm controls. Beyond that is the **international environment**, the same forces in other countries.

Strategy begins with **strategic analysis**: reading these layers (and the firm's own strengths and weaknesses) before choosing a strategy, instead of trusting intuition. For the macro layer, the standard tool is **PESTLE**: a checklist of six kinds of outside influence, Political, Economic, Socio-cultural, Technological, Legal and Environmental, scanned for the opportunities and threats they bring.

::: analogy The trek and the weather
A trekking group deals every day with its guide, porters, the hotel and other groups on the trail: that is its micro environment, and it can bargain with all of them. It cannot bargain with the monsoon, the forest department's permit rules or a landslide on the highway: that is the macro environment. A sensible leader checks the forecast, the rules and the roads before setting out (PESTLE), and checks them again for a trek abroad, where the rules and the weather are different (the international environment).
:::

::: key
The micro environment acts on a firm directly and regularly; the macro environment shapes it from beyond its direct control. PESTLE scans the macro environment through six lenses (political, economic, socio-cultural, technological, legal, environmental) to find the drivers of change, the opportunities and threats a strategy must answer, at home and, for a firm going global, abroad.
:::

## How it works

### The environment in layers

Organisations do not operate in a vacuum: they continuously act and react to what happens outside their periphery. In the context of business, environment may be defined as **a set of all external factors that weigh in the minds of the managers**; by analogy with the atmosphere, it can be pictured in layers (SM 2.2–2.3).

### Strategic analysis: look before you leap

The process of strategy formulation begins with **strategic analysis**: compiling information about the internal and external environments to assess possibilities while formulating strategic objectives (SM 2.3). Strategy formulation is not a task in which managers can get by with intuition, opinions, instincts and creative thinking: judgments about strategy must flow from analysis of the external environment and of the firm's internal resources and capabilities (SM 2.3).

- **Environmental scanning** is natural and continuous; some firms do it informally, others with a formal structure. Using informal techniques alone exposes the organisation to missed opportunities and unanticipated hazards; a **systematic approach** is essential for managing risk and uncertainty (SM 2.3–2.4).
- The **two important situational considerations** are (1) industry and competitive conditions, and (2) the organisation's own capabilities, resources, internal strengths, weaknesses and market position (SM 2.4).
- The **analytical sequence** runs from strategic appraisal of the external and internal situation, to evaluation of strategic alternatives, to the final choice of strategy (SM 2.4).
- Strategic analysis has **two major limitations**: it gives many innovative options but does not tell which one to pick (they may overlap, confuse or be hard to implement); and it can be time-consuming, hurting overall functioning and straining other innovations such as a new product (SM 2.5).

**Issues to consider** (SM 2.5–2.6): **strategy evolves over time** (a current strategy is the result of many small choices made over a long period and is updated as results become clear); a **balance of external and internal factors** (a perfect fit is unlikely, so opportunities, influences and constraints must be balanced); and **risk**, external (inconsistencies between strategies and environmental forces) or internal (forces within the organisation or interacting with it routinely):

| Strategic risk | Short time | Long time |
|---|---|---|
| External | Errors in interpreting the environment cause strategic failure | Changes in the environment make the strategy obsolete |
| Internal | Organisational capacity cannot cope with strategic demands | Changes in internal capacities and preferences create inconsistencies with the strategy |

**The framework of strategic analysis** (SM 2.7) lists what each side covers:

| Side | Analysis | Covers |
|---|---|---|
| External | Customer analysis | Segments, motivations, unmet needs |
| External | Competitor analysis | Strategic groups, performance, objectives, strategies, culture, cost structure |
| External | Market analysis | Size, growth, profitability, entry barriers |
| External | Environmental analysis | Technological, government, economic, cultural, demographic |
| Internal | Performance analysis | Profitability, sales, customer satisfaction, product quality, relative cost, new products, human resources |
| Internal | Determinants analysis | Past and current strategies, strategic problems, capabilities and constraints, financial resources, strengths and weaknesses |

External analysis yields opportunities, threats, trends and strategic uncertainties; internal analysis yields strengths, weaknesses, problems, constraints and uncertainties. Both lead to strategy identification and selection: identify strategic alternatives, select a strategy, implement the operating plan, review strategies (SM 2.7). The two halves meet in [[sm.int.swot|SWOT analysis]].

### The business environment and why interaction pays

::: definition Business environment
"All external factors, influences, or situations that in some way affect business decisions, plans, and operations." Organisational success is determined by its business environment, and even more from its relationship with it. (SM 2.9)
:::

There is a close and continuous interaction between a business and its environment, which strengthens the firm and helps it use its resources more effectively. It helps the business in five ways (SM 2.9):

1. **Determine opportunities and threats**: new needs and wants of consumers, changes in laws and social behaviour, new products competitors are bringing.
2. **Give direction for growth**: areas for growth and expansion become visible once the business understands the changes around it.
3. **Continuous learning**: managers are motivated to keep updating their knowledge, understanding and skills to meet predicted changes.
4. **Image building**: showing sensitivity to the environment improves the firm's image. The Study Material's example: in view of power shortages, many companies set up captive power plants for their factories and extended surplus capacity to the vicinity.
5. **Meeting competition**: analysing competitors' strategies and formulating one's own accordingly.

To flourish, a business must be aware of, assess and respond to the opportunities and threats in its environment, continuously evaluating it and modifying operations (SM 2.10).

### Micro and macro environment

The external environment has two major types (SM 2.10–2.11):

::: definition Micro environment
The small area or immediate periphery of an organisation, which influences it **regularly and directly**: suppliers, consumers, marketing intermediaries, competitors and the like, specific to the business. (SM 2.11)
:::

::: definition Macro environment
The portion of the outside world that significantly affects how an organisation operates but is **typically much beyond its direct control and influence**: economic, socio-cultural, technological, political and legal factors. (SM 2.11)
:::

Within the micro environment a firm must address six issues (SM 2.11): its employees, their characteristics and organisation; the existing customer base it relies on; the ways it can raise finance; who its suppliers are and how the links with them are developed; the local community within which it operates; and the direct competition and its comparative performance. The micro factors often relate the firm to the macro issues influencing the way it reacts in the market place.

| Point | Micro environment | Macro environment |
|---|---|---|
| Reach | Small area, immediate periphery | Broader dimensions of the outside world |
| Influence | Regular and direct | Significant |
| Control | Within the firm's direct dealings | Typically much beyond its direct control |
| Specific to | This business or firm | Largely general: socio-cultural factors act on almost all firms alike; political-legal ones are partly general, partly specific (SM 2.12, 2.14) |
| Examples | Suppliers, consumers, intermediaries, competitors | Economic, socio-cultural, technological, political, legal |

### Elements of the macro environment

"The environment includes factors outside the firm which can lead to opportunities for, or threats to the firm. Although, there are many factors, the most important of the factors are socio-economic, technological, supplier, competitors, and government" (Glueck and Jauch, quoted at SM 2.11). Classifying the environment into sectors helps a firm cope with its complexity. The Study Material describes five (SM 2.12–2.15):

**Demographic environment.** Demographics are the characteristics of a population classified by criteria such as age, gender and income: race, age, income, education, possession of assets, house ownership, job position, region. India's relatively young and large population attracts many multinationals. Firms must ask: *what demographic trends will affect the market size of the industry?* and *what demographic trends represent opportunities or threats?* The size, age distribution, geographic dispersion, ethnic mix and income distribution of a population all matter (SM 2.12).

**Socio-cultural environment.** A general factor that influences almost all enterprises in a similar manner, made up of a complex group of factors: social traditions, values and beliefs, level and standards of literacy, ethical standards, social stratification, conflict and cohesiveness. It differs from demographics because it is **not the characteristics of the population but its behaviour and belief system**. Core beliefs are persistent and hard for a business to change, so businesses adjust to social norms. It affects strategic management chiefly in **mission and objective setting**, and in **decisions on products and markets** (SM 2.12–2.13).

**Economic environment.** The overall economic situation at regional, national and global levels, including the markets for resources that affect the supply, cost, dependability, quality and availability of inputs. It determines the strength and size of the market: purchasing power depends on current income, prices, savings, circulation of money, debt and credit availability, and the income distribution pattern determines business possibilities. Higher interest rates hurt businesses with high debt, and in real estate they cut buyers' ability to borrow and so lower demand. The factors include GDP, per capita income, markets for goods and services, availability of capital, foreign exchange reserves, growth of foreign trade, strength of the capital market, interest rates, disposable income, unemployment and inflation (SM 2.13).

**Political-legal environment.** The general level of political development, how far business issues are politicised, political morality, law and order, political stability, the ruling party's ideology and practices, the effectiveness of government agencies, and the scope and type of government intervention; it is **partly general to all similar enterprises and partly specific** to an individual one. Business is highly guided by government policy: regulatory changes, taxes and duties. Businesses prefer a sound legal system and must know the laws on companies, competition, intellectual property, foreign exchange and labour. Nationalism also shapes it: *Make in India*, *Aatmanirbhar Bharat* and the **Production Linked Incentive** scheme, which rewards higher sales of domestically produced goods, invites foreign firms to set up in India and reduces reliance on imports (SM 2.14).

**Technological environment.** Technology has changed how people communicate and how businesses operate: less paperwork, efficient payment scheduling and inventory coordination, lower costs, and shrinking time and distance. Technological advances may force drastic changes in operational, production and marketing strategies. Technology creates new business opportunities while making existing products and services obsolete, so it is **both an opportunity and a threat**; artificial intelligence, machine learning and robotic process automation are the Study Material's examples (SM 2.14–2.15).

### PESTLE: the macro scan

::: definition PESTLE analysis
A framework for the analysis of macro environmental factors. It involves ==identifying the political, economic, socio-cultural, technological, legal and environmental influences on an organization== and providing a way of scanning the environmental influences that have affected or are likely to affect an organization or its policy. (SM 2.15)
:::

The framework was earlier called **PEST** (political, economic, social, technological) and was later expanded to include environmental and legal factors (SM 2.15). It is **simple to understand and quick to implement**, and its advantage is that it **encourages management into proactive and structured thinking** in decision making (SM 2.16).

**The key factors** (SM 2.16–2.17):

- **Political** factors are how and to what extent the government intervenes in the economy and in business; they also shape the goods and services the government wants (or does not want) provided, and governments greatly influence a nation's health, education and infrastructure.
- **Economic** factors have major impacts on how businesses operate and decide: interest rates affect the cost of capital and so how far a business grows; exchange rates affect the cost of exports and the supply and price of imports; money supply, inflation, credit flow, per capita income and growth rates matter too.
- **Social** factors affect the demand for a company's products and how the company operates.
- **Technological** factors can determine barriers to entry and the minimum efficient production level, and influence outsourcing decisions; technological shifts affect costs and quality and lead to innovation.
- **Legal** factors affect how a company operates, its costs, the demand for its products, and the ease of business.
- **Environmental** factors affect industries such as tourism, farming and insurance; growing awareness of climate change affects how companies operate and what they offer, both creating new markets and diminishing or destroying existing ones.

From these, a firm identifies the key environmental influences that are, in effect, **the drivers of change**: the factors to be considered in making meaningful decisions (SM 2.17). *Learn the Study Material's table below as six lists.*

| Factor | Drivers of change (SM 2.17) |
|---|---|
| Political | Political stability; political principles and ideologies; current and future taxation policy; regulatory bodies and processes; government policies; government term and change; thrust areas of political leaders |
| Economic | Economy situation and trends; market and trade cycles; specific industry factors; customer/end-user drivers; interest and exchange rates; inflation and unemployment; strength of consumer spending |
| Social | Lifestyle trends; demographics; consumer attitudes and opinions; brand, company, technology image; consumer buying patterns; ethnic/religious factors; media views and perception |
| Technological | Replacement technology/solutions; maturity of technology; manufacturing maturity and capacity; innovation potential; technology access, licensing, patents, property rights and copyrights |
| Legal | Business and corporate laws; employment law; competition law; health and safety law; international treaty and law; regional legislation |
| Environmental | Ecological/environmental issues; environmental hazards; environmental legislation; energy consumption; waste disposal |

### Going international

Internationalisation has emerged as the dominant commercial trend of the last couple of decades. It lets a business enter new markets in search of greater earnings and less expensive resources, achieve greater **economies of scale** and **extend the lifespan of its products**. The strategic-management process is essentially the same for global and domestic firms, but international processes are much more complicated because of additional variables and linkages; scanning the external environment is one way to identify opportunities and threats in global markets (SM 2.18).

**A global business has three characteristics** (SM 2.18):

1. It is a conglomerate of multiple units, located in different parts of the globe, all **linked by common ownership**.
2. The units draw on a **common pool of resources**: money, credit, information, patents, trade names and control systems.
3. The units **respond to some common strategy**; its managers and shareholders are also based in different nations.

**Steps in international strategic planning.** International development is expensive and challenging, so a thorough and structured approach is ideal (SM 2.18–2.19):

::: steps
1. **Evaluate global opportunities and threats** and rate them against the internal capabilities.
2. **Describe the scope** of the firm's global commercial operations.
3. **Create the firm's global business objectives.**
4. **Develop distinct corporate strategies** for the global business and the whole organisation.
:::

**Why do businesses go global?** Technological developments and evolving political views are the two important factors behind the rise of multinationals: worldwide communication links headquarters with operations abroad, and better transport has increased the mobility of money, people, raw materials and finished goods (SM 2.19). The reasons, in the Study Material's order (SM 2.19–2.20):

1. The **need to grow**, the first and foremost reason: opportunities found in other parts of the globe.
2. **Rapid shrinking of time and distance**: faster communication, speedier transport, growing financial flows, rapid technological change.
3. **Domestic markets are no longer adequate**, and the competition present at home may not exist in some international markets.
4. **Reliable or cheaper raw materials, cheap labour**, or a vast pool of talent.
5. **Reducing high transportation costs** by producing near the market.
6. **Foreign markets opening up or growing big**: exporters then set up overseas plants and sales branches for higher sales and better cash flow.
7. **The rise of services** as the largest single sector of the world economy, and **regional economic integration**.
8. **The collapse of international trade barriers**: more privatisation, less government interference, lower tariffs and customs barriers, more flow of business.
9. **Strategic alliances** across countries, to ward off economic and technological threats and leverage comparative and competitive advantages.

### The international environment

The social, cultural, demographic, environmental, political, governmental, legal and technological factors an international organisation faces are nearly limitless, and they multiply as the number of products and geographic areas grows. **An assessment of the external environment is the first step toward internationalisation** (SM 2.20). It can be done at **three levels** (SM 2.20):

| Level | What it involves |
|---|---|
| Multinational | Identifying, anticipating and monitoring significant components of the global environment on a large scale: global economic and macro developments, and governments' free or interventionist tendencies, judged on present and expected future impact |
| Regional | A more in-depth evaluation of the critical factors in a specific geographical area, to discover market opportunities for goods, services or innovations there |
| Country | A deeper look at the economic, legal, political and cultural dimensions, customised for each country, to develop effective market-entry strategies |

The international environment is now inherent to strategic management for businesses of all sizes with global interests. It involves **political risks, cultural differences, exchange rate fluctuations, legal compliances and taxation issues** (SM 2.21).

## Worked examples

:::: example Tapri Cold: PESTLE or not?
Before launching "Tapri Cold", a canned chilled chai, across India in 2026-27, Ananya's team collects these findings:

1. The government has announced a proposal to raise the tax rate on sweetened packaged beverages.
2. Interest rates have risen, so the loan for the ₹200 crore canning plant will cost more.
3. Young urban buyers are moving from fizzy drinks to "less sugar, more real" drinks.
4. A new aseptic canning process keeps the drink fresh for nine months without preservatives.
5. New food-labelling rules will require a front-of-pack sugar warning.
6. Rules on producer responsibility for packaging waste are tightening, while longer and hotter summers are raising demand for cold drinks.
7. BrewBucks is testing its own canned cold coffee in Mumbai.
8. The only domestic can maker capable of Tapri's volumes is asking for 60 days' advance payment.

**Required:**

(i) Classify each finding as a PESTLE factor (name it) or as part of the micro environment.

(ii) State whether each PESTLE finding is an opportunity or a threat for Tapri Cold.

(iii) Explain how a PESTLE analysis helps Tapri in this decision.

::: answer Model answer
**(i) and (ii) Classification**

| No. | Factor | Opportunity or threat |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Political: taxation policy | Threat (higher price or lower margin) |
| 2 | Economic: interest rates | Threat (higher cost of capital) |
| 3 | Socio-cultural: lifestyle trends, consumer attitudes | Opportunity (demand for Tapri's positioning) |
| 4 | Technological: replacement technology, innovation potential | Opportunity (no preservatives, wider reach) |
| 5 | Legal: health and safety law | Threat (cost; affects demand for a sweet drink) |
| 6 | Environmental: waste disposal, environmental legislation, climate | Both: waste rules add cost; hotter summers add demand |
| 7 | Micro: competitor | Not a PESTLE factor |
| 8 | Micro: supplier | Not a PESTLE factor |

Findings 7 and 8 belong to the micro environment, the immediate periphery of the firm that influences it regularly and directly (suppliers, consumers, marketing intermediaries, competitors). They are studied through competitor analysis and the industry's competitive forces, not through PESTLE.

**(iii) How PESTLE helps.** PESTLE analysis involves identifying the political, economic, socio-cultural, technological, legal and environmental influences on an organisation and provides a way of scanning the environmental influences that have affected or are likely to affect it. For Tapri it:

- separates the macro forces, which are much beyond Tapri's control, from the micro issues it can negotiate (the can maker's terms, the response to BrewBucks);
- identifies the key influences, the drivers of change, to be considered in the launch decision: the tax proposal and labelling rules (a less-sugar recipe answers both and fits the lifestyle trend), interest rates (phase the plant or lease capacity), the canning technology (a selling point), and waste rules (budget for take-back of cans);
- is simple to understand and quick to implement, and encourages management into proactive and structured thinking, so that threats are planned for before the plant is built.
:::

## Under the hood

### Industries differ, and so do their environments

Industries differ widely in economic characteristics, competitive situations and profit prospects: size and growth rate, pace of technological change, the geographic boundaries of the market, the number and size of buyers and sellers, how differentiated the products are, economies of scale, and distribution channels. Competition centres on price in some industries, on quality, features, quick service or brand reputation in others. An industry's traits and competitive conditions decide whether its profit prospects are poor, average or excellent: leading companies in unattractive industries can find it hard to earn respectable profits, while even weak companies in attractive industries can do well (SM 2.7–2.8). Reading the industry layer is the job of [[sm.ext.porter|Porter's five forces]] and [[sm.ext.industry|the product life cycle and value chain]].

### Demographic or socio-cultural?

The Study Material draws the line itself: demographics are the **characteristics** of a population (age, income, education, region); the socio-cultural environment is its **behaviour and belief system** (SM 2.12). "40% of Indore is under 25" is demographic; "young people in Indore prefer cafés to tea stalls for dates" is socio-cultural. In PESTLE both sit under **S**, since the Study Material's social drivers include demographics (SM 2.17).

### Political or legal?

The Study Material treats them as one "political-legal" element (SM 2.14) but as two letters in PESTLE, and its drivers table draws the line: **political** covers stability, ideology, taxation policy, regulatory bodies and processes, government policies and the thrust areas of political leaders; **legal** covers the laws themselves (business and corporate, employment, competition, health and safety, international and regional) (SM 2.17). ICAI's MCQ keys follow that line when a firm must comply with a specific rule: regulatory approvals for a new drug are keyed **Legal**, not Political (MTP May 2025 Series II MCQ (B)(iii)), and "changes in data privacy regulations", not "government incentives for digital transformation", is keyed as the PESTLE factor that forces a firm to change its data practices (MTP January 2026 Series II MCQ (A)(i)). In descriptive answers the line is looser: the Study Material's own answer on ABC Corp puts "regulatory frameworks" under political and "laws and regulations related to foreign investment, intellectual property rights, and labor laws" under legal (SM 2.51). Make each point once, under the heading that fits it best.

## Answer frame

### "How does PESTLE help X decide Y?" (5 marks)

1. One line: PESTLE identifies the political, economic, socio-cultural, technological, legal and environmental influences on an organisation and provides a way of scanning those that have affected or are likely to affect it.
2. **Six headings in order**, each with the Study Material's drivers that fit the case and what X learns:

| Heading | Drivers to pick from |
|---|---|
| Political | Stability, ideology, taxation policy, regulators, government policies and thrust areas |
| Economic | Economic trends, trade cycles, interest and exchange rates, inflation, unemployment, consumer spending |
| Socio-cultural | Lifestyle, demographics, attitudes, buying patterns, ethnic and religious factors, media |
| Technological | Replacement technology, maturity, manufacturing capacity, innovation, access, licensing, patents |
| Legal | Corporate, employment, competition, health and safety law, treaties, regional legislation |
| Environmental | Ecological issues, hazards, environmental legislation, energy use, waste disposal |

**3. Close:** a comprehensive view of the macro environment, simple and quick, encouraging proactive and structured thinking for an informed decision.

### "Explain business environment and how interaction helps" (5 marks)

Definition (all external factors, influences or situations that affect business decisions, plans and operations), then the five ways: opportunities and threats; direction for growth; continuous learning; image building; meeting competition. In a case, apply each to the facts.

### Three answers to "PESTLE for a new country"

::: cols
**Loses marks**

"PESTLE means political, economic, social, technological, legal and environmental. ABC should study all of them."

The acronym, with nothing applied.
---
**Passes**

Six headings, each with general examples ("government stability", "inflation").

Correct but generic: nothing from the case, no consequence for the decision.
---
**Full marks**

The one-line purpose, six headings with drivers chosen for the case and what each tells the firm (risk, cost, demand, compliance), and a closing line on the decision.
:::

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: BrewBucks in the PESTLE table
Competitors, suppliers and dealers listed under "economic" or "political". **Fix:** they are the **micro** environment, acting on the firm regularly and directly; the Study Material's own question asks which is *not* an element of the macro environment, and the answer is competitors (SM 2.47, 2.50). PESTLE is for forces much beyond the firm's direct control.
:::

::: mistake Every regulation filed under "political"
**Fix:** government intervention, policy, taxation and stability are political; the specific laws a firm must comply with (company, employment, competition, health and safety, data and product rules) are legal (SM 2.17). In the MCQs, ICAI keyed regulatory approvals for a new drug as legal, not political.
:::

::: mistake Demographics and culture used as synonyms
**Fix:** demographics are characteristics (age, income, region); socio-cultural factors are behaviour and beliefs (SM 2.12). An answer on "what kind of factor is the age profile of buyers?" says demographic.
:::

::: mistake Planning steps out of order
Writing "set global objectives" before "evaluate global opportunities and threats". **Fix:** evaluate (against internal capabilities), describe the scope, create objectives, develop strategies: you cannot set objectives for markets you have not assessed.
:::

## Recap

- Business environment: all external factors, influences or situations that affect business decisions, plans and operations; interaction with it gives opportunities and threats, direction for growth, continuous learning, image building and meeting competition.
- Micro environment: immediate periphery, regular and direct influence (suppliers, consumers, marketing intermediaries, competitors); macro environment: much beyond direct control.
- Strategic analysis precedes strategy: two considerations (industry and competitive conditions; own capabilities and position); limitations: many options with no pick, time-consuming; issues: strategy evolves, balance, risk.
- Macro elements: demographic (characteristics), socio-cultural (behaviour and beliefs), economic, political-legal, technological.
- PESTLE: political, economic, socio-cultural, technological, legal, environmental; earlier PEST; simple, quick, proactive and structured; it finds the drivers of change.
- Global business: units in many countries, common ownership, common resources, common strategy. Planning: evaluate, describe scope, set objectives, develop strategies.
- International environment: assess first, at multinational, regional and country levels; political risk, culture, exchange rates, legal compliance, taxation.
- Nine reasons to go global, led by the need to grow. (Tapri can phone its supplier, not the monsoon.)

## Flashcards

Q:: Define business environment (SM 2.9).
A:: All external factors, influences or situations that in some way affect business decisions, plans and operations.

Q:: Five ways in which a business's interaction with its environment helps it?
A:: Determine opportunities and threats; give direction for growth; continuous learning; image building; meeting competition.

Q:: Micro environment: meaning and constituents?
A:: The immediate periphery of the organisation, influencing it regularly and directly: suppliers, consumers, marketing intermediaries, competitors.

Q:: Macro environment: meaning?
A:: The part of the outside world that significantly affects how an organisation operates but is much beyond its direct control: economic, socio-cultural, technological, political and legal factors.

Q:: What does PESTLE stand for, and what was it called before?
A:: Political, economic, socio-cultural, technological, legal, environmental. Earlier PEST; later expanded to include legal and environmental factors.

Q:: Advantage of PESTLE analysis (SM 2.16)?
A:: Simple to understand and quick to implement; it encourages management into proactive and structured thinking in decision making.

Q:: How does the socio-cultural environment differ from the demographic environment?
A:: Demographics are the characteristics of a population (age, gender, income); socio-cultural factors are its behaviour and belief system.

Q:: Two major limitations of strategic analysis?
A:: It gives many options but does not tell which to pick; it can be time-consuming, hurting functioning and other innovations.

Q:: Three characteristics of a global business?
A:: Multiple units in different parts of the globe linked by common ownership; a common pool of resources; units responding to some common strategy.

Q:: Steps in international strategic planning, in order?
A:: Evaluate global opportunities and threats against internal capabilities; describe the scope of global operations; create global business objectives; develop distinct corporate strategies.

Q:: Three levels at which the international environment is assessed?
A:: Multinational (global, large scale), regional (a specific geographical area), country (economic, legal, political and cultural dimensions of each country).

Q:: The first and foremost reason why businesses go global?
A:: The need to grow.
