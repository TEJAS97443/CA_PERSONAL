## In plain words

### Kesar wants to be bigger than BrewBucks

Tapri Tea Co. Ltd makes chai premix in Indore and sells it through shops and its own kulhad cafés. BrewBucks has just opened coffee shops in twelve of Tapri's cities, and Kesar has called a 7 a.m. meeting.

> **Kesar:** Ananya, we must grow. Buy a tea garden in Assam! Buy Kadak Chai! And start making cricket bats.
> **Ananya:** Those are three different strategies, Kesar-ji. Only one has nothing to do with chai.
> **Kesar:** Cricket bats. Everyone at a tapri talks about cricket. Sab adjust ho jayega.

Growth (**expansion**) means enlarging the scope of the business and putting substantially more money into it. Every way of doing it answers one question: *how is the new business linked to the old one?*

- More of the present products, to present or new customers, or related new items for present customers: **intensification**.
- A business linked to chai by process, technology or marketing: **concentric diversification**. The tea garden is the step *before* Tapri's factory in the chain (**backward integration**); the kulhad cafés, which sell to the drinker, are the step *after* it (opening them was **forward integration**); Kadak Chai, a rival premix maker, is the *same* step (**horizontal integration**).
- A business with no link at all: **conglomerate diversification**. That is the cricket bat.

Tapri can grow alone (intensifying, diversifying, **innovating**), or with others: a **merger** or **acquisition** (two firms become one) or a **strategic alliance** (two firms work together and stay separate).

::: analogy The tailor who wants to grow
A tailor can stitch more shirts for the same customers; start making shoes for them; buy the cloth mill that supplies him; open a showroom for his shirts; buy the tailor shop next door; or open a mobile-repair counter. The first is intensification. The next four are related moves: shoes sold to his own customers, then backward, forward and horizontal. The last is conglomerate: nothing he knows about shirts helps him repair phones.
:::

::: key
Classify a growth move by its link with the present business. Linked by process, technology or marketing: concentric, either vertical (an earlier or later step of the same chain) or horizontal (the same step). No link of product, market or technology: conglomerate.
:::

## How it works

### Expansion in ICAI's words

Expansion is one of the four corporate strategies, with stability, retrenchment and combination (SM 4.4–4.5; the other three are in [[sm.choice.retrench|stability and retrenchment]], and the corporate level itself in [[sm.intro.levels|strategy levels]]).

::: definition Growth/Expansion strategy
Growth/Expansion strategy is implemented by redefining the business by enlarging the scope of business and ==substantially increasing investment in the business==. It is a strategy that can be equated with dynamism, vigour, promise and success. (SM 4.7)
:::

**Characteristics** (SM 4.7–4.8):

1. It involves a **redefinition of the business** of the corporation.
2. It is the **opposite of stability**: rewards in stability are limited, in expansion very high; the risks are opposites too.
3. It leads to **business growth**; a firm with a mammoth growth ambition can meet its objective only through expansion.
4. **Renewal** of the firm through fresh investments and new businesses, products or markets is facilitated only by expansion.
5. It is a **highly versatile** strategy, offering several permutations and combinations for growth: by altering its propositions regarding products, markets and functions, the firm can generate many alternatives and pick the one that suits it most.
6. It holds within its fold two major routes, **intensification and diversification**; both are growth strategies, and the difference lies in the way the firm actually pursues the growth.

**Major reasons for expansion strategy** (SM 4.8):

1. It may become imperative when the environment demands an increase in the pace of activity.
2. Strategists may feel more satisfied with the prospects of growth; chief executives may take pride in presiding over organisations perceived to be growth-oriented.
3. Expansion may lead to greater control over the market vis-a-vis competitors.
4. Advantages from the experience curve and scale of operations may accrue.
5. Expansion also includes intensifying, diversifying, acquiring and merging businesses (the line that leads into the types of growth below; ICAI's answer to MTP September 2024 Series II Q3(b) lists it as the fifth reason).

### I. Intensification: more from what you have

The organisation grows internally by intensifying its operations, cashing in on its internal capabilities and internal resources (SM 4.8–4.9):

- **Market penetration**: highly common; concentration on the current business, the firm directing its resources to the profitable growth of its existing product in the existing market.
- **Market development**: marketing present products to customers in related market areas, by adding different channels of distribution or by changing the content of advertising or the promotional media.
- **Product development**: substantial modification of existing products, or creation of new but related items that can be marketed to current customers through established channels.

### II. Diversification: a new business

::: definition Diversification
Diversification is defined as an entry into new products or product lines, new services or new markets, involving substantially different skills, technology and knowledge. (SM 4.9)
:::

Innovative and creative firms feel that diversification offers greater prospects of growth and profitability than intensification (SM 4.9). Two reasons in the Study Material (SM 4.10):

1. **Better use of existing facilities and capabilities**: the firm may have excess capacity or capability in manufacturing facilities, investible funds, marketing channels, competitive standing, market prestige, managerial and other manpower, research and development, raw material sources and so forth.
2. **Synergy**: it may be possible to improve the sales and profits of existing products by adding suitably related or new products, because of linkages in technology and/or in markets.

Based on the nature and extent of the relationship with existing businesses there are two broad categories: **concentric** (into related business, for synergistic gains) and **conglomerate** (into unrelated business, to explore opportunities beyond existing areas of expertise).

**(i) Concentric diversification** (related): the new business is ==linked to the existing businesses through process, technology or marketing==. The new product is a spin-off from the existing facilities and products or processes, so there are benefits of synergy with current operations; it is connected in a loop-like manner at one or more points in the firm's existing process, technology or product chain. ICAI's example: a company producing clothes ventures into the manufacture of shoes (SM 4.10). It is generally understood in two directions (SM 4.11–4.12):

- **(a) Vertically integrated diversification**: the firm remains in the vertically linked product-process chain and moves forward or backward in it.
    - **Backward integration** is concerned with creation of effective supply by entering the business of input providers, to increase its own supply capability or lessen its cost of production. Example: a supermarket chain buys farms that supply its fresh produce.
    - **Forward integration** is moving forward in the value chain into business lines that use existing products, including the businesses of distribution channels. Example: a coffee bean manufacturer merges with a coffee café.
- **(b) Horizontal integrated diversification**: a firm gets horizontally diversified by integrating through acquisition of one or more similar businesses operating at the **same stage** of the production-marketing chain; it can also integrate with firms producing complementary products or by-products, or take over competitors' products. Example: textile mill 1 acquires textile mills 2 and 3.

Example: a cement manufacturer diversifies into steel and rubber products (SM 4.12). |---|---|
| Exchange or share assets or competencies by exploiting: brand name; marketing skills; sales and distribution capacity; manufacturing skills; R&D and new product capability; economies of scale | Investment in new product portfolios; employment of new technologies; focus on multiple products; reduce risk by operating in multiple product markets; defend against takeover bids; provide executive interest |

*Is it worth diversifying into unrelated products?* Despite its complexity, conglomerate diversification financially makes a lot of sense (SM 4.13): it gives access to a new pool of customers, expanding the customer base; it allows access to markets and cross-selling of new products, increasing revenues; and it eases the management of losses, since profits in one business can keep a loss-making business afloat within the same organisation.

### (iii) Innovation

The Study Material lists expansion through innovation as item (iii), straight after the two kinds of diversification, within internal growth (SM 4.10, 4.13). Innovation drives upgradation of existing product lines or processes, leading to increased market share, revenues, profitability and, most important, customer satisfaction. Some argue that innovation leads to unnecessary expenses that do not give as much returns; on the contrary, for long-term growth it offers (SM 4.13–4.14):

1. **Helps to solve complex problems**: a business finds opportunities in existing problems of society through planned innovation in areas of expertise, developing customer-centric sustainable solutions (the shift to solar, wind and sea-wave energy: costly at first, sustainable in the long run).
2. **Increases productivity**: innovation simplifies and often automates existing tasks (MS Excel for finance professionals), adding to the productivity of teams and the organisation, with a ripple effect across industries.
3. **Gives competitive advantage**: the faster a business innovates, the farther it goes from its competitors' reach; innovative products need less marketing, retain existing customers and help acquire new ones with ease.

### B. External growth: mergers and acquisitions

Acquisition or merger with an existing concern is an **instant means** of achieving expansion: it circumvents the time, risks and skills involved in screening internal growth opportunities, seizing them and building up the resource base. Apart from the urge to grow, firms merge for **synergy**, which may come from physical facilities, technical and managerial skills, distribution channels, general administration, research and development and so on; only positive synergy is relevant, where the merged resources achieve more than the individual resources did before (SM 4.14–4.15).

- **Merger**: two or more companies come together to expand their business operations; the deal is finalised on friendly terms and both share profits in the newly created entity, combining to increase their strength and financial gains.
- **Acquisition**: one organisation takes over the other and controls all its business operations. The financially strong overpowers the weaker one, often during a recession or declining profit margins; the combined operations run under the name of the powerful entity, and the deal is often unfriendly, a forced association (SM 4.15).

The Study Material names four types of merger, quite similar to the types of diversification (SM 4.16–4.17):

| Type of merger | Who combines | Purpose or example |
|---|---|---|
| Horizontal | Firms in the same industry: a direct competitor | Economies of scale by shedding duplication of installations and functions, widening the product line, less working capital and fixed assets, getting rid of competition; Brooke Bond Lipton India Ltd. |
| Vertical | Same industry, different stages of production or distribution | Taking over suppliers is backward integration, buyers or distribution channels forward; operating and financial economies; an advantage from restricting inputs to other players or supplying them at a higher cost |
| Co-generic | Firms related by production processes, business markets or basic technologies | Extending the product line, or acquiring components required in daily operations; diversifying around a common set of resources: a refrigerator maker with a kitchen-appliance firm |
| Conglomerate | Unrelated firms: no linkage of customer groups, functions or technologies | In practice some overlap exists |

### B. External growth: strategic alliance

::: definition Strategic alliance
A strategic alliance is a relationship between two or more businesses that enables each to achieve certain strategic objectives which neither would be able to achieve on its own. The strategic partners maintain their status as independent and separate entities, share the benefits and control over the partnership, and continue to make contributions to the alliance until it is terminated. (SM 4.17)
:::

Strategic alliances are often formed in the global marketplace between businesses based in different regions of the world. An alliance is usually formed only if it provides an advantage to all the parties. **Advantages** (SM 4.17–4.18):

1. **Organisational**: it helps to learn necessary skills and obtain capabilities from partners; partners may enhance productive capacity, provide a distribution system or extend the supply chain; a complementary good or service creates synergy; a well-known and respected partner adds legitimacy and credibility to a new venture.
2. **Economic**: costs and risks are reduced by distributing them across the members; greater economies of scale as production volume rises and cost per unit falls; co-specialisation creates additional value (a leading computer maker bundling a leading monitor maker's monitor).
3. **Strategic**: rivals join together to cooperate instead of competing; vertical integration where partners are part of the supply chain; pooling of resources and skills creates competitive advantage, future business opportunities and new products and technologies; access to new technologies or joint research and development.
4. **Political**: an alliance with a local foreign business gains entry into a foreign market despite local prejudices or legal barriers to entry; politically influential partners improve influence and position.

**Disadvantages** (SM 4.18): the major disadvantage is **sharing**: of resources and profits, and of knowledge and skills that organisations may not like to share. Sharing is problematic for trade secrets; agreements protect them only as far as the parties are willing to abide by them or courts to enforce them. An ally may also become an opponent in future when it decides to separate out.

## Worked examples

::::: example Three acquisitions, three wrong labels
Three acquisition proposals reach Tapri's board, each with its sponsor's label:

(i) **Dev** wants to buy Rail Chai Express, a chain of 120 railway-station tea stalls that today buys its premix from Tapri and from Kadak Chai. His label: "horizontal integration, because both of us sell chai."

(ii) **Ritu** wants to buy Malwa Flexi-Pack Pvt Ltd, which makes the laminated foil from which Tapri's premix sachets are formed. Her label: "conglomerate diversification: plastics have nothing to do with chai, so it spreads our risk."

(iii) **Kesar** wants to buy Meerut Willow Works, which makes cricket bats from English willow and sells them through sports shops. His label: "concentric diversification, because everyone at a tapri talks about cricket."

Required:

(a) Examine whether each label is correct, and identify the correct type of diversification with reasons.

(b) Which of the three proposals can give Tapri synergy with its present business?

:::: answer Model answer
**(a)**

**(i) The label is incorrect: this is forward integration**, a form of vertically integrated (concentric) diversification. Horizontal integration means acquiring similar businesses operating at the **same stage** of the production-marketing chain, such as another premix maker. Rail Chai Express buys Tapri's premix and sells tea to the traveller, so it is a later stage of the same chain. Forward integration is moving forward in the value chain into business lines that use existing products, including the businesses of distribution channels. Selling the same drink does not place the two businesses at the same stage.

**(ii) The label is incorrect: this is backward integration**, a form of vertically integrated (concentric) diversification. The foil is an input to Tapri's product. Backward integration is concerned with creation of effective supply by entering the business of input providers, to increase the firm's own supply capability or lessen its cost of production. A business that looks unrelated can still be a link in the firm's own product-process chain; conglomerate diversification requires that no linkage exists at all.

**(iii) The label is incorrect: this is conglomerate diversification.** Concentric diversification needs a link with the existing business through process, technology or marketing. Bats are made from willow by a different process and sold through sports shops to cricketers, so no linkages related to product, market or technology exist; the new business has no common thread with Tapri's present position. What customers talk about over a cup is not a marketing link.

**(b) Synergy.** Concentric diversification brings benefits of synergy with current operations, because the new business is connected at one or more points of the firm's existing process, technology or product chain. Proposals (i) and (ii) can therefore give synergy: (i) assured outlets for Tapri's premix, (ii) assured supply of packaging at lower cost. Proposal (iii) gives none: being conglomerate, it can only be justified as an opportunity beyond Tapri's existing areas of expertise.

::::: example Tapri and Kadak Chai share a desk and a truck
Tapri and Kadak Chai Pvt Ltd compete for every grocery shelf in Madhya Pradesh. Both buy Assam CTC tea at the Guwahati auctions, and both send half-empty trucks to the North-East. They sign a five-year agreement: one joint buying desk at the auctions and one shared fleet of trucks to Guwahati and Shillong, with costs split by tonnage. Neither buys shares in the other; each keeps its own brands, blends and prices, and they go on competing in the shops.

Required:

(i) Identify the growth strategy, and state with reasons whether it is a merger.

(ii) Explain the advantages the two companies may derive, under the Study Material's categories.

(iii) State the main risk for Tapri.

:::: answer Model answer
**(i) Strategic alliance**, an external growth strategy. A strategic alliance is a relationship between two or more businesses that enables each to achieve certain strategic objectives which neither would be able to achieve on its own; the strategic partners maintain their status as independent and separate entities, share the benefits and control over the partnership, and continue to make contributions to the alliance until it is terminated. It is **not a merger**: in a merger two or more companies come together and share profits in a newly created entity, whereas here no new entity is created, no shares change hands and each company keeps its own business and profits.

**(ii) Advantages:**

1. **Organisational**: each partner extends its supply chain through the shared fleet and gains buying capacity at the auctions; each can learn skills from the other's buyers.
2. **Economic**: costs and risks are reduced by distributing them across the two members; larger joint volumes at the auction and full trucks give greater economies of scale, so the cost per kilogram falls.
3. **Strategic**: rivals join together to cooperate instead of competing in buying and transport, while still competing for customers; pooling resources and skills creates a competitive advantage against larger players such as BrewBucks.
4. **Political**: not a driver here. This category matters when an alliance with a local business helps a firm enter a foreign market despite local prejudices or legal barriers, or when a partner is politically influential.

**(iii) Risk: sharing.** The major disadvantage of an alliance is the sharing of resources, profits, knowledge and skills. Tapri's auction bids, volumes and quality grades will be visible to its closest rival; such information is protected by the agreement only as far as the partner is willing to abide by it or the courts to enforce it, and an ally may become an opponent when it decides to separate out. Here the ally is already an opponent in the shops.

## Under the hood

### One move, two names

The Study Material calls diversification an internal strategy (SM 4.9), yet horizontal integration happens "through acquisition" (SM 4.11). ICAI's answers classify by the diversification type even when the facts say "acquired": Zeon Beverages acquiring a cold-storage logistics company and setting up its own fruit-processing unit was answered as "vertically integrated concentric diversification" (RTP September 2025 Q13). If the question is about mergers, name the merger type too.

### Two different "diversifications"

Ansoff's **diversification** is new products in new markets, "related / unrelated" (SM 4.9, 4.25); this lesson classifies by the link with the present business. So an Ansoff diversification may be concentric or conglomerate. The chapter's own MCQ answers (SM 4.38) add: conglomerate is another name for unrelated diversification; diversification primarily helps to reduce risk; unreliable or costly suppliers point to backward integration.

## Answer frame

### "Identify the growth strategy" (5 marks)

1. **Name it**: "The strategy adopted is backward integration, a form of vertically integrated (concentric) diversification."
2. **Define it** in the Study Material's words.
3. **Apply** the facts: which link exists, which stage of the chain is entered.
4. **Conclude**, then what else is asked (advantages, risks, the difference from another type).

### "Distinguish between concentric and conglomerate diversification"

ICAI's suggested answers (RTP September 2024 Q14 and four MTPs) give three points:

::: compare
| Point | Concentric diversification | Conglomerate diversification |
|---|---|---|
| (i) What is added | Related products or markets | Areas unrelated to the current line of business |
| (ii) Linkage | Linked to the existing businesses through process, technology or marketing | No such linkages; disjointed from the existing businesses |
| (iii) Usual reason | Opportunities are available in the existing line of business | Opportunities in the current line are limited, or outside are highly lucrative |
:::

Point (iii) is from ICAI's suggested answers; the Study Material gives (i) and (ii) (SM 4.10–4.12). For "Explain the expansion strategy and its characteristics" (1 + 4), write the definition, then the characteristics in the Study Material's order (May 2025 Q8(b)).

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: the supermarket that "integrated horizontally" with a farm
Buying farms is called horizontal "because the supermarket gets bigger". **Fix:** horizontal is the *same* stage (another supermarket). Farms supply the supermarket, so it is **backward** integration (SM 4.11).
:::

::: mistake Forward and backward swapped
"Backward" read as "going back to an old business". **Fix:** direction is about the chain: towards inputs is backward, towards distribution and the customer is forward.
:::

::: mistake Calling every partnership a merger
**Fix:** if both firms remain separate entities sharing resources, benefits and control, it is a **strategic alliance**; a merger creates one entity. (Where ICAI's facts said two firms "join forces" to design products, without saying they stay separate, the answer was a co-generic merger: MTP September 2024 Series II Q1(a).)
:::

::: mistake "Unrelated because it is a new product"
**Fix:** a new product linked by process, technology or marketing is concentric (clothes to shoes; a dairy launching vegan yogurt and organic milk, January 2026 MCQ 11). Conglomerate needs *no* link.
:::

Q: Case scenario. Tapri's premix reaches shops through 300 independent distributors. Kesar suspects that several of them push Kadak Chai harder because it pays a higher margin, so Tapri buys Sharma Distributors, its largest distributor in Madhya Pradesh, and keeps it as a Tapri-only channel. Which strategy is this?
- [x] Forward integration

- [ ] Concentric diversification, because the games are about tea

- [ ] Backward integration
- [x] Conglomerate diversification
> Concentric diversification needs a link through process, technology or marketing. Designing and printing games, toy-shop channels and game buyers have nothing in common with making and selling premix: no linkage of product, market or technology. A shared theme is not a link.

- [x] It is a strategic alliance
- [x] The partners maintain their status as independent and separate entities
- [ ] It is a horizontal merger, because both gain economies of scale
- [x] Sharing of knowledge and skills is its major disadvantage

- [ ] Defend against takeover bids
- [ ] Provide executive interest
- [x] Sharing sales and distribution capacity
- [ ] Employment of new technologies

Q: A maker of ceiling fans merges with a maker of LED lights. Neither competes with the other, neither supplies the other, and both sell through the same electrical retailers using similar motor-and-electronics know-how, so the fan maker extends its product line. The merger is:

- [x] Co-generic
- [ ] Vertical
- [ ] Conglomerate
> Related through business markets and basic technologies, extending the product line around a common set of resources, but neither a direct competitor (horizontal) nor a supplier or buyer (vertical): co-generic (SM 4.16). Conglomerate would need no linkage at all.

## Recap

- Corporate strategies: stability, expansion, retrenchment, combination (SM 4.4).
- Expansion: redefining the business by enlarging scope and substantially increasing investment; opposite of stability in risk and reward; routes: intensification and diversification.
- Internal growth: intensification (penetration, market development, product development), diversification, innovation; external: mergers and acquisitions, strategic alliance.
- Concentric = linked by process, technology or marketing: vertical (backward to inputs, forward to distribution) or horizontal (same stage).
- Conglomerate = no linkage of product, market or technology.
- Mergers: horizontal, vertical, co-generic, conglomerate.
- Strategic alliance: partners stay independent; advantages organisational, economic, strategic, political; the major disadvantage is sharing. (The cricket bats stay a dream.)

## Flashcards

Q:: Growth/Expansion strategy: ICAI's definition?
A:: It is implemented by redefining the business by enlarging the scope of business and substantially increasing investment in the business; it can be equated with dynamism, vigour, promise and success.

Q:: Expansion strategy: its two major routes?
A:: Intensification and diversification.

Q:: Intensification: its three options?
A:: Market penetration, market development and product development.

Q:: Diversification: ICAI's definition?
A:: An entry into new products or product lines, new services or new markets, involving substantially different skills, technology and knowledge.

Q:: Concentric diversification: how is the new business linked?
A:: Through process, technology or marketing; a spin-off from existing facilities and products, with synergy. Two directions: vertical and horizontal integration.

Q:: Backward integration?
A:: Creation of effective supply by entering the business of input providers, to increase own supply capability or lessen cost of production (a supermarket buying farms).

Q:: Forward integration?
A:: Moving forward in the value chain into business lines that use existing products, including distribution channels (a coffee bean maker merging with a café).

Q:: Horizontal integrated diversification?
A:: Acquiring similar businesses at the same stage of the production-marketing chain, or firms with complementary products or by-products, or competitors' products.

Q:: Conglomerate diversification?
A:: No linkages related to product, market or technology; disjointed from the existing businesses in every way, with no common thread (a cement maker making steel and rubber products).

Q:: The four types of merger?
A:: Horizontal, vertical, co-generic and conglomerate.

Q:: Strategic alliance: definition?
A:: A relationship between two or more businesses that enables each to achieve strategic objectives neither could achieve alone; partners stay independent, share benefits and control, and contribute until it is terminated.

Q:: Strategic alliance: the four categories of advantage, and the major disadvantage?
A:: Organisational, economic, strategic and political; the major disadvantage is sharing (of resources, profits, knowledge and skills).
