## In plain words

### Two bosses, one company

Tapri has two very different leaders. Harpreet Gill runs the chai premix division, a mature, profitable business: every plant has a monthly tonnage target, a bonus for each tonne above it, a deduction for wastage, and a review meeting on the first Monday. Kesar has just taken charge of TapriNow, the loss-making delivery app in a market that changes every quarter: he holds town halls, talks about "hot chai in ten minutes for every Indian", and lets young engineers try wild ideas.

> **Harpreet:** Targets, rewards, penalties. My plants run like a Swiss watch.
> **Kesar:** And my team runs like a festival. Harpreet, when did your plant last invent something?
> **Dev:** I have discounted both your claims. You are both still right, which is irritating.

Both are exercising **strategic leadership**: setting direction, turning strategy into action and getting people to deliver it. Kesar's style, inspiring people with charisma, enthusiasm and a vision of a higher calling, is **transformational leadership**. Harpreet's style, using the authority of the office to exchange rewards and penalties for clear goals, is **transactional leadership**. Neither is "better": transformational suits turbulent environments and organisations that must embrace major change; transactional suits static environments, mature industries and organisations that are performing well.

A strategic leader also plays many roles at once (visionary, culture builder, crisis manager, head cheerleader) and, above all, pushes good strategy execution: watching what is happening, energising people, keeping the organisation responsive, insisting on ethics, and correcting course.

::: analogy Two cricket coaches
One coach makes a losing team believe it can win the cup, and players train harder than anyone asked them to. Another runs a winning team on fitness tests, match fees and a clear rule: miss fielding practice and you sit out. The first is what a struggling team needs; the second keeps a champion team sharp. A good board knows which coach the team needs this season.
:::

::: key
Strategic leadership sets direction by developing and communicating a vision, formulates strategies, brings about the changes needed to implement them and inspires people to execute. Transformational leadership inspires change; transactional leadership exchanges rewards for results and improves the current situation.
:::

## How it works

### What strategic leadership is

::: definition Strategic leadership
Strategic leadership sets the firm's direction by developing and communicating vision of future, formulate strategies in the light of internal and external environment, brings about changes required to implement strategies and inspire the staff to contribute to strategy execution. (SM 5.51)
:::

A manager as a strategic leader has **many leadership roles** to play: visionary, chief entrepreneur and strategist, chief administrator, culture builder, resource acquirer and allocator, capabilities builder, process integrator, crisis manager, spokesperson, negotiator, motivator, arbitrator, policy maker, policy enforcer, and head cheerleader. Sometimes it is useful to be authoritarian; sometimes a perceptive listener and compromising decision maker; sometimes strongly participative; sometimes a coach and adviser (SM 5.51).

A strategic leader is a **change agent** who initiates strategic changes and ensures they are successfully implemented. For the most part, major change efforts have to be **top-down and vision-driven**; leading change starts with diagnosing the situation and then deciding which of several ways to handle it (SM 5.51). (The change process itself is in [[sm.impl.digital|strategic change]].)

### The five leadership roles in pushing strategy execution

Managers have **five leadership roles** to play in pushing for good strategy execution (SM 5.51–5.52):

::: steps
1. **Staying on top of what is happening**: closely monitoring progress, solving out issues, and learning what obstacles lie in the path of good execution.
2. **Promoting a culture of esprit de corps** that mobilises and energises organisational members to execute strategy in a competent fashion and perform at a high level.
3. **Keeping the organisation responsive** to changing conditions, alert for new opportunities, bubbling with innovative ideas, and ahead of rivals in developing competitively valuable competencies and capabilities.
4. **Exercising ethical leadership** and insisting that the company conduct its affairs like a model corporate citizen.
5. **Pushing corrective actions** to improve strategy execution and overall strategic performance.
:::

The Study Material's examples (SM 5.52): **N. R. Narayan Murthy** is celebrated for the values he institutionalised as CEO of Infosys, leaving a strong management development programme that builds strategic leaders with ethical values; **Dhirubhai Ambani** of Reliance could conceptualise and create sweeping strategies, give clear direction, and inspire employees with strong interpersonal skills.

### The managerial frame of reference

Strategic leaders must adapt their frames of reference to deal with rapid, complex changes (SM 5.54).

::: definition Managerial frame of reference
A managerial frame of reference is the set of assumptions, premises, and accepted wisdom that bounds a manager's understanding of the company, the industry in which it competes, and the core competencies that it exploits in the pursuit of strategic competitiveness (and above-average returns). It is the foundation on which a manager's mindset is built. (SM 5.54)
:::

Its importance: ==competitive battles are not between companies or products but between mindsets or managerial frames==, so effective strategic leaders must be able to deal with the diverse and cognitively complex competitive situations of today's landscape (SM 5.54).

### Responsibilities of a strategic leader

A strategic leader has several responsibilities (SM 5.54–5.55):

1. Making strategic decisions.
2. Formulating policies and action plans to implement strategic decisions.
3. Ensuring effective communication in the organisation.
4. Managing human capital (perhaps the most critical of the strategic leader's skills).
5. Managing change in the organisation.
6. Creating and sustaining a strong corporate culture.
7. Sustaining high performance over time.

The strategic leadership skills of a company's managers are thus **resources** that affect performance and must be developed for the company's future benefit. Unlike strategic leadership, **managerial leadership** is generally concerned with the **short-term, day-to-day activities** (SM 5.55).

### Transformational and transactional leadership

The two basic approaches to leadership (SM 5.55–5.56):

- **Transformational leadership style** uses **charisma and enthusiasm** to inspire people to exert themselves for the good of the organisation. It may be appropriate **in turbulent environments, in industries at the very start or end of their life-cycles, in poorly performing organisations**, when there is a need to inspire a company to embrace major changes. Transformational leaders offer excitement, vision, intellectual stimulation and personal satisfaction; they inspire involvement in a mission, giving followers a "dream" or "vision" of a higher calling, to elicit more dramatic changes in performance. They motivate followers to do more than originally expected by stretching their abilities and self-confidence, and promote innovation throughout the organisation.
- **Transactional leadership style** focuses on **designing systems and controlling** the organisation's activities and is more likely to be associated with **improving the current situation**. Transactional leaders try to build on the existing culture and enhance current practices. The style **uses the authority of its office to exchange rewards, such as pay and status**, and prefers a more formalised approach to motivation, setting **clear goals with explicit rewards or penalties** for achievement or non-achievement. It may be appropriate **in static environments, in mature industries, and in organisations that are performing well**, and is better suited to persuading people to work efficiently and run operations smoothly.

::: compare
| Basis | Transformational leadership | Transactional leadership |
|---|---|---|
| Means of influence | Charisma and enthusiasm inspire people to exert themselves for the organisation | Authority of the office used to exchange rewards such as pay and status |
| When appropriate | Turbulent environments; industries at the very start or end of their life-cycles; poorly performing organisations; when major change must be embraced | Static environments; mature industries; organisations performing well |
| Motivation | Excitement, vision, intellectual stimulation, personal satisfaction; a "dream" of a higher calling | Formalised: clear goals with explicit rewards or penalties |
| Towards change | Elicits dramatic changes in performance; promotes innovation | Builds on the existing culture; enhances current practices; runs operations smoothly |
:::

## Worked examples

:::: example Three businesses, three new heads
Tapri Tea Co. Ltd has bought two companies and must now choose heads, and the right leadership style, for three businesses:

- **Nilgiri Leaf Co.**, a 90-year-old tea-estate business, profitable every year, in a stable market where little changes.
- **ChaiBot**, a start-up making smart chai-vending machines, in an industry so new that no company in it has yet made a profit; its technology changes every few months.
- **Tapri Instant**, Tapri's own instant-tea sachets: sales have fallen for four years as customers move to premixes, the industry is at the end of its life-cycle, and the business must reinvent itself or close.

Kesar's view: "Nilgiri Leaf and Tapri Instant are both old businesses, so both need the same steady, rules-based head."

Required:

(i) Recommend a leadership style for the head of each business, with reasons, and comment on Kesar's view.

(ii) Distinguish between the two styles.

::: answer Model answer
**(i) Recommended styles.** Two basic approaches to leadership are the transformational and the transactional leadership styles.

- **Nilgiri Leaf: transactional leadership.** Transactional leadership may be appropriate in a static environment, in mature industries and in organisations that are performing well. It focuses on designing systems and controlling the organisation's activities, builds on the existing culture, enhances current practices, and is better suited to persuading people to work efficiently and run operations smoothly.
- **ChaiBot: transformational leadership.** Transformational leadership may be appropriate in turbulent environments and in industries at the very start of their life-cycles. It uses charisma and enthusiasm to inspire people to exert themselves for the good of the organisation, and promotes innovation throughout the organisation.
- **Tapri Instant: transformational leadership.** The business is in an industry at the end of its life-cycle, is performing poorly and must embrace major change: transformational leadership may be appropriate in industries at the very start or end of their life-cycles, in poorly performing organisations, and when there is a need to inspire a company to embrace major changes.

**Kesar's view** is not correct. The age of a business is not the test. Nilgiri Leaf is mature, stable and performing well, which suits transactional leadership; Tapri Instant is at the end of its life-cycle and performing poorly, which calls for transformational leadership.

**(ii) Distinction:**

| Basis | Transformational leadership | Transactional leadership |
|---|---|---|
| Means of influence | Uses charisma and enthusiasm to inspire people | Uses the authority of its office to exchange rewards, such as pay and status |
| When appropriate | Turbulent environments; industries at the very start or end of their life-cycles; poorly performing organisations; when major change must be embraced | Static environments; mature industries; organisations that are performing well |
| Motivation | Excitement, vision, intellectual stimulation and personal satisfaction; a "dream" or "vision" of a higher calling | Formalised: clear goals with explicit rewards or penalties for achievement or non-achievement |
| Approach to change | Elicits dramatic changes in organisational performance; promotes innovation | Builds on the existing culture and enhances current practices |
:::

:::: example Six months at Tapri Foods
Meera Kulkarni was appointed head of Tapri Foods, the strategic business unit that runs the samosa factory and the frozen-snacks range, with a strategy to supply airline caterers. Six months later:

- she reads the monthly report but has not visited the factory, and learnt only last week that the first airline's food-safety audit had been stuck for two months;
- the factory and sales teams blame each other for every missed delivery;
- a rival launched a baked samosa that the airlines prefer, and nobody in the unit noticed for three months;
- when a supervisor proposed relabelling stock near its expiry date to meet an order, Meera said nothing;
- the airline audit has now failed twice, for the same reasons both times.

Required: Which leadership roles in pushing for good strategy execution is Meera failing to play? Advise her.

::: answer Model answer
A manager as a strategic leader has to play many leadership roles, such as visionary, chief entrepreneur and strategist, culture builder, crisis manager, motivator and policy enforcer. Leading change has to start with diagnosing the situation and then deciding which of several ways to handle it. Managers have five leadership roles to play in pushing for good strategy execution, and Meera is failing in all five:

1. **Staying on top of what is happening**: closely monitoring progress, solving out issues, and learning what obstacles lie in the path of good execution. Reading a monthly report without visiting the factory let the audit stay stuck for two months. Meera should monitor progress closely and in person.
2. **Promoting a culture of esprit de corps** that mobilises and energises organisational members to execute strategy competently and perform at a high level. Teams that blame each other show its absence; Meera should build one team around the airline goal.
3. **Keeping the organisation responsive** to changing conditions, alert for new opportunities, bubbling with innovative ideas, and ahead of rivals in developing competitively valuable competencies and capabilities. The rival's baked samosa went unnoticed for three months.
4. **Exercising ethical leadership** and insisting that the company conduct its affairs like a model corporate citizen. Relabelling stock near expiry must be refused clearly; silence is not ethical leadership.
5. **Pushing corrective actions** to improve strategy execution and overall strategic performance. An audit failed twice for the same reasons shows that no corrective action was taken after the first failure.

Meera should play each of these roles actively if the airline strategy is to be executed well.
:::

:::: example The CEO who saw only lorries
Harish Bhatt has spent 25 years in the trucking business and is now chief executive of Tapri Cold Chain. He believes: "Our competitors are other cold-chain truck fleets; technology is a back-office expense." Meanwhile, a quick-commerce company has begun offering its own refrigerated delivery to food brands at lower cost, and two of Tapri Cold Chain's largest customers have moved to it. Harish takes every decision himself and announces it by notice; three senior engineers have left in six months and nobody has been hired; he has shelved a proposal for route-planning software as "a fashion"; and the unit's profit has fallen for three quarters in a row.

Required:

(i) Which concept in strategic leadership explains why Harish missed the new competitor? Explain it and its significance.

(ii) Which responsibilities of a strategic leader is Harish neglecting?

::: answer Model answer
**(i) Managerial frame of reference.** A managerial frame of reference is the set of assumptions, premises and accepted wisdom that bounds a manager's understanding of the company, the industry in which it competes, and the core competencies that it exploits in the pursuit of strategic competitiveness and above-average returns. It is the foundation on which a manager's mindset is built. Harish's frame ("our competitors are truck fleets; technology is a back-office expense") bounds what he can see, so a competitor with a different business model was invisible to him.

**Significance.** In today's competitive landscape strategic leaders are challenged to adapt their frames of reference so that they can deal with rapid, complex changes. Competitive battles are not between companies or products but between mindsets or managerial frames, so effective strategic leaders must be able to deal with the diverse and cognitively complex competitive situations that are characteristic of today's competitive landscape.

**(ii) Responsibilities neglected.** A strategic leader has several responsibilities: making strategic decisions; formulating policies and action plans to implement strategic decisions; ensuring effective communication in the organisation; managing human capital; managing change in the organisation; creating and sustaining a strong corporate culture; and sustaining high performance over time. Harish is neglecting:

1. **Ensuring effective communication in the organisation**: decisions announced only by notice.
2. **Managing human capital**, perhaps the most critical of the strategic leader's skills: three senior engineers lost and not replaced.
3. **Managing change in the organisation**: the route-planning software shelved as "a fashion".
4. **Sustaining high performance over time**: profit down for three quarters in a row.

The strategic leadership skills of a company's managers are resources that affect company performance and must be developed for the company's future benefit: Harish must first widen his frame of reference.
:::

## Under the hood

### "Mature" or "growing or mature"?

The chapter's main text says the transactional style may be appropriate "in static environment, in mature industries, and in organizations that are performing well" (SM 5.56); the chapter's own answer to the "differentiate" question says "in growing or mature industries" (SM 5.78). Writing "static environments, mature industries and organisations that are performing well", as the main text does, is safe.

### Strategic leadership is not managerial leadership

Strategic leadership sets direction through a vision and inspires people to move towards it; managerial leadership is generally concerned with the short-term, day-to-day activities (SM 5.55). A case in which a leader only supervises daily output is describing managerial, not strategic, leadership.

### Where leadership meets the rest of the chapter

The change agent is the leader of [[sm.impl.digital|Lewin's unfreezing, changing and refreezing]]; the culture builder is the chief executive whose "sincere, sustained commitment" a culture change needs ([[sm.impl.structure|organisation structure and culture]]); and "pushing corrective actions" is the bridge to [[sm.impl.control|strategic control]].

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: Bunty's "transformational" plant director
A plant director who sets fixed targets and docks salaries for missed standards is called "transformational, because he transformed productivity". **Fix:** the style is judged by how the leader influences people. Authority of office, explicit rewards and penalties, and monitoring deviations make it **transactional**.
:::

::: mistake "Transformational is always better"
**Fix:** each style has its setting. Transactional leadership may be appropriate in static environments, mature industries and organisations that are performing well; transformational in turbulent environments, at the start or end of the life-cycle, and in poorly performing organisations needing major change.
:::

::: mistake Writing the seven responsibilities when the question asks for the five roles
**Fix:** "leadership roles in pushing for good strategy execution" = the five roles (staying on top … pushing corrective actions). "Responsibilities of a strategic leader" = the seven (making strategic decisions … sustaining high performance).
:::

## Recap

- Strategic leadership: sets direction by developing and communicating a vision, formulates strategies, brings about the changes required to implement them, inspires staff to execute.
- Many roles: visionary, chief entrepreneur and strategist, chief administrator, culture builder … crisis manager, spokesperson, negotiator, motivator … head cheerleader; authoritarian, listener, participative or coach as the situation needs.
- The strategic leader is a change agent; major change is top-down and vision-driven, starting with diagnosis.
- Five roles in execution: stay on top; esprit de corps; keep responsive; ethical leadership; corrective actions.
- Seven responsibilities: strategic decisions; policies and action plans; communication; human capital (most critical); change; strong culture; high performance over time.
- Frame of reference: the assumptions, premises and accepted wisdom bounding a manager's understanding; battles are between mindsets.
- Transformational: charisma, vision, innovation; turbulent, start or end of life-cycle, poor performers. Transactional: authority, rewards and penalties, systems and control; static, mature, performing well. (Pick the coach for the season.)
