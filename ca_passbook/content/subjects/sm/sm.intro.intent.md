## In plain words

### Kesar's napkin

After a long board meeting Kesar writes the "Tapri Dream" on a paper napkin and hands it to Ananya: *Be India's favourite chai in every town by 2035. Fresh chai and snacks for commuters, students and offices. Double café revenue in five years. Never cheat a customer. Kulhads, not plastic.*

> **Kesar:** That is our strategy. Print it on every cup.
> **Ananya:** Five lines, Kesar-ji, and four different jobs. The bank asks where we are going. The café manager asks what we sell and to whom. Dev asks how much, by when. The new supplier wants to know what we will never do.
> **Dev:** I only need the "how much, by when" line. I will discount the rest.

Ananya sorts the napkin into four boxes, the components of **strategic intent**: the purpose a firm strives for, the "why" behind everything it does.

- **Vision**: where do we want to go? ("India's favourite chai in every town by 2035.")
- **Mission**: what business are we in, for whom, and why do we exist? ("Fresh chai and snacks for commuters, students and offices.")
- **Goals and objectives**: what results, measured how, by when? ("Double café revenue in five years.")
- **Values**: what will we never compromise, whatever the gain? ("Never cheat a customer. Kulhads, not plastic.")

The order matters. The vision points the way, the mission says what the firm does to get there, objectives turn both into numbers someone can be held to, and values sit underneath all three, deciding how every one of them is pursued.

::: analogy The family road trip
A family plans a drive to Goa. "Goa for Diwali" is the vision: where we want to land. "A road holiday for the five of us, with stops the children will enjoy" is the mission: what kind of trip this is, and for whom. "Pune by 6 pm on day one, fuel within ₹15,000" are the objectives: measurable and dated. "Nobody drives tired, seat belts always" are the values: no deadline justifies breaking them. And the reason anyone gets in the car at all, a family that wants time together, is the intent.
:::

::: key
Strategic intent is the philosophical base of strategic management: what the organisation strives for and why. It is expressed in vision (where we want to go), mission (what business we are in and why we exist), goals and objectives (measurable results within a time frame), and values (principles never compromised), which guide every decision.
:::

## How it works

### Strategic intent: the "why"

Strategic management was defined in [[sm.intro.meaning|the previous lesson]] as a dynamic process of formulation, implementation, evaluation and control of strategies **to realise the organisation's strategic intent** (SM 1.13).

::: definition Strategic intent
Strategic intent refers to purposes of what the organisation strives for. Senior managers must define "what they want to do" and "why they want to do"; "why they want to do" represents the strategic intent of the firm. Clarity in strategic intent is extremely important for the future success and growth of the enterprise, irrespective of its nature and size. (SM 1.13)
:::

Strategic intent (SM 1.13–1.14):

- is the **philosophical base** of strategic management: the purposes the organisation endeavours to achieve;
- gives a perspective of the means that will lead the organisation to reach its vision in the long run, and an idea of what it desires to attain in future;
- answers the question **what the organisation strives or stands for**;
- indicates the **long-term market position** it desires to create or occupy, and the opportunity for exploring new possibilities;
- provides the **framework** within which the firm adopts a predetermined direction and operates to achieve strategic objectives.

**Where it appears.** At the corporate level, strategic intent takes the form of **vision and mission statements**; at the business level, the **business definition and business model**. It is generally stated in broad terms; stated in precise terms, it becomes the aims to be achieved operationally, the **goals and objectives** (SM 1.13–1.14).

### Vision: where we want to go

Very early in strategy making, senior managers must decide what directional path the company should take and what changes in its **product-market-customer-technology focus** would improve its market position and future prospects (SM 1.15).

::: definition Vision
Vision implies the blueprint of the company's future position. It describes where the organisation wants to land, depicts its aspirations and provides a glimpse of what it would like to become in future. Every sub-system of the organisation is required to follow its vision. (SM 1.14)
:::

**Strategic vision** is top management's view about the company's direction and its product-customer-market-technology focus. It delineates management's aspirations for the business, providing a **panoramic view of "where we are to go"** and a convincing rationale for why this makes good business sense. It points out a particular direction, charts a strategic path to be followed and moulds organisational identity. A clearly articulated strategic vision communicates management's aspirations to stakeholders and helps steer the energies of company personnel in a common direction (SM 1.15–1.16). Henry Ford's vision of a car in every garage had power because it captured the imagination of others, aided internal efforts to mobilise the company's resources, and served as a reference point for gauging the merits of its strategic actions (SM 1.16). The Study Material's Indian example: HDFC Bank's vision of being a world-class Indian bank, which keeps "where we want to go" at the centre of its strategic decisions (SM 1.16).

**Essentials of a strategic vision** (SM 1.16), four points, in order:

1. The entrepreneurial challenge in developing a strategic vision is to **think creatively** about how to prepare a company for the future.
2. Forming a strategic vision is an exercise in **intelligent entrepreneurship**.
3. A well-articulated strategic vision **creates enthusiasm** among the members of the organisation.
4. The best-worded vision statement **clearly illuminates the direction** in which the organisation is headed.

### Mission: what business we are in

::: definition Mission
Mission delineates the firm's business, its goals and ways to reach the goals. It explains the reason for the existence of the firm in the society and is designed to help potential shareholders and investors understand the purpose of the firm. A mission statement helps to identify "what business the firm undertakes"; it defines the present capabilities, activities, customer focus and role in society. (SM 1.14)
:::

A mission answers the basic question **"what business are we in and what we do"**. Firms that fail to conceptualise and articulate their mission and business definition with clarity fumble in identifying opportunities and fail to formulate strategies to use them; the two ideas are absolutely central to strategic planning (SM 1.16).

**Why should an organisation have a mission?** Seven reasons, in the Study Material's order (SM 1.17):

1. To ensure **unanimity of purpose** within the organisation.
2. To develop a **basis, or standard, for allocating** organisational resources.
3. To provide a **basis for motivating the use** of the organisation's resources.
4. To establish a **general tone or organisational climate**, to suggest a business-like operation.
5. To serve as a **focal point** for those who can identify with the organisation's purpose and direction.
6. To facilitate the **translation of objectives and goals into a work structure** involving the assignment of tasks to responsible elements within the organisation.
7. To **specify organisational purposes** and translate them into goals so that cost, time and performance parameters can be assessed and controlled.

A company's mission statement is typically focused on its ==present business scope, "who we are and what we do"==: it broadly describes the organisation's present capability, customer focus, activities and business makeup (SM 1.17). The Study Material's example: HDFC Bank's two-fold mission, to be the preferred provider of banking services for target retail and wholesale customer segments, and to achieve healthy growth in profitability consistent with the bank's risk appetite (SM 1.17).

**Writing a good mission.** It should reflect the organisation's philosophy as perceived by senior managers, and be **precise, clear, feasible, distinctive and motivating** (SM 1.17). Useful points (SM 1.18):

- It gives the organisation its own **special identity, business emphasis and path for development**, one that sets it apart from similarly positioned companies.
- A company's business is defined by **what needs** it is trying to satisfy, **which customer groups** it targets, the **technologies and competencies** it uses and the **activities** it performs.
- Good mission statements are **unique** to the organisation for which they are developed.

**"What business are we in?"** Peter Drucker and Theodore Levitt were among the first to insist that, as the first step in business planning, every firm must clarify its corporate mission and define accurately the business it is engaged in, by answering basic questions (SM 1.18). The Study Material lists eleven; here they are paired into six steps, in its order:

::: steps
1. What is our mission? What is our ultimate purpose?
2. What do we want to become? What kind of growth do we seek?
3. What business are we in? Do we understand our business correctly and define it accurately in its broadest connotation?
4. Whom do we intend to serve? What human need do we intend to serve through our offer?
5. What brings us to this particular business?
6. What would be the nature of this business in the future? In what business would we like to be in the future?
:::

The corporate mission is also an expression of the **growth ambition** of the firm: its future visualised, the corporation's dream crystallised, a grand design of its future. It amplifies what brings the firm to this business and what purpose it seeks to achieve, so it serves as a **justification for the firm's very presence** and legitimises its existence (SM 1.19).

According to Drucker, the answer to "What business are we in?" should have a **marketing or external perspective**, not be restated as the production or generic activities of the business (SM 1.19). The Study Material's table, with a Tapri row added:

| Company | Production-oriented answer | Marketing-oriented answer |
|---|---|---|
| Indian Oil | We produce oil and gasoline products | We provide various types of safe and cost-effective energy |
| Indian Railways | We run a railroad | We offer a transportation and material-handling system |
| Lakme | In the factory, we make cosmetics | In the retail outlet, we sell hope |
| Tapri (ours) | We brew tea and fry samosas | We sell India a ten-minute break |

### Goals and objectives: what results, by when

Business organisations translate their vision and mission into goals and objectives. Some authors distinguish them: **goals** are open-ended attributes that denote future states or outcomes; **objectives** are close-ended attributes, precise and expressed in specific terms, which translate the goals into long-term and short-term perspectives. Several theorists do not make the distinction, and the Study Material uses the terms interchangeably (SM 1.19–1.20).

::: definition Objectives
Objectives are the organisation's performance targets, the results and outcomes it wants to achieve. They function as yardsticks for tracking an organisation's performance and progress. (SM 1.20)
:::

All organisations have objectives, and their pursuit is unending. They give meaning and a sense of direction; structure and activities are designed, and resources allocated, around them; they act as benchmarks for guiding activity and evaluating performance (SM 1.20). The vision, mission, business definition and business model explain the philosophy of the organisation, but goals and objectives represent the results to be achieved in multiple areas of business (SM 1.14).

**Characteristics of objectives.** Objectives with strategic focus relate to outcomes that strengthen the organisation's overall business position and competitive vitality. To be meaningful and serve their intended role, objectives must possess ten characteristics (SM 1.20), in this order:

1. They should define the organisation's **relationship with its environment**.
2. They should be **facilitative towards achievement of mission** and purpose.
3. They should provide the **basis for strategic decision-making**.
4. They should provide **standards for performance appraisal**.
5. They should be **concrete and specific**.
6. They should be **related to a time frame**.
7. They should be **measurable and controllable**.
8. They should be **challenging**.
9. Different objectives should **correlate with each other**.
10. They should be set **within the constraints of organisational resources and external environment**.

**Short-term and long-term.** A company's objectives ought to include both. Quarterly or annual targets focus attention on immediate improvements; targets three to five years away prompt the question of what to do now. A company aiming to double its sales within five years cannot wait until the third or fourth year to begin growing (SM 1.21).

- **Seven areas of long-term objectives** (SM 1.21): profitability, productivity, competitive position, employee development, employee relations, technological leadership, public responsibility.
- Long-term objectives are the results expected from pursuing strategies; strategies are the actions taken to accomplish them. The time frame of both should be consistent, **usually two to five years** (SM 1.21).
- Short-range objectives can be identical to long-range ones when the organisation is already performing at the target level (a firm that wants 15% profit growth every year and is achieving it). They differ most when managers are trying to raise performance and cannot reach the long-range target in one year: then **short-range objectives serve as steps** towards the long-term objectives (SM 1.21).

**Benefits of clearly established objectives** (SM 1.22): they provide direction, allow synergy, aid in evaluation, establish priorities, reduce uncertainty, minimise conflicts, stimulate exertion, and aid in both the allocation of resources and the design of jobs.

### Values: what we never give up

::: definition Values / value system
Values are the deep-rooted principles which guide an organisation's decisions and actions. Collins and Porras define core values as inherent and sacrosanct: they can never be compromised, either for convenience or short-term economic gain. Values often reflect the values of the company's founders (Hewlett-Packard's "HP Way"); they are the source of a company's distinctiveness and must be maintained at all costs. (SM 1.14–1.15)
:::

While strategic intent is the purpose an organisation aims to achieve, values form the **omnipresent foundation of every decision** management takes: an organisation without values is like an organisation with no real intent (SM 1.14). Common examples are **integrity, trust, accountability, humility, innovation and diversity** (SM 1.22).

Why values matter (SM 1.22):

- They set the tone for how people think and behave, **especially in situations of dilemma**.
- They create a **sense of shared purpose**, a strong foundation focused on the longevity of the company's success.
- **Internally**, employees prefer employers whose values resonate with them; **externally**, most consumers prefer to buy from companies whose purpose reflects their own values and beliefs. Values have both internal and external implications.
- During the Covid-19 pandemic, many leaders put people before everything else, showing how deep their organisations' foundations were.

Values remain the **centre, or core,** of vision, mission and goals and of putting them all into action: vision is followed by mission, then goals, and finally execution through real actions (SM 1.23). The Study Material's example: HDFC Bank's five core values, operational excellence, customer focus, product leadership, people and sustainability (SM 1.23).

**Intent or values: which is wider?** The Study Material answers its own question: they are two different concepts. **Intent** is the purpose of doing business; **values** are the principles that guide the decision making of the business. They go hand in hand and intent is sometimes driven by values, so values are, more or less, **wider than intent** (SM 1.23).

## Worked examples

:::: example Sorting Kesar's napkin
Ananya types up a longer version of Kesar's napkin for the board of Tapri Tea Co. Ltd:

(a) "To be the chai every Indian reaches for, in every town, by 2035."

(b) "We serve fresh, affordable chai and snacks to commuters, students and offices through kulhad cafés, packaged premix and our delivery app."

(c) "To raise café revenue from ₹260 crore in 2025-26 to ₹520 crore in 2030-31."

(d) "We never adulterate, never short-pour and never hide a price."

(e) "To be the best."

(f) "To cut the average delivery time of app orders from 32 minutes to 25 minutes during 2026-27."

**Required:**

(i) Identify the component of strategic intent that each statement represents.

(ii) Evaluate statement (e) against the characteristics an objective must possess.

(iii) Calculate the constant annual growth rate that statement (c) requires, and state the café revenue targets for 2026-27 and 2027-28 as short-range objectives (round to one decimal place of a crore).

::: answer Model answer
**(i) Components**

| Statement | Component | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| (a) | Vision | Where Tapri wants to land; its future position and aspiration |
| (b) | Mission | Present business scope: who we are, what we do, for which customers |
| (c) | Long-term objective | Concrete, measurable, time-bound result over five years |
| (d) | Values | Principles never to be compromised for short-term gain |
| (e) | None, as worded | Too vague to be an objective or a vision |
| (f) | Short-term objective | Specific, measurable, within one year |

**(ii) Statement (e).** Objectives, to be meaningful, should define the organisation's relationship with its environment, be facilitative towards the mission, provide a basis for strategic decision-making and standards for performance appraisal, be concrete and specific, related to a time frame, measurable and controllable, challenging, correlated with other objectives and set within the constraints of resources and environment. "To be the best" is not concrete or specific (best at what, for whom?), has no time frame, cannot be measured or controlled, and gives no standard for appraising performance. It also fails as a vision, since the best-worded vision statement clearly illuminates the direction in which the organisation is headed. It should be rewritten as a vision ("India's most loved chai brand by 2035") or as measurable objectives.

**(iii) Short-range steps.** Doubling in five years needs a constant growth rate of

$$g = 2^{1/5} - 1 = 0.1487 \qquad \text{that is, } 14.87\% \text{ a year}$$

| Year | Café revenue target (₹ crore) |
|---|---:|
| 2025-26 (actual) | 260.0 |
| 2026-27 | 298.7 |
| 2027-28 | 343.1 |
| 2030-31 | 520.0 |

Each year's target is the previous year's × 1.1487. These annual targets are the short-range objectives that serve as steps toward the long-term objective; as the Study Material notes, a company that wants to double its sales within five years cannot wait until the third or fourth year to begin growing.
:::

:::: example The cold-chain company that described its trucks
Tapri Cold Chain Pvt Ltd, Tapri's cold-chain subsidiary, has 220 refrigerated trucks. Its board-approved mission statement reads: "We own and operate 220 refrigerated trucks and keep them running full." Its new CEO, Nikhil Sood, wants to sell space on the trucks to dairies, pharmacies and ice-cream makers as well as to Tapri, but managers keep refusing outside orders, saying "our mission is Tapri's trucks".

**Required:**

(i) Comment on the mission statement in the light of Drucker's question "What business are we in?", and suggest a better one.

(ii) Explain why Tapri Cold Chain needs a clearly defined mission.

::: answer Model answer
**(i) The statement.** According to Peter Drucker, every organisation must ask "What business are we in?" and get a correct and meaningful answer, which should have a marketing or external perspective and should not be restated as the production or generic activities of the business. Tapri Cold Chain has written a production-oriented answer: it describes its assets (220 trucks), not the needs it satisfies or the customers it serves. A company's business is defined by what needs it is trying to satisfy, which customer groups it targets, the technologies and competencies it uses and the activities it performs. A marketing-oriented mission could read: "We keep India's perishable products fresh from the factory to the shelf, for food, dairy and healthcare businesses, through a reliable temperature-controlled network." Such a statement is precise, clear, feasible, distinctive and motivating, and it supports the CEO's plan instead of blocking it.

**(ii) Why a mission is needed.** A mission explains the reason for the existence of the firm and identifies what business it undertakes. A clear mission would:

1. ensure unanimity of purpose, ending the conflict between the CEO and his managers;
2. develop a basis, or standard, for allocating resources (trucks, routes, sales staff) between Tapri and outside customers;
3. provide a basis for motivating the use of the organisation's resources;
4. establish a general tone or organisational climate, suggesting a business-like operation;
5. serve as a focal point for those who identify with the company's purpose and direction;
6. facilitate the translation of objectives and goals into a work structure, with tasks assigned to responsible people;
7. specify organisational purposes and translate them into goals, so that cost, time and performance parameters can be assessed and controlled.

Firms that fail to articulate their mission and business definition clearly fumble in identifying opportunities; the managers refusing outside orders show exactly that.
:::

:::: example Seventy per cent Assam
Tapri's premix packs say "100% Assam tea". A supplier offers a blend that is 70% Assam and 30% cheaper leaf, which tastes the same in blind tests and would add ₹9 crore a year to profit. Dev suggests keeping the old label "until the stock of printed packs runs out, about eighteen months". Tapri's published values include integrity and "never hide anything from a customer".

**Required:** Identify the component of strategic intent that should guide the board, explain its importance and advise the board.

::: answer Model answer
**Component.** The decision should be guided by Tapri's **values (value system)**: the deep-rooted principles which guide an organisation's decisions and actions.

**Importance.** As defined by Collins and Porras, core values are inherent and sacrosanct; they can never be compromised, either for convenience or short-term economic gain. They are the source of a company's distinctiveness and must be maintained at all costs. A company's values set the tone for how its people think and behave, especially in situations of dilemma, and create a shared purpose focused on the longevity of the company's success. They have internal implications (employees prefer employers whose values resonate with them) and external implications (most consumers prefer companies whose purpose reflects their own values and beliefs).

**Advice.** Selling a 70% Assam blend under a "100% Assam" label, for convenience (using up printed stock) and short-term gain (₹9 crore), would compromise integrity and the promise never to hide anything from a customer. The board should reject Dev's proposal. If the blend is adopted, the label must be changed before the new blend is sold. Profit targets are pursued within the values, not at their cost.

## Under the hood

### Business definition and business model

At the business level, strategic intent is expressed as the **business definition and business model** rather than vision and mission statements (SM 1.13). The current Study Material names them as forms of strategic intent and, with vision and mission, as the statements that "explain the philosophy of the organisation" (SM 1.14), but does not develop them further.

### Mission: present scope or future dream?

The Study Material says both. A mission statement is "typically focused on its present business scope, 'who we are and what we do'" (SM 1.17), and two pages later "the corporate mission is an expression of the growth ambition of the firm... the firm's future visualised" (SM 1.19). For a distinction question, use the first: ICAI's suggested answers contrast vision as the future identity with mission as the present scope and purpose. Use the second when a question quotes it (a mission as the firm's justification for existence, its "grand design").

### Vision versus mission: points from ICAI's suggested answers

The suggested answer to "Distinguish between Vision and Mission" (MTP January 2025 Series II Q3(b), repeated in MTP May 2025 Series II) adds points that are not in the current Study Material text: the vision describes a **future identity** while the mission serves as an **ongoing and time-independent guide**; the mission provides a path to realise the vision in line with the values; a well-crafted mission can remain the same for decades, while a vision is more specific about the future state and the time frame, and describes what will be achieved if the organisation is successful. They appear in the Answer frame below.

- **Present against future.** In ICAI's keys a statement about the present scope, the customers served or the reason for existing is the **mission** ("to bring the best user experience to its customers...", September 2024 MCQ 13); a statement about a future position, often with a date, is the **vision** ("We aim to lead the global transition towards 100% clean energy in the next 20 years" while the firm installs panels in one region, MTP May 2026 Series II (B)(ii)).

## Answer frame

### "Explain the significance of a mission statement" (5 marks)

1. Opening: a mission statement is typically focused on the present business scope, "who we are and what we do"; it describes present capability, customer focus, activities and business makeup.
2. The seven reasons, in order: unanimity of purpose; basis for allocating resources; basis for motivating the use of resources; general tone or organisational climate; focal point; translation of objectives into a work structure; specifying purposes so that cost, time and performance can be assessed and controlled.
3. If Drucker is asked: the eleven questions, then "the answer should have a marketing or external perspective", with one production-versus-marketing pair.

### "Distinguish between vision and mission"

| Point | Vision | Mission |
|---|---|---|
| Question | Where do we want to go? | What business are we in, and why do we exist? |
| Time | Future identity and future position | Present business scope; an ongoing, time-independent guide |
| Content | Aspirations; product-market-customer-technology focus | Business, goals and ways to reach them; capabilities, activities, customers, role in society |
| Role | Charts a strategic path, moulds identity, creates enthusiasm | Provides a path to realise the vision in line with values; legitimises existence |
| Change | More specific about the future state and the time frame | Can remain the same for decades if well crafted |

The "time-independent" and "decades" rows come from ICAI's suggested answer (MTP January 2025 Series II Q3(b)), not from the Study Material text.

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: the "vision" with a revenue target in it
"Our vision is ₹520 crore of café revenue by 2031." That is a long-term objective: concrete, measurable, time-bound. **Fix:** a vision is the blueprint of the future position (where the organisation wants to land); numbers and dates belong to objectives.
:::

::: mistake Mission written as a description of the factory
"We make 50 lakh premix packs a year." **Fix:** Drucker's test: answer "What business are we in?" from the customer's side, with the needs served and the customer groups targeted, not the production activity.
:::

::: mistake Ten characteristics, six remembered
Answers stop at "specific, measurable, time-bound, challenging" and lose the rest. **Fix:** start from the environment and end with resources: environment, mission, decision-making, appraisal, concrete and specific, time frame, measurable and controllable, challenging, correlated, within constraints.
:::

::: mistake Values treated as negotiable when the money is big enough
**Fix:** core values are inherent and sacrosanct: never compromised either for convenience or short-term economic gain (Collins and Porras). In a dilemma case, the answer is the value, not the profit.
:::

Q: Case scenario. Sahyadri Travels Pvt Ltd, a Pune bus operator, publishes four statements: (i) "To be India's most trusted intercity bus brand by 2035." (ii) "We carry students, families and workers between Pune and the towns of western Maharashtra in safe, affordable buses." (iii) "Cut the average delay per trip from 40 minutes to 15 minutes by March 2027." (iv) "We never send out a bus we would not put our own families on." Which of the following is correct?
- [ ] (i) Mission, (ii) Vision, (iii) Value, (iv) Objective
- [ ] (i) Vision, (ii) Objective, (iii) Mission, (iv) Value
- [ ] (i) Objective, (ii) Mission, (iii) Vision, (iv) Value
- [x] (i) Vision, (ii) Mission, (iii) Objective, (iv) Value
> (i) is a future position, (ii) the present business scope and customers, (iii) concrete, measurable and time-bound, (iv) a principle never to be compromised.

Q: Which of the following is NOT one of the four essentials of a strategic vision in the Study Material?
- [ ] Forming a strategic vision is an exercise in intelligent entrepreneurship
- [ ] A well-articulated strategic vision creates enthusiasm among the members of the organisation
- [x] A strategic vision must state the profit target for each of the next five years
- [ ] The best-worded vision statement clearly illuminates the direction in which the organisation is headed
> The essential missing from the options is the first in the Study Material's list: the entrepreneurial challenge of thinking creatively about how to prepare the company for the future (SM 1.16). Profit targets are objectives, not part of a vision.

Q: Dev drafts three objectives for the café division: "Cut the cost per cup by 5% within 12 months", "Open 300 new cafés next year" (with a capital budget sufficient for 80), and "Halve the number of baristas by March 2027 while also halving the average service time". Which characteristics of objectives do the second and third drafts breach? Select all that apply.
- [x] Objectives should be set within the constraints of organisational resources
- [ ] Objectives should be related to a time frame
- [x] Different objectives should correlate with each other
- [ ] Objectives should be challenging
> 300 cafés on money for 80 ignores resource constraints; fewer baristas and faster service pull against each other. Both drafts have a time frame and are certainly challenging.

Q: Which of the following correctly distinguishes strategic intent from values?
- [ ] Intent is the set of principles that guide decision making; values are the purpose of doing business
- [ ] Intent is fixed for decades, while values are revised each year along with the objectives
- [x] Intent is the purpose of doing business; values are the principles that guide decision making, and intent is sometimes driven by values
- [ ] Intent is expressed only at the business level, values only at the corporate level
> SM 1.23: the two go hand in hand, and because intent is sometimes driven by values, values are more or less wider than intent. The first option swaps the two. Values are sacrosanct, never revised for convenience, and intent appears at both levels (vision and mission at the corporate level, business definition and business model at the business level).

Q: Long-term objectives and the strategies that pursue them should have a consistent time frame of usually:
- [ ] One quarter to one year
- [x] Two to five years
- [ ] Ten to fifteen years
- [ ] Until the vision is achieved
> SM 1.21. Quarterly and annual targets are the short-range steps toward them.

## Recap

- Strategic intent: what the organisation strives for and why ("why they want to do"); the philosophical base of strategic management; vision and mission at the corporate level, business definition and business model at the business level.
- Vision: the blueprint of the future position, where the organisation wants to land; four essentials: think creatively, intelligent entrepreneurship, creates enthusiasm, illuminates the direction.
- Mission: what business we are in and why we exist; focused on the present business scope; seven reasons from unanimity of purpose to cost-time-performance control.
- Drucker: answer "What business are we in?" with a marketing (external) perspective, not a production one.
- Objectives: performance targets and yardsticks; ten characteristics; both short-term and long-term; seven long-term areas; usually two to five years.
- Values: deep-rooted principles, sacrosanct (Collins and Porras), never compromised for convenience or short-term gain; internal and external implications; wider than intent.
- Order: vision, then mission, then goals, then action, with values at the core. (Goa is the vision; the seat belt is a value.)

## Flashcards

Q:: What is strategic intent?
A:: The purposes the organisation strives for: "why they want to do" what they want to do; the philosophical base of strategic management, giving the framework and direction for achieving strategic objectives.

Q:: In what forms is strategic intent expressed at the corporate and business levels?
A:: Corporate level: vision and mission statements. Business level: business definition and business model. In precise terms: goals and objectives.

Q:: Define vision (SM 1.14).
A:: The blueprint of the company's future position; it describes where the organisation wants to land and gives a glimpse of what it would like to become in future.

Q:: The four essentials of a strategic vision?
A:: Thinking creatively about preparing the company for the future; an exercise in intelligent entrepreneurship; creates enthusiasm among members; the best-worded statement clearly illuminates the direction.

Q:: Define mission (SM 1.14).
A:: It delineates the firm's business, its goals and ways to reach them; explains the reason for its existence in society; defines present capabilities, activities, customer focus and role in society.

Q:: The seven reasons an organisation should have a mission?
A:: Unanimity of purpose; basis for allocating resources; basis for motivating the use of resources; general tone or climate; focal point; translating objectives into a work structure; specifying purposes so cost, time and performance can be controlled.

Q:: Drucker: how should "What business are we in?" be answered?
A:: With a marketing or external perspective, not restated as production activities (Lakme: "In the factory we make cosmetics; in the retail outlet we sell hope").

Q:: Define objectives (SM 1.20).
A:: The organisation's performance targets, the results and outcomes it wants to achieve; yardsticks for tracking performance and progress.

Q:: The ten characteristics of objectives?
A:: Define relationship with the environment; facilitate the mission; basis for strategic decisions; standards for appraisal; concrete and specific; time frame; measurable and controllable; challenging; correlated; within resource and environmental constraints.

Q:: The seven areas of long-term objectives?
A:: Profitability, productivity, competitive position, employee development, employee relations, technological leadership, public responsibility.

Q:: Define values and give the Study Material's examples.
A:: Deep-rooted principles that guide decisions and actions; inherent and sacrosanct, never compromised for convenience or short-term gain. Examples: integrity, trust, accountability, humility, innovation, diversity.

Q:: Intent or values: which is wider, per the Study Material?
A:: Values: intent is the purpose of doing business, values the principles that guide decisions, and intent is sometimes driven by values.
