## In plain words

### Kesar prices a can of chai

Tapri is launching Kulhad Cold Brew, a chilled chai in a can. Kesar has already decided the price.

> **Kesar:** It costs us ₹52 a can. Add 40%, round it off, sell at ₹75. Simple.
> **Ananya:** BrewBucks sells its cold coffee at ₹100 and the corner shop sells iced tea at ₹40. The market has already decided the price, Kesar-ji. Our job is the cost.
> **Kesar:** Then what do we decide?
> **Ananya:** Which products to push, which to milk, which to let go. And which of our activities actually make people pay.

Ananya is doing the first job of strategic analysis: **understanding the product and the industry** it sits in. A **product** is what a business sells: a physical good (a can of chai) or a service, even an experience (a kulhad café evening). Products can be touched or not, they carry a price the market largely sets, they have features that satisfy a need, everything in the business revolves round them, and they do not live for ever.

That last point gives the first tool. Plot a product's **sales against time** and you get an S-shaped curve with four stages: **introduction** (few buyers, almost no rivals, high price), **growth** (demand takes off, rivals arrive, prices fall), **maturity** (growth slows, competition gets tough, profits come down) and **decline** (sales fall sharply as something new replaces it). This is the **product life cycle (PLC)**. Knowing the stage of each product tells you what to do with it: expand the young ones, use the mature ones for cash, harvest or retrench the declining ones.

The second tool looks inside. Michael Porter's **value chain** breaks a business into the activities it performs: five **primary activities** that move the product to the customer (bring inputs in, make it, deliver it, sell it, service it) and four **support activities** that help all of them (buying, technology, people, the firm's infrastructure). **Value chain analysis** asks how much value each activity adds, or subtracts, so the firm can see where its competitive advantage really comes from.

::: analogy The mango season
A mango seller lives the PLC every summer. Early in the season, few mangoes, few sellers, high prices. Then every cart is full, buyers pour in, more sellers arrive and prices fall. At the peak, everyone sells the same Alphonso at the same price and margins are thin. By the monsoon, sales collapse as the market moves to jamun and pears. His day is a value chain: buying at the mandi and carrying the crates home, ripening and cutting, wheeling the cart to the station, shouting "Alphonso, sweet as sugar", and replacing the one bad mango a regular complains about. Behind it sit haggling with the wholesaler, his carbide-free ripening trick, training his nephew, and the notebook where he keeps accounts. His profit comes from doing some of these better than the next cart.
:::

::: key
Understand the industry through its product. The **product life cycle** (introduction, growth, maturity, decline) tells you the strategic choice for each product or business; the **value chain** (five primary and four support activities) tells you which activities create the value customers pay for, and so where competitive advantage is won.
:::

## How it works

After the macro environment ([[sm.ext.pestle|PESTLE and the international environment]]), the Study Material narrows down to the product and the industry it is sold in (SM 2.21). Four steps follow.

### Step 1: know what a product is

Businesses sell products; a product can be a good or a service, a physical good or an experience. The Study Material lists five characteristics of business products (SM 2.21–2.22):

1. **Products are either tangible or intangible.** A tangible product can be handled, seen and physically felt (a car, book, pen, table, mobile handset); an intangible product is not a physical good (telecom services, banking, insurance, repair services).
2. **Product has a price.** The dynamics of supply and demand influence the market price, the price at which quantity provided equals quantity desired. The price that may be paid is determined by the market, the quality, the marketing and the targeted group. In the present competitive world ==price is often given by the market and businesses have to work on costs== to maintain profitability: on account of competition they cannot fix the market price by adding a profit margin to costs.
3. **Products have certain features that deliver satisfaction.** A product feature is a component of a product that satisfies a consumer need. Features determine pricing and are altered during development to optimise the user experience; they distinguish the product in function, design, quality and experience. The customer's cumulative experience from purchase to the end of the product's useful life is an important component of a feature.
4. **Product is pivotal for business.** The product is at the centre of business around which all strategic activities revolve; it enables production, quality, sales, marketing, logistics and other business processes. Product is the driving force behind business activities.
5. **A product has a useful life.** Every product has a usable life after which it must be replaced, and a life cycle after which it is reinvented or may cease to exist: fixed-line telephone instruments have largely been replaced by mobile phones.

What happens in each stage, in the Study Material's words, and the strategic choice it points to (SM 2.22–2.23):

| Stage | What happens | Strategic choice |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | Slow sales growth, because customers are not aware of the product; competition almost negligible; prices relatively high; markets limited | Expansion may be a feasible alternative |
| Growth | Rapid market acceptance: demand expands rapidly, prices fall, competition increases, market expands; customers know the product and show interest in buying | Expansion may be a feasible alternative |
| Maturity | Slowdown in the growth rate; competition gets tough and the market stabilises; profit comes down because of stiff competition; the firm works to maintain stability | Use as a source of cash for investment in other businesses that need resources |
| Decline | Sharp downward drift in sales; sales and profits fall sharply because a new product replaces the existing one | A combination of strategies (diversification or retrenchment), such as selective harvesting and retrenchment |

**Why strategists use it.** The main advantage of the PLC approach is that it can be used to **diagnose a portfolio of products (or businesses)** to establish the stage at which each exists, paying particular attention to the businesses in the declining stage. Depending on the diagnosis, the appropriate strategic choice is made, and in this way **a balanced portfolio of businesses** may be built up (SM 2.23).

### Step 3: break the business into its value chain

Successful businesses produce value for their consumers (satisfaction) and for themselves and their shareholders (profits); companies that generate more value are more likely to profit. Understanding an organisation's value chain is critical for evaluating how much value it generates. Strategists use value chain analysis to **break down each process** the business employs, which can improve the sequence of operations, enhance efficiency and create a competitive advantage. It can be used by businesses of all sizes, from sole proprietorships to multinational organisations, to evaluate and optimise their processes (SM 2.23–2.24).

::: definition Value chain analysis
"Value chain analysis is a method of examining each activity in value chain of a business in order to identify areas for improvements." In doing it, you analyse how each stage in the process **adds or subtracts value** from the end product or service (SM 2.24).
:::

It has been widely used to describe the activities within and around an organisation and relate them to an assessment of its **competitive strength** (its ability to provide value-for-money products or services). It was originally introduced as an **accounting analysis** to shed light on the "value added" of separate steps in complex manufacturing processes, to find where cost improvements could be made and/or value creation improved. Michael Porter linked its two basic steps, **identifying separate activities** and **assessing the value added from each**, to an analysis of the organisation's **competitive advantage** (SM 2.24).

The key insight: organisations are much more than a random collection of machines, material, money and people. These resources are of no value unless deployed into activities and organised into systems and routines that produce what the final consumer values. So it is the **competences to perform particular activities** and the **ability to manage linkages between activities** that are the source of competitive advantage. Porter argued that understanding strategic capability must start with identifying these separate value activities (SM 2.24–2.25).

**The five primary activities** (SM 2.25):

- **Inbound logistics**: the activities concerned with receiving, storing and distributing the inputs to the product or service: materials handling, stock control, transport, warehousing.
- **Operations**: transform these inputs into the final product or service: machining, packaging, assembly, testing.
- **Outbound logistics**: collect, store and distribute the product to customers: for tangible products, warehousing, materials handling, transport; for services, arrangements for bringing customers to the service if it is at a fixed location (sports events).
- **Marketing and sales**: the means by which consumers or users are made aware of the product or service and are able to purchase it: sales administration, advertising, selling. In public services, the communication networks that help users access a service.
- **Service**: all activities that enhance or maintain the value of a product or service: installation, repair, training, spares.

**The four support activities**, each linked to the groups of primary activities (SM 2.25–2.26):

- **Procurement**: the processes for acquiring the various resource inputs to the primary activities (**not the resources themselves**); it occurs in many parts of the organisation.
- **Technology development**: all value activities have a "technology", even if it is simply know-how. Key technologies may concern the product (R&D, product design), processes (process development) or a particular resource (raw material improvements).
- **Human resource management**: a particularly important area that transcends all primary activities: recruiting, managing, training, developing and rewarding people.
- **Infrastructure**: the systems of planning, finance, quality control and information management, crucially important to performance in the primary activities; also the structures and routines of the organisation that sustain its culture.

### Step 4: see where the value is created

The concept of **value creation** was introduced primarily for providing products and services to customers with more worth. Value is measured by a product's **features, quality, availability, durability, performance and services** for which customers are willing to pay; the concept later widened to value creation for stakeholders, whose investment the business should make appreciate. Value creation is an activity or performance by the firm that increases the worth of goods, services, business processes or even the whole business system; ultimately it gives competitive advantage and helps earn above-average profits (SM 2.36–2.37).

At the most basic level, how profitable a company becomes depends on **three factors** (SM 2.37):

1. the **value** customers place on the company's products;
2. the **price** that the company charges for its products; and
3. the **costs** of creating those products.

The value a customer places on a product reflects its **utility**: the happiness or satisfaction gained from consuming or owning it. Utility must be distinguished from price: it is a function of the product's attributes, such as performance, design, quality, and point-of-sale and after-sale service (SM 2.37).

Porter argues that a company can generate competitive advantage in two ways, through **differentiation** (superior and special value in features, quality or after-sales service, which lets it demand a higher price; it earns higher profits if its expenses stay comparable to rivals') or through **cost advantage**. Many functions decide whether it achieves either, and the value chain, by dividing the organisation into primary and supporting activities, is ==an excellent tool to examine the origin of competitive advantage==: it shows the potential sources of differentiation and the organisation's cost behaviour (SM 2.37–2.38). The Study Material closes by describing value creation as the value the consumer wants to pay over and above the price the business charges: the consumer values the product more than it actually costs them (SM 2.38).

## Worked examples

:::: example Four products, four birthdays
Ananya is reviewing four of Tapri Tea Co. Ltd's products for the board.

(a) **Kulhad Cold Brew** cans were launched five months ago and are sold only in Tapri's own cafés in three cities. Sales are growing slowly because few customers know what cold brew chai is. No rival sells anything like it, and the price is high.

(b) **TapriNow**, the delivery app, has trebled its orders in a year. Two rivals have launched similar apps, delivery charges across the market have fallen, and new customers sign up every week because they now know the service.

(c) **Tapri Classic premix**, sold for 14 years, now grows at 2% a year. Six national brands fight for the same shelf, the market has stabilised and profit margins are falling because of stiff competition.

(d) **Chai Flask Service**, which delivers flasks of chai to offices, has seen sales and profits fall steeply for two years as offices install chai vending machines.

Using the product life cycle concept, identify the stage of each product and advise the strategic choice Tapri should make for each.

::: answer Model answer
The **product life cycle** is an S-shaped curve that exhibits the relationship of sales with respect to time for a product passing through four successive stages: introduction, growth, maturity and decline. Its main advantage is that it can be used to diagnose a portfolio of products to establish the stage of each, so that an appropriate strategic choice can be made and a balanced portfolio built up.

| Product | Stage | Reason (the facts) | Strategic choice |
|---|---|---|---|
| (a) Kulhad Cold Brew | Introduction | Slow sales growth due to lack of awareness, negligible competition, high price, limited market | Expansion may be feasible: build awareness, extend to more cities and outlets |
| (b) TapriNow | Growth | Demand expanding rapidly, competition increasing, prices falling, customers aware and interested | Expansion: invest to grow while the market expands |
| (c) Tapri Classic premix | Maturity | Slowdown in growth rate, tough competition, stabilised market, falling profit | Maintain stability and use it as a source of cash for products that need resources, such as (a) |
| (d) Chai Flask Service | Decline | Sharp fall in sales and profits as a new product (vending machines) replaces it | A combination of strategies such as selective harvesting or retrenchment; diversification may also be considered |

Particular attention should be paid to the Chai Flask Service, as it is in the declining stage. Using the premix's cash to fund the cold brew and the app gives Tapri a balanced portfolio, as the PLC concept suggests.
:::

## Under the hood

### Primary and support activities side by side

| Point | Primary activities | Support activities |
|---|---|---|
| What they do | Move the product from inputs to the customer and after | Help the primary activities perform |
| Number and names | Five: inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, service | Four: procurement, technology development, human resource management, infrastructure |
| How they relate | Each group is linked to the support activities | Procurement occurs in many parts; HRM transcends all primary activities |

This is why ICAI's suggested answer agrees that the two kinds are **intertwined**: each group of primary activities is linked to support activities (SM 2.25), and competitive advantage comes from managing the linkages between activities (SM 2.25).

### The PLC elsewhere in the paper

- The PLC works for businesses as well as products (SM 2.22), which is how it reaches portfolio analysis: the **ADL matrix** is a portfolio technique based on the product life cycle ([[sm.choice.ansoff|Ansoff's matrix and the ADL matrix]]); compare the [[sm.choice.portfolio|BCG and GE matrices]].
- Chapter 4 classifies strategies by the **stage of the business life cycle**: entry/introduction stage, market penetration strategy; growth stage, growth/expansion strategy; maturity stage, stability strategy; decline stage, retrenchment/turnaround strategy (SM 4.3). "A product has reached the maturity stage of the product life cycle" is the first of the major reasons for a **stability strategy** (SM 4.6), which is how ICAI asked it in May 2026 Q5(c) ([[sm.choice.retrench|stability and retrenchment]]); the decline stage's retrenchment and diversification are covered there and in [[sm.choice.growth|growth strategies]].
- The value chain's activities are where [[sm.int.generic|Porter's generic strategies]] are achieved: a focuser develops innovative ways of managing the value chain (SM 3.33).

### One MCQ in the Study Material's own test

The Study Material's Test Your Knowledge asks in which stage "the emphasis on product design is very high, the intensity of competition is low, and the market growth rate is low", and its key is **introduction** (SM 2.48, 2.50). Low competition plus slow growth points to introduction, not maturity.

::: trick In, Make, Out, Sell, Serve; and PTHI
Primary activities in order follow the product's journey: **In**bound logistics, **Make** (operations), **Out**bound logistics, **Sell** (marketing and sales), **Serve** (service). Support activities: **P**rocurement, **T**echnology development, **H**uman resource management, **I**nfrastructure ("PTHI", read upwards from the bottom of Porter's figure: procurement, technology, HRM, firm infrastructure at the top). Condition: transport belongs to inbound logistics when it carries inputs and to outbound logistics when it carries the finished product.

## Answer frame

### "What do you mean by value chain analysis? Delineate the support (or primary) activities." (5 marks)

1. **Meaning (1 mark; May 2025 Q7(a) printed the split as 1 + 4)**: a method of examining each activity in the value chain of a business to identify areas for improvement; how each stage adds or subtracts value; Porter linked it to competitive advantage.
2. **The activities (4 marks)**: name each in ICAI's order, with one line on what it covers.
   - Support: procurement (processes for acquiring inputs, not the resources themselves); technology development (product, process, resource); human resource management (transcends all primary activities); infrastructure (planning, finance, quality control, information management; structures that sustain culture).
   - Primary: inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, service, with an example each.
3. In an "intertwined" question, open with "Yes, I agree": each group of primary activities is linked to support activities.

### "Explain the characteristics of business products" (5 marks)

Five points for five marks, in the Study Material's order: tangible or intangible (with examples); has a price (market price where quantity provided equals quantity desired; businesses work on costs); features that deliver satisfaction (function, design, quality, experience); pivotal for business (centre of all strategic activities); has a useful life (replaced, reinvented or ceases to exist).

### "Write a short note on the PLC and its significance in portfolio diagnosis"

The S-shaped curve of sales against time; the four stages with their features; then the significance: diagnose the portfolio, attention to declining businesses, expansion for introduction and growth, mature businesses as sources of cash, selective harvesting or retrenchment for declining ones, a balanced portfolio. The Study Material's own answer includes a sketch of the curve with the stages labelled (SM 2.57–2.58): draw one.

### A PLC case: "identify the stage and suggest the strategy" (5 marks)

1. **Stage, from the facts**: quote the case facts that match the stage's features ("growth rate slow, market stabilised, profit down due to stiff competition": maturity).
2. **The strategic choice the PLC points to**: expansion (introduction, growth); source of cash and stability (maturity); selective harvesting, retrenchment or diversification (decline).
3. **The corporate strategy, if asked**: for a mature product, a stability strategy, with its reasons (the product has reached maturity; staff comfortable with the status quo; a relatively stable environment; expansion perceived as threatening; consolidation after rapid expansion), as in ICAI's answer to May 2026 Q5(c).

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: the delivery van that went into the wrong box
Bunty files every truck under outbound logistics, including the one bringing potatoes in. **Fix:** inbound logistics receives, stores and distributes the **inputs** (materials handling, stock control, transport); outbound logistics collects, stores and distributes the **product to customers**. Ask what the van carries.
:::

::: mistake "Procurement means the raw materials"
**Fix:** procurement refers to the **processes** for acquiring the resource inputs to the primary activities, not to the resources themselves (SM 2.25). Negotiating with suppliers is procurement, a support activity.
:::

::: mistake Calling every sales slowdown "decline"
A product whose growth has slowed in a stabilised market, with profits squeezed by stiff competition, is in **maturity**. **Fix:** decline needs a sharp downward drift in sales and profits, typically because a new product replaces the existing one.
:::

::: mistake Cost-plus pricing in a characteristics answer
Writing "businesses fix price by adding a margin to cost". **Fix:** the Study Material says the opposite: because of competition, price is often given by the market and businesses work on reducing costs to maintain profitability.
:::

::: quiz Check yourself
Q: Case scenario. Malabar Filter Kaapi Ltd has two products. Its bottled coffee decoction, sold for 18 years, still earns most of the group's profit, but its sales have barely moved for three years, nine brands now crowd the same shelves and margins slip every quarter; no new product has replaced decoction. Its new single-serve filter-coffee pods, launched last year, need money for machines and advertising. A director proposes "exiting decoction before it dies". Using the product life cycle concept, which advice is most appropriate for the decoction business?
- [ ] Retrench or harvest it, because falling margins mean it has entered the decline stage
- [x] Treat it as a mature business: maintain stability and use it as a source of cash to fund the coffee pods
- [ ] Expand it aggressively, because a product with most of the group's profit is in its growth stage
- [ ] Raise its price, because competition in its stage is almost negligible
> Slow growth, tough competition, a stabilised market and profits squeezed by stiff competition are maturity, and mature businesses may be used as sources of cash for businesses that need resources. Decline needs a sharp downward drift in sales and profits as a new product replaces the existing one, which has not happened. Expansion suits introduction and growth; negligible competition is introduction.

Q: Tapri's planning department, finance, quality-control laboratory and ERP system belong to which part of the value chain?
- [ ] Operations, a primary activity
- [ ] Technology development, a support activity
- [ ] Human resource management, a support activity
- [x] Infrastructure, a support activity
> Infrastructure is the systems of planning, finance, quality control and information management, and the structures and routines that sustain the culture.

Q: Which of the following are support activities in Porter's value chain? Select all that apply.
- [x] Procurement
- [ ] Outbound logistics
- [x] Technology development
- [ ] Service
- [x] Human resource management
> The four support activities are procurement, technology development, human resource management and infrastructure. Outbound logistics and service are primary activities.

Q: Customers value a premium kulhad gift box at ₹250; the company sells it at ₹200 and its cost of value creation is ₹160. What is the customer's surplus, in ₹?
= 50
> Customer's surplus = value to the customer − price = 250 − 200 = ₹50. The firm's margin is 200 − 160 = ₹40, and the profitable pricing band runs from ₹160 to ₹250.

Q: A tea estate's trucks carry plucked leaf from its gardens to its factory, where the leaf is stored before processing. This activity is:
- [x] Inbound logistics
- [ ] Outbound logistics
- [ ] Procurement
- [ ] Operations
> The trucks carry inputs to the factory, and the leaf is stored before it is processed: receiving, storing and distributing inputs is inbound logistics. Outbound logistics carries the finished product to customers.

## Recap

- Five characteristics of business products: tangible or intangible, has a price (often given by the market, so businesses work on costs), features that deliver satisfaction, pivotal for business, has a useful life.
- PLC: an S-shaped curve of sales against time through introduction, growth, maturity and decline; it works for businesses as well as products.
- Introduction: slow sales, negligible competition, high prices, limited markets. Growth: demand expands rapidly, prices fall, competition increases.
- Maturity: growth slows, competition tough, market stable, profit down. Decline: sales and profits fall sharply as a new product replaces it.
- PLC use: diagnose the portfolio; expand introduction and growth businesses, use mature ones for cash, harvest or retrench declining ones; build a balanced portfolio.
- Value chain analysis examines each activity to find improvements; Porter linked it to competitive advantage, which comes from competences in activities and managing linkages.
- Primary: inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, service. Support: procurement, technology development, HRM, infrastructure.
- Profitability depends on value to customer, price and cost: customer's surplus = value − price; firm's margin = price − cost; price profitably between cost and value. (The mango seller knew it all along.)

## Flashcards

Q:: What are the five characteristics of business products in the Study Material?
A:: Products are tangible or intangible; product has a price; products have features that deliver satisfaction; product is pivotal for business; a product has a useful life.

Q:: Define the product life cycle (PLC).
A:: An S-shaped curve showing the relationship of sales with respect to time for a product passing through four successive stages: introduction, growth, maturity and decline. It works for businesses too.

Q:: PLC: features of the introduction stage?
A:: Slow sales growth (customers unaware), competition almost negligible, prices relatively high, markets limited.

Q:: PLC: features of the maturity stage?
A:: Slowdown in growth rate, tough competition, stabilised market, profit down because of stiff competition; the firm works to maintain stability.

Q:: PLC: what strategic choice suits each stage?
A:: Introduction and growth: expansion may be feasible. Maturity: a source of cash for other businesses. Decline: a combination such as selective harvesting and retrenchment (or diversification).

Q:: What is the main advantage of the PLC approach?
A:: It can diagnose a portfolio of products or businesses to establish the stage of each, with particular attention to declining ones, so that a balanced portfolio can be built.

Q:: Define value chain analysis.
A:: A method of examining each activity in the value chain of a business to identify areas for improvement: how each stage adds or subtracts value from the end product or service.

Q:: Value chain: the five primary activities?
A:: Inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, and service.

Q:: Value chain: the four support activities?
A:: Procurement, technology development, human resource management, and infrastructure.

Q:: Value chain: what is procurement?
A:: The processes for acquiring the various resource inputs to the primary activities, not the resources themselves; it occurs in many parts of the organisation.

Q:: On what three factors does a company's profitability depend?
A:: The value customers place on its products, the price it charges, and the costs of creating those products.

Q:: Value creation figure: what are customer's surplus, firm's margin and the profitable pricing band?
A:: Customer's surplus = value to customer − price; firm's margin = price − firm's cost of value creation; the profitable pricing band runs from the cost of value creation up to the value to the customer.
