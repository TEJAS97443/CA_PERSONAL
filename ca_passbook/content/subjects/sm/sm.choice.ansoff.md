## In plain words

### Five ideas before breakfast

Kesar walks into Ananya's cabin with a list.

> **Kesar:** Sugar-free chai premix! Our chai in Dubai! Cold coffee cans for gyms in Singapore! A second cup at half price before 8 a.m.!
> **Ananya:** Four ideas, four boxes. Two questions sort them: is the product new, and is the market new?
> **Kesar:** And which box makes the most money?
> **Ananya:** The boxes tell you what kind of growth it is. Money is Dev's department.

Those two questions make **Ansoff's product market growth matrix**. Old product, old market: sell more to the people you already serve (**market penetration**, the half-price second cup). Old product, new market: take it to new people or places (**market development**, chai in Dubai). New product, old market: give your present customers something new (**product development**, the sugar-free premix). New product, new market: start afresh on both counts (**diversification**, the cold coffee cans), the riskiest box, because neither the product nor the market is one you know.

Then Ananya asks two different questions about each business: how old is its industry (just born, growing, mature, ageing), and how strong is Tapri in it (dominant down to weak)? The **ADL matrix** crosses the two and gives, in each of its 20 cells, a list of strategies to choose from.

::: analogy The biryani house
A biryani house can sell more biryani to its regulars (penetration), open the same biryani in a new city (market development), add a new kebab for the regulars (product development), or open a pizza place in a new city (diversification). Which of these makes sense also depends on how old the food street is and how famous the biryani house already is on it: that is the ADL question.
:::

::: key
Ansoff asks two questions, new product? new market?, and gives four growth strategies: penetration, market development, product development and diversification (the riskiest). ADL crosses the stage of industry maturity with the firm's competitive position, and the cell gives the strategies.
:::

## How it works

### Strategic options

The Study Material groups Ansoff's matrix, the ADL matrix, the BCG matrix and the GE matrix as **strategic options**: models that help strategists take decisions about the individual products or businesses in a firm's portfolio, primarily in multi-product, multi-business firms (SM 4.23, 4.32). The BCG and GE matrices are in [[sm.choice.portfolio|BCG and GE matrices]].

### Ansoff's product market growth matrix

The Ansoff's product market growth matrix (proposed by Igor Ansoff) is a useful tool that helps businesses decide their **product and market growth strategy**; it is a portfolio-planning tool for identifying growth opportunities for the future (SM 4.23).

::: diagram Ansoff's product market growth matrix

:::

The Study Material's definitions, each with its Gucci example (SM 4.24–4.25):

| Strategy | ICAI's definition | Gucci example |
|---|---|---|
| **Market penetration** | Selling **existing products into existing markets**, by making more sales to present customers without changing products in any major way | Luxury clothing in European markets, with new designs |
| **Market development** | Selling **existing products into new markets**, by identifying and developing new markets for current products | Luxury clothing in Chinese markets |
| **Product development** | Introducing **new products into existing markets**, by offering modified or new products to current markets | Casual clothing in European markets |
| **Diversification** | Marketing **new products in new markets**, by starting up or acquiring businesses outside the current products and markets | Casual clothing in Chinese markets |

The fine print that answers carry (SM 4.24–4.25):

- **Penetration** may need greater spending on advertising or personal selling; in a mature market it needs an aggressive promotional campaign with a pricing strategy that makes the market unattractive for competitors; it is also done by increasing usage by existing customers.
- **Market development** may be achieved through new geographical markets, new product dimensions or packaging, new distribution channels, or different pricing policies to attract different customers or create new market segments.
- **Product development** may require the development of new competencies.
- **Diversification** ==is risky because it does not rely on either the company's successful product or its position in established markets==; the business typically moves into markets in which it has little or no experience. ICAI's suggested answers call it the most risky strategy in the matrix (MTP May 2026 Series I Q1(c)).
- As market conditions change over time, a company may shift strategies: when its present market is fully saturated, it may have no choice other than to pursue a new market.

Penetration, market development and product development are the **intensification** options of the growth chapter, and diversification may be related or unrelated: both are classified in [[sm.choice.growth|growth strategies]]. The Study Material's **product-market expansion grid** (Table 3, SM 4.9) lists what each box involves:

| Box | Options in Table 3 |
|---|---|
| Market penetration | Increase market share; increase product usage; increase the frequency used; increase the quantity used; find new application for current users |
| Product development | Add product features, product refinement; develop a new-generation product; develop new product for the same market |
| Market development | Expand geographically; target new segments |
| Diversification | New products and new markets: related or unrelated |

### The ADL matrix

::: definition ADL matrix
The ADL matrix (derived its name from Arthur D. Little) is a portfolio analysis technique that is based on **product life cycle**. The approach forms a two-dimensional matrix based on stage of industry maturity and the firm's competitive position, environmental assessment and business strength assessment. (SM 4.25)
:::

- **Stage of industry maturity** is an environmental measure: the position in the industry's life cycle. The matrix uses four stages: **embryonic, growth, mature, ageing**.
- **Competitive position** is a measure of business strengths that places a product or SBU in one of five positions: **dominant, strong, favourable, tenable, weak**.

Four stages by five positions make it a four by five matrix (SM 4.26). The five positions are assessed on these criteria (SM 4.27):

## Worked examples

::::: example Four Tapri moves on Ansoff's grid
In the context of Ansoff's product market growth matrix, identify with reasons the growth strategy in each of the following moves Tapri has planned for next year.

(a) In Pune, where Tapri already runs 30 cafés, anyone who shows a BrewBucks loyalty card gets the first kulhad chai free, to win over BrewBucks' regulars.

(b) Chai-spiced cookies, a product Tapri has never made, baked for its cafés and sold across the counter to the same café customers.

(c) Tapri's cold-chain fleet, which has only ever carried Tapri's own milk and snacks, starts renting out its refrigerated trucks to pharmaceutical distributors for vaccine transport.

(d) The existing masala premix, unchanged, sold through the Tapri app to households in 200 small towns where Tapri has no café or shop.

:::: answer Model answer
The Ansoff's product market growth matrix helps a business decide its product and market growth strategy by asking whether the product, and the market, are existing or new.

**(a) Market penetration**: existing products into existing markets. Pune is a market Tapri already serves and the chai is unchanged. Customers won from a competitor in the same market increase Tapri's market share, which is market penetration; overcoming competition calls for an aggressive promotional campaign supported by pricing (SM 4.24). New customers do not by themselves make a new market.

**(b) Product development**: new products into existing markets, by offering modified or new products to current markets. The cookies are new to Tapri, but they are sold to its present café customers through its present cafés. A new product for the existing market is product development, however different the product is; it may require the development of new competencies (here, baking).

**(c) Diversification**: new products in new markets, by starting up businesses outside the company's current products and markets. Tapri has never sold refrigerated transport (the fleet only served Tapri itself), and pharmaceutical distributors are customers it has never served. Using an existing asset does not make the service an existing product. It is the riskiest option, because Tapri relies neither on a successful product nor on its position in an established market.

**(d) Market development**: existing products into new markets. The premix is unchanged; the 200 towns are new geographical markets, reached through a new distribution channel. The app is the channel, not a new product.

::::: example Three Tapri businesses on the ADL matrix
Ananya assesses three Tapri businesses for the board.

(i) **Chai premix.** The packaged premix industry is mature: growth has slowed and the brands are established. Tapri has a considerable degree of freedom over its choice of strategies and can act without its market position being unduly threatened by competitors.

(ii) **Kulhad cafés.** The café industry is in its growth stage. It is fragmented and no one competitor stands out clearly; as one of the leaders, Tapri has a reasonable degree of freedom.

(iii) **Cold-chain fleet.** The cold-chain trucking industry is mature. Tapri's performance in it is generally unsatisfactory, although opportunities for improvement exist.

Identify the competitive position and stage of industry maturity of each business on the ADL matrix, and the strategies the matrix suggests.

:::: answer Model answer
The ADL matrix is a portfolio analysis technique based on the product life cycle, which crosses the stage of industry maturity (embryonic, growth, mature, ageing) with the firm's competitive position (dominant, strong, favourable, tenable, weak).

**(i) Chai premix: strong position in a mature industry.** A strong firm has a considerable degree of freedom over its choice of strategies and is often able to act without its market position being unduly threatened by competitors. Suggested strategies: **lower cost, focus, differentiate, grow with industry**.

**(ii) Kulhad cafés: favourable position in a growth industry.** A favourable position generally comes about when the industry is fragmented and no one competitor stands out clearly, giving market leaders a reasonable degree of freedom. Suggested strategies: **focus, differentiate, defend**.

**(iii) Cold-chain fleet: weak position in a mature industry.** Performance in this category is generally unsatisfactory, although opportunities for improvement exist. Suggested strategies: **withdraw, divest**.

::::: example Advise Tapri when the home market is full
Tapri's 40 cafés in Indore and Bhopal already hold about 60% of the organised café market in the two cities, and sales at those cafés grew only 2% last year. The board wants growth. Dev adds a condition: the company can build only one new capability at a time, either a new product or a new market, never both at once. Ananya puts four proposals before the board:

1. A loyalty app with a "tenth cup free" offer at the existing cafés.

2. The same café format and menu, opened in Nagpur and Raipur.

3. A range of herbal infusions (tulsi, ginger, mulethi) for the existing café customers.

4. Bottled cold coffee for supermarkets in Dubai.

Required: Draw Ansoff's product market growth matrix, place the four proposals on it, and advise the board which proposal to take up first and which to postpone.

:::: answer Model answer
The Ansoff's product market growth matrix, proposed by Igor Ansoff, helps a business decide its product and market growth strategy and identify growth opportunities for the future. A business can adopt the four strategies simultaneously for different products, but its choice must fit its resources and its markets.

::: diagram The four proposals on the matrix
{"type": "table", "head": ["", "Existing products", "New products"], "rows": [["Existing markets", "Market penetration: loyalty app, tenth cup free", "Product development: herbal infusions"], ["New markets", "Market development: cafés in Nagpur and Raipur", "Diversification: bottled cold coffee in Dubai"]], "rowLabels": true, "cells": {"1,2": "hl"}, "caption": "Amber: diversification, the risky option, which needs a new product and a new market together."}
:::

1. **Market penetration (loyalty app)**: selling existing products into existing markets, by making more sales to present customers without changing products in any major way. It needs no new capability, but Tapri already holds about 60% of a market that grew only 2%: the present market is close to saturation, so penetration can defend share and add frequency of use, but cannot carry the growth the board wants.
2. **Market development (Nagpur and Raipur)**: selling existing products into new markets, here new geographical markets. It needs one new capability (a new market) and relies on Tapri's proven product. As the Study Material notes, when the present market is fully saturated a company may have no choice other than to pursue new markets.
3. **Product development (herbal infusions)**: offering new or modified products to current markets. It needs one new capability (a new product) and may require the development of new competencies, but it sells into the same saturated cities.
4. **Diversification (bottled cold coffee in Dubai)**: marketing new products in new markets. It needs two new capabilities at once, breaking the board's condition, and it is the riskiest strategy because it relies neither on Tapri's successful product nor on its position in established markets.

**Advice:** Take up **market development** first: it uses the existing product, needs only one new capability, and answers the saturation of the home market. Continue the loyalty app as low-cost penetration to defend share in Indore and Bhopal, without relying on it for growth. Take up **product development** next, once the new cities are running. **Postpone diversification** until Tapri has built the new product and new-market capabilities separately. As market conditions change over time, Tapri can shift between these strategies.

## Under the hood

### When BCG and ADL disagree

The two matrices measure different things. MTP May 2026 Series II (A)(ii) describes an EV division that is a **Question Mark** under BCG (low current market share) but **Dominant** under ADL (strong industry position and technological leadership); ICAI's answer is that the classification differs "due to conflicting metrics of growth and industry position". BCG looks at market growth and relative market share; ADL at the industry's life-cycle stage and a broader assessment of business strength.

### Ansoff's "diversification" is not always conglomerate

Table 3 marks the diversification box "related / unrelated" (SM 4.9). In a growth-strategy answer, say which kind it is: a dairy launching a product line of vegan yogurt and organic milk was keyed as concentric diversification (January 2026 MCQ 11), a gaming company launching a sports drink as conglomerate (RTP May 2025 Q13).

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: the new label that became a "new product"
New labels or packs for a new country turn into "product development". **Fix:** packaging adapted for new customers is one of the Study Material's means of **market development**; the product is still the existing one (MTP September 2024 Series I Q1(c)).
:::

::: mistake "Increase usage" filed under product development
"Brush your teeth twice a day" or "a second cup before 8 a.m." is still the existing product to existing customers: **market penetration** (the chapter's own toothpaste case, SM 4.49).
:::

::: mistake ADL positions mixed with BCG names
Writing "Dominant = Star" or "Weak = Dog". **Fix:** ADL's five positions (dominant, strong, favourable, tenable, weak) measure business strength against the industry's life-cycle stage; BCG's quadrants come from market growth and relative market share. A business can be a Question Mark and Dominant at once.
:::

## Recap

- Ansoff (Igor Ansoff): a product-market growth matrix for identifying growth opportunities; two questions, new product? new market?
- Penetration: existing products, existing markets (more sales to present customers; increase usage).
- Market development: existing products, new markets (new regions, segments, channels, packaging, pricing).
- Product development: new or modified products, existing markets; may need new competencies.
- Diversification: new products, new markets; risky; related or unrelated.
- ADL (Arthur D. Little): portfolio technique based on the product life cycle; stage of industry maturity (embryonic, growth, mature, ageing) × competitive position (dominant, strong, favourable, tenable, weak): a four by five matrix.
- Each ADL cell lists strategies: dominant-embryonic "fast grow, build barriers, act offensively"; weak-ageing "withdraw". (Don't Stop For Tea, Waiter.)

## Flashcards

Q:: Ansoff's matrix: what is it for?
A:: A tool, proposed by Igor Ansoff, that helps businesses decide their product and market growth strategy: a device for identifying growth opportunities for the future.

Q:: Market penetration?
A:: Selling existing products into existing markets, by making more sales to present customers without changing products in any major way.

Q:: Market development?
A:: Selling existing products into new markets: new geographical markets, new product dimensions or packaging, new distribution channels or different pricing to attract different customers or create new segments.

Q:: Product development?
A:: Introducing new or modified products into existing markets; it may require the development of new competencies.

Q:: Diversification (Ansoff): what is it and why is it risky?
A:: Marketing new products in new markets; risky because it relies neither on the company's successful product nor on its position in established markets.

Q:: Gucci in the Study Material: casual clothing in Chinese markets is which strategy?
A:: Diversification (new product, new market). Luxury clothing in China is market development; casual clothing in Europe is product development; luxury clothing in Europe with new designs is penetration.

Q:: ADL matrix: definition?
A:: A portfolio analysis technique (named after Arthur D. Little) based on the product life cycle: a two-dimensional matrix of stage of industry maturity and the firm's competitive position.

Q:: ADL: the four stages of industry maturity?
A:: Embryonic, growth, mature and ageing.

Q:: ADL: the five competitive positions?
A:: Dominant, strong, favourable, tenable and weak.

Q:: ADL: what is a "favourable" position?
A:: It generally comes about when the industry is fragmented and no one competitor stands out clearly; market leaders have a reasonable degree of freedom.

Q:: ADL: what is a "tenable" position?
A:: The firm performs satisfactorily and can justify staying in the industry, but is generally vulnerable to increased competition from stronger, more proactive companies.

Q:: ADL: strategies for a strong position in a mature industry?
A:: Lower cost, focus, differentiate, grow with industry.
