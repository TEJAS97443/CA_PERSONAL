## In plain words

### The record quarter that the market did not like

Tapri Tea Co. Ltd has just announced its best quarter ever. Kesar is ordering sweets for the whole office.

> **Kesar:** Record profit, Dev! I postponed the cold-chain truck overhaul and froze all hiring. Sab adjust ho jayega.
> **Dev:** And our share price fell 6% the day we announced it.
> **Kesar:** Who sells a share after a record profit?
> **Dev:** People who are pricing next year's broken trucks. The market discounts too, Kesar. Even faster than I do.

Kesar was chasing **profit maximisation**: taking each decision so that this period's profit is as large as possible. The shareholders were judging something else, **wealth maximisation**: what their shares are worth today, which depends on all the cash the business will produce in future, how soon it comes and how risky it is. Postponing the overhaul raised this quarter's profit and lowered the value of every future quarter.

Profit is easy to count, but the word hides choices: profit of which period, before or after tax, in total or per share? It ignores risk (a wild gamble and a safe bet with the same profit look equal) and timing (₹1 crore next year and ₹1 crore in ten years look equal). Wealth, measured by the market price of the shares, takes all three into account. That is why the Study Material treats ==wealth maximisation as the better objective== for a world that is uncertain and stretches over many periods, and why it calls the same idea **value creation**.

::: analogy The mango orchard
Profit maximisation counts this season's mangoes. You can pick every raw mango today and chop the branches for firewood: a record season and a dead tree. Wealth maximisation asks what a buyer would pay for the orchard today. That price counts every future season, how soon each harvest comes, and how likely the storms are.
:::

::: key
Profit maximisation asks "how much profit?"; wealth maximisation asks "how much are the owners' shares worth?", and so counts cash flows, their timing and their risk. In an uncertain, multi-period world, wealth (value) maximisation is the better objective of financial management.
:::

## How it works

### Why the finance manager needs a goal at all

Efficient financial management requires the existence of some objectives or goals, because the judgment as to whether or not a financial decision is efficient must be made in the light of some objective. Various objectives are possible; the Study Material takes up two for discussion: **profit maximisation** and **wealth / value maximisation** (SM 1.12).

### 1. Profit maximisation, and why it is at best a limited objective

It has traditionally been argued that the primary objective of a company is to earn profit, so the objective of financial management is also profit maximisation: the finance manager makes decisions so that the profits of the concern are maximised, and each alternative is judged by whether it gives maximum profit (SM 1.13).

But profit maximisation cannot be the sole objective of a company; it is **at best a limited objective**. If profit is given undue importance, a number of problems can arise; the Study Material discusses four, in this order (SM 1.13):

1. **The term profit is vague.** It does not clarify what exactly it means and conveys a different meaning to different people: profit may be short term or long term; it may be total profit or rate of profit.
2. **It ignores risk.** There is a direct relationship between risk and profit: many risky propositions yield high profit, and the higher the risk, the higher the possibility of profits. If profit maximisation is the only goal, the risk factor is altogether ignored, and the finance manager will accept highly risky proposals too if they give high profits. In practice risk is a very important consideration and has to be balanced with the profit objective.
3. **It ignores the time pattern of returns.** Proposal A may give more profit than proposal B, yet if A's returns begin to flow, say, 10 years later, B may be preferred: its overall profit is lower, but its returns flow earlier and quicker.
4. **It is too narrow.** It fails to take into account social considerations and the obligations to the various interests of workers, consumers and society, and ethical trade practices. If these are ignored, a company cannot survive for long: profit maximisation at the cost of social and moral obligations is a **short-sighted policy**.

### 2. Wealth maximisation, also called value creation

Shareholders' wealth is the result of **cost-benefit analysis adjusted for timing and risk**, that is, for the time value of money (SM 1.14):

$$\text{Wealth} = \text{PV of benefits} - \text{PV of costs}$$

The benefits are measured in **cash flows**, not accounting profit. The **shareholder value maximisation model** holds that the primary goal of the firm is to maximise its **market value**, so business decisions should seek to increase the net present value of the economic profits of the firm. To measure and maximise shareholders' wealth, the finance manager follows three things (SM 1.14):

::: steps
1. **Cash flow approach, not accounting profit**: count the rupees that actually come in and go out.
2. **Cost-benefit analysis**: set every benefit against every cost of the decision.
3. **Application of the time value of money**: bring each cash flow to its present value, at a rate that reflects its risk. Wealth is the present value of the benefits less the present value of the costs.
:::

**How is the wealth of a firm measured?** By the market. In Van Horne's words, the value of a firm is represented by the **market price of the company's common stock**: the focal judgment of all market participants, which takes into account present and prospective future earnings per share, the timing and risk of these earnings, the dividend policy of the firm and many other factors. "The market price serves as a performance index or report card of the firm's progress", showing how well management is doing on behalf of the shareholders (SM 1.14).

**Value creation.** The Study Material treats wealth maximisation and value creation under one heading (SM 1.14). ICAI's Suggested Answer to a value creation case puts it this way: value creation refers to making strategic decisions that generate ==sustainable returns exceeding the cost of capital== (MTP Series II for September 2025, Q4(a)). A project creates value only if it earns more than the cost of the funds it uses ([[fm.coc.wacc|the cost of capital]]); the rupee measure of that is its NPV ([[fm.capbud.npv|net present value]]).

### 3. Other goals, and why wealth still comes first

A business may follow other goals too (SM 1.15):

- Achieving a higher growth rate
- Attaining a larger market share
- Gaining leadership in the market in terms of products and technology
- Promoting employee welfare
- Increasing customer satisfaction
- Improving community life, supporting education and research, solving societal problems, etc.

These are important, but the primary goal remains wealth maximisation, as it is critical for the very existence of the business. If it is not met, the public and institutions lose confidence in the enterprise and will not invest further in its growth; and if growth is restricted, the other goals, such as community welfare, will not be fulfilled either (SM 1.16).

### 4. Profit versus value: where the two goals part company

In any company the management is the decision-taking authority, and as a normal tendency it may pursue its own goals (**profit maximisation**). But where there is significant outside participation (shareholding, lenders and so on), management cannot pursue only its own goals, because of the **constant supervision of the various stakeholders**: employees, creditors, customers, government. Each evaluates management by the fulfilment of its own objective, and the survival of management is threatened if the objective of any of them remains unfulfilled (SM 1.16).

The wealth maximisation objective is generally in accord with the interests of the various groups (owners, employees, creditors and society), so it may be consistent with the management objective of **survival**. Owing to the limitations of profit maximisation (timing, social consideration and so on), in today's real world, which is **uncertain and multi-period**, wealth maximisation is a better objective. Where the time period is short and the degree of uncertainty is not great, the two amount to essentially the same (SM 1.16).

Two ways profit can be bought at the cost of value (SM 1.17): a costly investment may make losses in the short term but substantial profits in the long term, so cutting it raises today's profit; and a firm that wants to show a short-term profit may postpone major repairs or replacement, which is likely to hurt its long-term profitability (Kesar's trucks).

**Risk, in ICAI's own illustration.** Two products earn ₹10,000 and ₹11,000 a year for five years, ₹50,000 against ₹55,000. Profit maximisation favours the second. But if it is riskier, the choice is not as straightforward as the figures seem: a trade-off exists between risk and return, and shareholders would demand a sufficiently large return to compensate for the greater risk (SM 1.18, Illustration 1).

## Formats and formulas

::: formula Shareholders' wealth
$$\text{Wealth} = \text{PV of benefits} - \text{PV of costs}$$
PV = present value. Benefits and costs are cash flows, discounted for timing and risk (SM 1.14).
:::

::: formula Value of a firm
$$V = N \times MP \qquad \text{or} \qquad V = V_e + V_d$$
V = value of the firm; N = number of shares; MP = market price of a share; Ve = value of equity; Vd = value of debt (SM 1.15).
:::

## Worked examples

::::: example The kiosk and the tea estate
Tapri Tea Co. Ltd has ₹1,000 lakh to invest today and two five-year proposals:

- **Station kiosks**: 60 more Tapri Express kiosks at railway stations, bringing in ₹320 lakh at the end of every year for five years.
- **Tea estate**: a Darjeeling estate whose new bushes take three years to mature, bringing in nothing in years 1 to 3, then ₹900 lakh in year 4 and ₹950 lakh in year 5.

Treat each year's inflow as its profit before charging the ₹1,000 lakh cost. Tapri's shareholders expect 12% on a proposal of normal risk. Present value factors:

| Year | PV factor at 12% | PV factor at 15% |
|---|---:|---:|
| 1 | 0.893 | 0.870 |
| 2 | 0.797 | 0.756 |
| 3 | 0.712 | 0.658 |
| 4 | 0.636 | 0.572 |
| 5 | 0.567 | 0.497 |

**Required:**

(i) Rank the proposals under profit maximisation, using total profit over the five years.

(ii) Calculate the wealth each creates for shareholders at 12%, and rank them under wealth maximisation.

(iii) The estate's crop depends on the weather, and the shareholders want 15% from it. Recalculate its wealth, and state which limitations of profit maximisation the case shows.

:::: answer Show the working
**(i) Profit maximisation**

| Particulars | Kiosks (₹ lakh) | Tea estate (₹ lakh) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Total inflows, years 1–5 | 1,600 | 1,850 |
| Less: Cost | 1,000 | 1,000 |
| **Total profit** | **600** | **850** |

Profit maximisation ranks the **tea estate first**: ₹850 lakh against ₹600 lakh.

**(ii) Wealth maximisation at 12%**

Wealth = PV of benefits − PV of costs.

- Kiosks: an equal annual flow, so PV = ₹320 lakh × (0.893 + 0.797 + 0.712 + 0.636 + 0.567) = 320 × 3.605 = **₹1,153.60 lakh**.
- Tea estate: PV = 900 × 0.636 + 950 × 0.567 = 572.40 + 538.65 = **₹1,111.05 lakh**.

| PV of benefits at 12% | 1,153.60 | 1,111.05 |
| Less: PV of costs | 1,000.00 | 1,000.00 |
| **Wealth created** | **153.60** | **111.05** |

Wealth maximisation ranks the **kiosks first**: the estate's larger profit arrives so late that it is worth less today.

**(iii) The estate at 15%**

PV = 900 × 0.572 + 950 × 0.497 = 514.80 + 472.15 = ₹986.95 lakh. Wealth created = 986.95 − 1,000 = **−₹13.05 lakh**: at a rate that reflects its risk, the estate destroys value although it shows the largest profit.

**Limitations shown.** Profit maximisation (a) ignores the time pattern of returns: ₹850 lakh, mostly in years 4 and 5, was ranked above ₹600 lakh spread evenly from year 1; (b) ignores risk: the weather-dependent estate was judged at the same yardstick as the kiosks; and (c) is vague: "profit" here meant total profit over five years, and a rate of profit or a one-year profit would give yet other answers. Wealth maximisation uses cash flows, cost-benefit analysis and the time value of money, so Tapri should choose the **station kiosks**.

:::: example The kulhad switch
Since 2025, Tapri's cafés in 12 cities have served chai only in kulhads (clay cups) and compostable paper cups, after three municipal corporations banned plastic-lined cups. Packaging cost has risen ₹14 crore a year. At the annual general meeting a shareholder says: "The company exists to maximise profit. Every rupee spent beyond what the law demands in the other cities reduces profit and is against the objective of financial management."

Examine the shareholder's view in the light of the objectives of financial management. (4 marks)

::: answer Model answer
**Relevant concept.** Profit maximisation cannot be the sole objective of a company; it is at best a limited objective. It is too narrow: it fails to take into account social considerations and the obligations to workers, consumers and society and ethical trade practices; if these are ignored a company cannot survive for long, and profit maximisation at the cost of social and moral obligations is a short-sighted policy. The objective of financial management is the maximisation of shareholders' wealth, measured by the market value of the shares, which takes into account the timing and risk of all future cash flows.

**Facts of the case.** The switch costs ₹14 crore a year more than plastic-lined cups. It is required by law in three cities and adopted voluntarily in the rest.

**Analysis.** The extra cost lowers the current year's accounting profit, which is all the shareholder is looking at. Wealth maximisation looks at the present value of all future benefits less all costs. The voluntary switch may protect future cash flows: it reduces the risk of fines and disruption if more cities ban plastic-lined cups, and supports customer satisfaction and the brand. Goals such as increasing customer satisfaction and improving community life are important, but the primary goal remains wealth maximisation, as it is critical for the very existence of the business enterprise; and the wealth maximisation objective is generally in accord with the interests of owners, employees, creditors and society.

**Conclusion.** The shareholder's view applies the narrow profit maximisation objective. Judged by wealth maximisation, the switch is consistent with the objective of financial management if the present value of the future benefits it protects exceeds the present value of its extra cost, and the board should evaluate it on that basis, not by its effect on one year's profit.
:::

State, with brief reasons, whether each statement is true or false. (4 marks)

(i) Where the time period is short and the degree of uncertainty is not great, profit maximisation and wealth maximisation amount to essentially the same.

(ii) Wealth maximisation ignores dividends, since it looks only at the market price of the shares.

(iii) Under wealth maximisation the finance manager measures the benefits of a decision in terms of accounting profit.

(iv) Tapri has 4 crore equity shares. If its share price rises from ₹330 to ₹345 because of a decision, the decision has added ₹60 crore to shareholders' wealth.

::: answer Model answer
| Statement | True or false | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| (i) | True | With a short horizon and little uncertainty, timing and risk hardly differ between alternatives, so the two objectives amount to essentially the same (SM 1.16). |
| (ii) | False | The market price takes into account the timing and risk of earnings and the dividend policy of the firm (Van Horne, SM 1.14). |

## Under the hood

### "Not an operationally feasible criterion"

A favourite ICAI wording. The Suggested Answers agree with the statement: profit maximisation can be a short-term objective and cannot be the sole objective; it fails to serve as an operational criterion for maximising the owners' economic welfare and fails to provide an operationally feasible measure for ranking alternative courses of action in terms of their economic efficiency. It suffers from four limitations (MTP Series I for May 2026, Q4(a)):

1. **Vague term**: short-term or long-term profit? Before or after tax? Total profit or profit per share?
2. **Timing of return**: it does not distinguish returns received in different periods and gives no consideration to the time value of money.
3. **It ignores the risk factor.**
4. **The term maximisation is also vague.**

### Why value beats profit: ICAI's four reasons

The RTPs for May 2024 (Q8(i)) and May 2025 (Q10(a)) answer "how is wealth maximisation superior?" with Van Horne's definition and four reasons:

1. Value maximisation considers all future cash flows, dividends, earnings per share and the risk of a decision; profit maximisation does not consider the effect on EPS, dividends paid or any other return to shareholders.
2. A firm maximising shareholders' wealth may pay regular dividends; a firm maximising profit may refrain from paying dividends.
3. Shareholders would prefer an increase in the firm's wealth to an increasing flow of profits.
4. The market price of a share reflects the shareholders' expected return, the firm's long-term prospects, differences in the timing of returns, risk, and the importance of the distribution of returns.

They close with a line worth quoting: profit maximisation can be considered **a part of the wealth maximisation strategy**.

### Two disadvantages of the winner

Wealth maximisation is not free of faults (SM 1.17): it offers no clear relationship between financial decisions and the share price (prices move for many reasons), and it can lead to management anxiety and frustration (managers watch a ticker they do not control). ICAI asks for these in "advantages and disadvantages of both goals".

## Answer frame

### "Explain the limitations of profit maximisation" (2 or 4 marks)

1. One line of context: profit maximisation cannot be the sole objective; it is at best a limited objective.
2. The four limitations in ICAI's order, each with its one-line reason: vague (short or long term, total or rate); risk ignored (higher risk, higher possible profit, risky proposals accepted); timing ignored (proposal A's returns 10 years later); too narrow (social considerations, short-sighted policy).

### "Distinguish between profit maximisation and wealth maximisation"

| Point | Profit maximisation | Wealth maximisation |
|---|---|---|
| Objective | Large amount of profits | Highest market value of shares |
| Measure | Accounting profit | Cash flows, discounted (PV of benefits − PV of costs) |
| Risk | Ignores risk or uncertainty | Recognises risk or uncertainty |
| Timing | Ignores the timing of returns | Recognises the timing of returns |
| Time horizon | Emphasises short-term gains | Emphasises long-term gains |
| Sustainability | Low: not sustainable in the long term | High: critical for the very existence of the business |

### "Comment" on a statement or a case

Verdict first ("The statement is true / not correct because…"), then the relevant limitations or advantages from the Study Material, then the facts of the case applied to them, then the conclusion: which objective the decision serves, and which ICAI prefers (wealth maximisation, in an uncertain multi-period world).

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: "wealth maximisation means making the owners rich at everyone else's cost"
**Fix:** the Study Material says the opposite: the wealth maximisation objective is generally in accord with the interests of owners, employees, creditors and society, and so may be consistent with management's objective of survival (SM 1.16). Its price is a report card that counts the long term.
:::

::: mistake Writing the comparison table's disadvantages as the four limitations
"Limitations of profit maximisation" asks for SM 1.13: vague, risk, timing, too narrow. The table's disadvantages (short-term gains, risk, timing, requires immediate resources) are a separate list. **Fix:** answer the list the question names; when the question says "disadvantages", use the table.
:::

::: mistake Calling the larger total profit the larger wealth
The tea estate made ₹850 lakh of profit and created ₹111.05 lakh of wealth (−₹13.05 lakh at its risk-adjusted 15%). **Fix:** wealth is present value of benefits less present value of costs; a larger profit that comes later or with more risk can be worth less.
:::

::: mistake Saying wealth maximisation "always" differs from profit maximisation
**Fix:** where the time period is short and the degree of uncertainty is not great, the two amount to essentially the same (SM 1.16). They part company when time and risk matter.
:::

## Recap

- Profit maximisation is at best a limited objective: vague, ignores risk, ignores the time pattern of returns, too narrow (in that order).
- Wealth maximisation measures success by the market value of the shares; wealth = PV of benefits − PV of costs.
- To measure wealth: cash flow approach (not accounting profit), cost-benefit analysis, time value of money.
- V = N × MP, or V = Ve + Vd. The market price is the "report card" of the firm (Van Horne).
- Value creation: decisions that earn sustainable returns above the cost of capital (ICAI's MTP answer); the SM treats it with wealth maximisation.
- Wealth maximisation stays the primary goal among growth, market share, leadership, employee welfare, customer satisfaction and community goals.
- In an uncertain, multi-period world wealth maximisation is better; with a short period and little uncertainty the two are essentially the same.
- Comparison table: profit (easy to calculate; short-term, ignores risk and timing, requires immediate resources) against wealth (long-term, risk, timing, shareholders' return; no clear link to share price, management anxiety). Kesar's record quarter cost him 6%.

## Flashcards

Q:: What are the four limitations of profit maximisation as an objective, in ICAI's order?
A:: (i) The term profit is vague; (ii) it ignores risk; (iii) it does not take into account the time pattern of returns; (iv) it is too narrow (ignores social considerations and obligations).

Q:: How is shareholders' wealth defined in the wealth maximisation model?
A:: Wealth = present value of benefits − present value of costs: cost-benefit analysis adjusted for timing and risk (time value of money).

Q:: Which three things does the finance manager follow to measure and maximise shareholders' wealth?
A:: Cash flow approach (not accounting profit); cost-benefit analysis; application of the time value of money.

Q:: According to Van Horne, what represents the value of a firm?
A:: The market price of the company's common stock: the focal judgment of all market participants, a performance index or report card of the firm's progress.

Q:: Write the two formulas for the value of a firm given in the Study Material.
A:: V = N × MP (number of shares × market price of shares), or V = Ve + Vd (value of equity + value of debt).

Q:: What does the shareholder value maximisation model hold?
A:: That the primary goal of the firm is to maximise its market value; business decisions should seek to increase the net present value of the economic profits of the firm.

Q:: Profit maximisation: the objective, the two advantages and the four disadvantages in ICAI's table?
A:: Objective: large amount of profits. Advantages: easy to calculate profits; easy to determine the link between financial decisions and profits. Disadvantages: emphasises short-term gains; ignores risk or uncertainty; ignores timing of returns; requires immediate resources.

Q:: Wealth maximisation: the objective, the four advantages and the two disadvantages in ICAI's table?
A:: Objective: highest market value of shares. Advantages: long-term gains; recognises risk or uncertainty; recognises timing of returns; considers shareholders' return. Disadvantages: no clear relationship between financial decisions and share price; can lead to management anxiety and frustration.

Q:: When do profit maximisation and wealth maximisation amount to essentially the same?
A:: Where the time period is short and the degree of uncertainty is not great.

Q:: Why does wealth maximisation remain the primary goal over other goals such as growth or community welfare?
A:: It is critical for the very existence of the business: if it is not met, the public and institutions lose confidence and will not invest further in its growth, and restricted growth leaves the other goals unfulfilled.

Q:: Value creation: ICAI's one-line meaning in the MTP Suggested Answer?
A:: Making strategic decisions that generate sustainable returns exceeding the cost of capital.
