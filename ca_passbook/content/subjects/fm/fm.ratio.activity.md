## In plain words

### The godown that was full to the ceiling

It is October, and Tapri's premix godown in Indore is stacked to the ceiling for Diwali. Kesar walks Dev through it like a museum.

> **Kesar:** Look at this, Dev. Sixty crore of premix. Nobody in India has more chai sitting ready.
> **Dev:** That is the problem, Kesar-ji. Sitting. How many times did this godown empty and refill last year?
> **Kesar:** Ten times? We sold ₹540 crore at cost, and on average ₹54 crore was lying here.
> **Dev:** Then each packet waits 36 days for a customer. Stock that sits is cash that sleeps.

Kesar has just computed an **activity ratio**: what the business did in a year (here the **cost of goods sold**, ₹540 crore) divided by what it held to do it (the **average inventory**, ₹54 crore). The answer, 10, is the **inventory turnover**: the stock "turned over" ten times. Divide the 360 days of a year by it and you get the days a rupee of stock waits to be sold: 36.

The same idea works for every asset. Tapri's ₹900 crore of sales came from ₹505 crore of total assets, so each rupee of assets "produced" ₹1.78 of sales. Credit customers took ₹720 crore of goods while owing Tapri ₹36 crore on average: they turned over 20 times and paid in about 18 days. Suppliers sold Tapri ₹432 crore on credit while being owed ₹48 crore on average: Tapri paid them 9 times a year, every 40 days.

::: analogy The kettle at the tapri
A roadside tapri with one kettle that is refilled and emptied forty times a day earns more than a five-star coffee shop whose ten kettles are refilled four times a day. The kettle is the asset; the refills are its turnover. A big godown, a big factory or a big pile of debtors is only impressive if it keeps emptying and refilling.
:::

::: key
An activity ratio divides a year's sales (or cost of goods sold) by an asset. The higher it is, the more efficiently the asset is used; and 360 divided by a turnover gives the number of days the asset holds each rupee: days in stock, days to collect from debtors, days taken to pay creditors.
:::

## How it works

### Asset management ratios

Activity ratios (also called efficiency, performance or turnover ratios) are employed to evaluate the efficiency with which the firm manages and utilises its assets; for this reason they are often called **asset management ratios**. They usually indicate the frequency of sales with respect to an asset, are calculated with reference to sales or cost of goods sold, and are expressed as a rate or "times" (SM 3.13–3.14). ICAI lists them in this order (SM 3.14):

- (a) Total assets turnover ratio
- (b) Fixed assets turnover ratio
- (c) Capital turnover ratio / net assets turnover ratio
- (d) Current assets turnover ratio
- (e) Working capital turnover ratio, which is further segregated into (i) inventory / stock turnover, (ii) receivables (debtors) turnover and (iii) payables (creditors) turnover.

### Same sales, five bases

Tapri's year to 31 March 2026 ([[fm.ratio.liquidity|the balance sheet from the liquidity lesson]]): sales ₹900 crore, total assets ₹505 crore, net assets (capital employed) ₹435 crore, fixed assets ₹355 crore, current assets ₹150 crore, working capital ₹80 crore. The same ₹900 crore divided by smaller and smaller bases:

::: steps
1. **Total assets turnover** = sales (or COGS) ÷ total assets = 900 ÷ 505 = **1.78 times**: the efficiency with which the firm uses its total assets; the higher the ratio, the better (SM 3.14).
2. **Fixed assets turnover** = 900 ÷ 355 = **2.54 times**: the efficiency in using fixed assets. A firm whose plant and machinery are old may show a higher ratio than one that bought them recently, because the old plant stands in the books at a written-down value (SM 3.15).
3. **Capital turnover** (net assets turnover) = 900 ÷ 435 = **2.07 times**: since net assets equal capital employed, it shows the sales generated per rupee of long-term investment; the higher, the more efficient the use of owners' and long-term creditors' funds (SM 3.15).
4. **Current assets turnover** = 900 ÷ 150 = **6.00 times** (SM 3.15).
5. **Working capital turnover** = 900 ÷ 80 = **11.25 times**: sales generated for every rupee of working capital. Higher is more efficient, but ==a very high working capital turnover ratio indicates that the company needs to raise additional working capital for future needs== (SM 3.15).
:::

ICAI allows the average of total, fixed, current or net assets or working capital in the denominator, and when averages of total assets, net assets, capital employed or shareholders' funds are available, they may be preferred (SM 3.16, 3.18). Tapri's figures above use closing balances because only one balance sheet is given.

### Working capital turnover, split three ways

::: diagram The three turnovers inside working capital
{"type": "tree", "root": {"label": "Working capital\nturnover", "children": [
 {"label": "Inventory\nturnover", "note": "COGS", "color": "a"},
 {"label": "Receivables\nturnover", "note": "credit sales", "color": "b"},
 {"label": "Payables\nturnover", "note": "credit purchases", "color": "c"}
 ]},
 "caption": "Under each turnover, its numerator; the denominator is the average balance (stock, debtors, creditors). Each pairs a balance with the flow that passes through it: stock is carried at cost, so it pairs with cost of goods sold; debtors arise from credit sales; creditors from credit purchases."}
:::

Tapri's balances (₹ crore): inventory 48 at the start of the year and 60 at the end; trade receivables 32 and 40; trade payables 44 and 52. Credit sales were ₹720 crore (cafés sell for cash), cost of goods sold ₹540 crore, and credit purchases ₹432 crore (the rest of the year's purchases were paid for in cash).

::: steps
1. **Inventory turnover** = COGS ÷ average inventory, where average inventory = (opening + closing) ÷ 2 = (48 + 60) ÷ 2 = 54. So 540 ÷ 54 = **10 times**, and 360 ÷ 10 = **36 days** in stock. It shows how fast inventory is used or sold: a high ratio is good from the point of view of liquidity; a low ratio means stock is not used, not sold or lost, and stays on a shelf or in the warehouse for a long time (SM 3.16).
2. **Receivables (debtors) turnover** = credit sales ÷ average accounts receivable = 720 ÷ 36 = **20 times**. A low ratio reflects liberal credit terms; a high ratio, rapid collection (SM 3.17).
3. **Average collection period** (receivables velocity) = average receivables ÷ average daily credit sales = 36 ÷ (720 ÷ 360) = 36 ÷ 2 = **18 days**, which is also 360 ÷ 20. It is the average number of days taken to collect a receivable, also called the number of days of receivable or the days' sales in receivables, and it gives unique guidance in setting the credit policy (SM 3.17).
4. **Payables (creditors) turnover** = annual net credit purchases ÷ average accounts payable = 432 ÷ 48 = **9 times**: how fast the company pays its creditors. A low ratio reflects liberal credit terms granted by suppliers; a high ratio, prompt settlement (SM 3.17).
5. **Average payment period** (payables velocity) = average payables ÷ average daily credit purchases = 48 ÷ (432 ÷ 360) = **40 days**, or 360 ÷ 9. The firm compares the credit it receives from suppliers with the credit it gives customers, and with the credit customary in its industry (SM 3.17–3.18).
:::

Tapri takes 40 days to pay its suppliers and gives its customers only 18: its average creditors (₹48 crore) more than cover its average debtors (₹36 crore). ICAI adds that these three ratios are also relevant to examine liquidity (SM 3.18), which is why [[fm.ratio.liquidity|the liquidity ratios]] should be read with them.

## Formats and formulas

*Reproduce these from memory, with the numerator that belongs to each denominator.*

| Ratio | Numerator | Denominator |
|---|---|---|
| Total assets turnover | Sales or COGS | Total assets |
| Fixed assets turnover | Sales or COGS | Fixed assets |
| Capital (net assets) turnover | Sales or COGS | Net assets |
| Current assets turnover | Sales or COGS | Current assets |
| Working capital turnover | Sales or COGS | Working capital |
| Inventory turnover | COGS or sales | Average inventory |
| Raw material inventory turnover | Raw material consumed | Average raw material stock |
| Receivables turnover | Credit sales | Average receivables |
| Payables turnover | Net credit purchases | Average payables |

::: formula Asset-based turnovers
$$\text{Turnover} = \frac{\text{Sales or COGS}}{\text{Asset base}}$$
Asset base = total assets, fixed assets, net assets (capital employed), current assets or working capital; their averages may be used (SM 3.14–3.16).
:::

::: formula Inventory turnover
$$\text{Inventory turnover} \qquad = \frac{\text{COGS}}{\text{Average inventory}}$$
$$\text{Average inventory} \qquad = \frac{\text{Opening stock} + \text{Closing stock}}{2}$$
$$\text{Raw material inventory turnover} \qquad = \frac{\text{Raw material consumed}}{\text{Average raw material stock}}$$
ICAI prints the numerator as "Cost of Goods Sold / Sales" (SM 3.16): use COGS unless the question says "based on sales".
:::

::: formula Receivables turnover and average collection period
$$\text{Receivables turnover} \qquad = \frac{\text{Credit sales}}{\text{Average receivables}}$$
$$\text{Average collection period} \qquad = \frac{\text{Average receivables}}{\text{Average daily credit sales}} \qquad = \frac{\text{360 days}}{\text{Receivables turnover}}$$
Average daily credit sales = credit sales ÷ number of days in the year (say 360); with months or weeks, use 12 or 52 in place of 360 (SM 3.17).
:::

::: formula Payables turnover and average payment period
$$\text{Payables turnover} \qquad = \frac{\text{Net credit purchases}}{\text{Average payables}}$$
$$\text{Average payment period} \qquad = \frac{\text{Average payables}}{\text{Average daily credit purchases}} \qquad = \frac{\text{360 days}}{\text{Payables turnover}}$$
Net credit purchases are the year's (annual) credit purchases; 12 months or 52 weeks may replace 360 days (SM 3.17).
:::

::: formula Purchases from the trading account
$$\text{Purchases} \qquad = \text{COGS} + \text{Closing} - \text{Opening stock}$$
Needed when a question gives sales, gross profit and stocks but not purchases.
:::

::: note ICAI's notes for turnover ratios (SM 3.18)
1. Only selling and distribution expenses differentiate cost of goods sold (COGS) and cost of sales (COS); in their absence, COGS = cost of sales.
2. Cost of goods sold or cost of sales can be used to calculate turnover ratios, eliminating the profit part.
3. Averages of total, fixed, current or net assets or working capital can also be taken in the denominator; when average figures are available they may be preferred.
4. Ratios shall be calculated based on requirement and availability of information and may deviate from the original formulae; if required, assumptions should be given.
:::

:::: example Rasoi Packaging: the bank that found the turnover too good
Rasoi Packaging Pvt Ltd prints Tapri's premix pouches and has applied to its bank to renew a cash credit limit. Its sales for 2025-26 were ₹7.20 crore, 80% on credit, and its cost of goods sold ₹5.40 crore. All purchases are on credit, and the stock at the start of the year was ₹40 lakh. All ratios are on closing balances unless stated; assume a 360-day year.

| Ratio | Figure |
|---|---|
| Total assets turnover (on sales) | 2.4 times |
| Fixed assets turnover (on sales) | 4 times |
| Working capital turnover (on sales) | 12 times |
| Inventory turnover (on COGS and average inventory) | 10.8 times |
| Average collection period (on credit sales) | 30 days |
| Payables turnover (on credit purchases) | 20 times |

Current liabilities are trade creditors and the bank's cash credit.

**Required:**

(i) Calculate total assets, fixed assets, current assets, working capital and current liabilities, and the capital (net assets) turnover ratio.

(ii) Calculate the average and closing stock.

(iii) Calculate the debtors and the cash balance.

(iv) Calculate the trade creditors and the cash credit.

(v) The bank's officer says a working capital turnover of 12 is "too good to be comfortable". Comment, and calculate the cash released if the collection period were brought down to 20 days with credit sales unchanged.

::: answer Show the working
**Working notes** (₹ lakh)

1. Credit sales = 80% × 720 = **576**.
2. Total assets = sales ÷ total assets turnover = 720 ÷ 2.4 = **300**; fixed assets = 720 ÷ 4 = **180**; current assets = 300 − 180 = **120**.
3. Working capital = 720 ÷ 12 = **60**; current liabilities = 120 − 60 = **60** (current ratio 2).
4. Net assets (capital employed) = total assets − current liabilities = 300 − 60 = 240, so capital turnover = 720 ÷ 240 = **3 times**.

**(ii) Stock**

Average stock = COGS ÷ inventory turnover = 540 ÷ 10.8 = **₹50 lakh** (360 ÷ 10.8 = 33.3 days). Closing stock = 2 × average − opening = 2 × 50 − 40 = **₹60 lakh**.

**(iii) Debtors and cash**

Debtors = credit sales × collection period ÷ 360 = 576 × 30 ÷ 360 = **₹48 lakh**. Cash = current assets − stock − debtors = 120 − 60 − 48 = **₹12 lakh** (balancing figure).

**(iv) Creditors and cash credit**

Credit purchases = COGS + closing stock − opening stock = 540 + 60 − 40 = ₹560 lakh. Trade creditors = 560 ÷ 20 = **₹28 lakh** (about 18 days of purchases). Cash credit = current liabilities − creditors = 60 − 28 = **₹32 lakh** (balancing figure).

**(v) Comment**

A higher working capital turnover means more sales for every rupee of working capital, but ICAI's interpretation is that a very high working capital turnover ratio indicates that the company needs to raise additional working capital for future needs (SM 3.15). Rasoi runs ₹720 lakh of sales on ₹60 lakh of working capital: it pays its suppliers in about 18 days while it waits 30 days for its customers and holds about 33 days of stock, and it already borrows ₹32 lakh on cash credit. Any growth in sales will need more working capital, so the bank's caution is justified.

At a collection period of 20 days, debtors = 576 × 20 ÷ 360 = ₹32 lakh, so **₹16 lakh** of cash is released (48 − 32), half of the cash credit.

## Under the hood

### Cost of goods sold or sales?

Inventory is carried at cost, so dividing cost of goods sold by it keeps both sides at cost; ICAI's note puts it as using COGS or cost of sales "eliminating profit part" (SM 3.18), and its note 3 on all ratios asks that the numerator be taken in correspondence with the denominator (SM 3.13). Questions often say which to use ("based on cost of goods sold", "use sales as turnover"); when they do not, ICAI's suggested answers note the choice as an assumption. In the Study Material's Practical Problem 9 (SM 3.91), the stock turnover and fixed assets turnover "may be calculated either on Sales or on Cost of Goods Sold".

### When the question leaves something out

- **No credit sales given:** ICAI takes total sales as credit sales, and states it ("all sales are credit sales" appears as an assumption in Practical Problem 9, SM 3.91).
- **No opening balance:** the closing balance stands in for the average. MTP Series I for May 2025 says so in its answer: "In the absence of credit sales and opening debtors, total sales is considered as credit sales and closing debtors as average debtors."
- **Bills:** bills receivable are part of receivables and bills payable part of payables: Practical Problem 9 divides credit sales by "Bills Receivable + Sundry Debtors" (SM 3.89–3.90).

### Raw material turnover and the CMA link

For a manufacturer the inventory is in layers (raw materials, work-in-progress, finished goods). Raw material inventory turnover = raw material consumed ÷ average raw material stock (SM 3.16); the same ratio in Paper 4 is built from the cost of materials consumed in [[cma.mat.ratios|inventory turnover in Cost and Management Accounting]]. May 2025 Q1(a) gave a raw material inventory turnover of 4 and a finished goods holding period of 0.75 month and asked for both stocks.

### Days, and the working capital cycle

The days in stock, the collection period and the payment period are the raw material of the operating cycle: how long cash stays locked between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. The cycle itself, and how it drives the working capital requirement, are in [[fm.wc.basics|working capital and the operating cycle]].

Balance = annual flow × days ÷ 360 (or × months ÷ 12). Average debtors = credit sales × collection period ÷ 360; average stock = COGS × days in stock ÷ 360; average creditors = credit purchases × payment period ÷ 360. Conditions: the period is based on the same flow and on average balances, and the year length matches the question's.

## Answer frame

### "Discuss stock turnover ratio" and "ratios for operating efficiency"

The chapter's theory questions include "Discuss Stock Turnover ratio and Gearing ratio" and "Discuss the financial ratios for evaluating company performance on operating efficiency and liquidity position aspects" (SM 3.65). For a turnover ratio, write:

1. What it measures: the efficiency with which the firm manages and utilises the asset (activity ratios are asset management ratios).
2. The formula, with the numerator that matches the denominator (COGS for stock, credit sales for debtors, credit purchases for creditors) and the average balance.
3. The conversion to days or months.
4. The interpretation in ICAI's words (a high stock turnover is good for liquidity; a low one means stock stays on the shelf; a low debtors' turnover reflects liberal credit terms).
5. The comparison that gives it meaning: previous years, competitors, the industry.

For **operating efficiency** (SM 3.34): ratio analysis throws light on the degree of efficiency in the management and utilisation of assets; the activity ratios measure this operational efficiency; and the solvency of a firm is, in the ultimate analysis, dependent upon the sales revenues generated by the use of its assets, total as well as its components.

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: stock at cost, sales at selling price
Dividing sales by average stock mixes a selling-price numerator with a cost denominator and overstates the turnover (Tapri: 900 ÷ 54 = 16.7 instead of 10). **Fix:** use cost of goods sold for inventory unless the question says "based on sales".
:::

::: mistake Total sales in the debtors turnover
Using ₹900 crore of sales when only ₹720 crore was on credit makes Tapri's collection period 14.4 days instead of 18: cash sales never create a debtor. **Fix:** credit sales ÷ average receivables; take total sales only when credit sales are not given, and say so.
:::

::: mistake Cost of goods sold in the creditors turnover
Creditors arise from purchases, not from goods sold. **Fix:** derive purchases (COGS + closing stock − opening stock) and use the credit part of them.
:::

::: mistake The closing balance that pretended to be an average
When the opening balance is given, the average is (opening + closing) ÷ 2. Using the closing balance alone mis-states the turnover whenever the balance moved during the year. **Fix:** average whenever you can; use the closing balance only when nothing else is given, and state it.
:::

Q: A spice wholesaler's cost of goods sold for the year is ₹45 lakh. Its stock fell from ₹8 lakh at the start of the year to ₹5 lakh at the end. Three-quarters of its purchases are on credit, and its trade creditors were ₹4.50 lakh at the start of the year and ₹6 lakh at the end. What is its average payment period, in days (360-day year)?
= 60
> Purchases = COGS + closing stock − opening stock = 45 + 5 − 8 = ₹42 lakh (a fall in stock means less was bought than was sold); credit purchases = 75% × 42 = ₹31.50 lakh; average creditors = (4.50 + 6) ÷ 2 = ₹5.25 lakh; payment period = 5.25 ÷ 31.50 × 360 = 60 days (payables turnover 6 times). Using COGS instead of purchases gives 56 days; adding the fall in stock gives 52.5 days; using all purchases gives 45 days.

Q: Case scenario. Tapri's head of modern trade reports that credit sales to supermarket chains were ₹48 crore, debtors were ₹5 crore at the start of the year and ₹11 crore at the end, and that "supermarkets pay us in about 82 days". Dev, who uses a 360-day year, says the figure does not follow ICAI's method. What is the average collection period on ICAI's formula?
- [ ] 82.5 days
- [x] 60 days
- [ ] 37.5 days
- [ ] 6 days
> Average debtors = (5 + 11) ÷ 2 = ₹8 crore; turnover = 48 ÷ 8 = 6 times; collection period = 360 ÷ 6 = 60 days. 82.5 days uses closing debtors; 37.5 days uses opening debtors; 6 is the turnover in times, not days.

Q: According to ICAI, a very high working capital turnover ratio indicates that the company:
- [ ] Holds idle working capital
- [x] Needs to raise additional working capital for future needs
- [ ] Has a very high current ratio
- [ ] Is selling too little for its assets
> Higher working capital turnover means efficient use of working capital, but a very high ratio means sales are being run on too thin a working capital, so more will be needed (SM 3.15).

Q: A company's sales are ₹80 lakh, cost of goods sold ₹60 lakh, opening stock ₹8 lakh and closing stock ₹12 lakh. Nothing is said about the basis. What is its inventory turnover?
- [x] 6 times
- [ ] 8 times
- [ ] 7.5 times
- [ ] 5 times
> COGS ÷ average stock = 60 ÷ 10 = 6 times. 8 uses sales; 7.5 uses opening stock alone; 5 uses closing stock alone.

Q: Which of these statements are correct according to ICAI? Select all that apply.
- [x] A low debtors' turnover ratio reflects liberal credit terms granted to customers
- [x] A low creditors' turnover ratio reflects liberal credit terms granted by suppliers
- [x] A firm with old plant may show a higher fixed assets turnover than one that bought its plant recently
- [ ] A high inventory turnover is bad from the point of view of liquidity
> The first three are ICAI's interpretations (SM 3.15, 3.17). A high inventory turnover is good for liquidity: stock turns into sales quickly (SM 3.16).

## Recap

- Activity ratios (efficiency, performance, turnover; "asset management ratios") = sales or COGS ÷ an asset, in "times".
- Total assets, fixed assets, capital (net assets), current assets and working capital turnover: sales or COGS ÷ that base; averages may be used.
- A very high working capital turnover means more working capital will be needed.
- Inventory turnover = COGS ÷ average inventory; raw material turnover = materials consumed ÷ average raw material stock.
- Debtors turnover = credit sales ÷ average receivables; collection period = 360 ÷ turnover (or average receivables ÷ daily credit sales).
- Creditors turnover = credit purchases ÷ average payables; payment period = 360 ÷ turnover. Purchases = COGS + closing − opening stock.
- Balance = annual flow × days ÷ 360. (A full godown is only impressive if it keeps emptying.)

## Flashcards

Q:: Why does ICAI call activity ratios "asset management ratios"?
A:: Because they evaluate the efficiency with which the firm manages and utilises its assets.

Q:: List ICAI's activity ratios in order.
A:: Total assets turnover; fixed assets turnover; capital (net assets) turnover; current assets turnover; working capital turnover, segregated into inventory, receivables and payables turnover.

Q:: Formula: inventory turnover ratio and average inventory?
A:: Cost of goods sold (or sales) ÷ average inventory; average inventory = (opening stock + closing stock) ÷ 2.

Q:: Formula: raw material inventory turnover ratio?
A:: Raw material consumed ÷ average raw material stock.

Q:: Formula: average collection period, two ways?
A:: Average accounts receivable ÷ average daily credit sales; or 360 days (12 months, 52 weeks) ÷ receivables turnover ratio.

Q:: Formula: payables turnover ratio?
A:: Annual net credit purchases ÷ average accounts payable.

Q:: What does a very high working capital turnover ratio indicate, per ICAI?
A:: That the company needs to raise additional working capital for future needs.

Q:: What differentiates cost of goods sold from cost of sales?
A:: Only selling and distribution expenses; in their absence COGS equals cost of sales.

Q:: Why may a firm with old plant show a higher fixed assets turnover?
A:: Its plant stands at a lower written-down value, so the same sales are divided by a smaller fixed asset figure.

Q:: How are purchases found from a trading account?
A:: Purchases = cost of goods sold + closing stock − opening stock.

Q:: What does a low debtors' turnover ratio reflect?
A:: Liberal credit terms granted to customers (a high ratio shows rapid collection).
