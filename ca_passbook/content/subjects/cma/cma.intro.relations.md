## In plain words

### Two honest people, two different numbers

At Tapri's board meeting, Dev and Ritu present the delivery segment for 2025-26.

> **Dev:** The delivery segment earned ₹31.20 lakh. Audited, in the annual report, page 112.
> **Ritu:** And the Nagpur zone lost money on every order. Per unit, please: the annual report cannot show you that.
> **Kesar:** So which of you is lying?
> **Dev:** Neither. The present value of this argument is zero.

Both are right, because they keep different accounts for different jobs. **Financial accounting** tells shareholders, lenders and the government how the whole company (or a whole segment) performed, from historical records, usually once a year, in a set format. **Cost accounting** finds the cost of each **cost object**, a café, a cup, a delivery run, for Tapri's own managers, using actual costs and costs fixed in advance (standards), whenever a report is needed and in whatever layout helps.

**Management accounting** is wider still. It takes cost accounts, financial accounts, statistics and other tools, adds qualitative information (how strong is BrewBucks' brand?), and looks forward, to help managers plan and decide. And under all three sits **financial management**: the decisions about where the money comes from, where it is invested and how much goes back to shareholders. Cost and management accounting is an application of it, for decision making.

::: analogy The health check-up, the fitness tracker and the coach
Financial accounting is your annual health check-up: one report on the whole body, in a standard format, for anyone who needs to see it. Cost accounting is the fitness tracker on each arm and leg: it shows which knee is weak, every day, in whatever screen you choose. Management accounting is the coach who reads both, adds your diet and your rival's training plan, and draws up next season. Financial management is deciding whether the savings go into a gym membership or a bicycle.
:::

::: key
Financial accounting reports the financial performance of the entity, as a whole or by segment, to outsiders, from historical data, usually yearly, in a set format. Cost accounting ascertains the cost of each cost object for cost control and decisions, from historical and predetermined costs, as and when required. Management accounting is wider and forward-looking; all three serve financial management's decisions.
:::

## How it works

"Cost and Management Accounting as a discipline is interrelated and dependent on other disciplines of accounting" (SM 1.8). The Study Material compares it with three of them.

### 1. Cost accounting and management accounting (SM 1.8–1.9)

The two terms are often used synonymously, but there are a few differences. Management accounting is ==an open-ended discipline which enables managers to take informed decisions==, and it takes inputs from cost accounts, financial accounts, statistical and operation management tools. In practice, cost accounting is concerned with accumulating and allocating costs to different cost objects, while management accounting is concerned with providing information to internal users for decision making (SM 1.11).

::: compare Difference between Cost Accounting and Management Accounting (SM 1.8–1.9)
| Basis | Cost Accounting | Management Accounting |
|---|---|---|
| (i) Nature | Records the quantitative aspect only | Records both qualitative and quantitative aspects |
| (ii) Objective | Records the cost of producing a product and providing a service | Provides information to management for planning and co-ordination |
| (iii) Area | Deals only with cost ascertainment | Wider in scope: includes financial accounting, budgeting, taxation, planning etc. |
| (iv) Recording of data | Uses both past and present figures | Focused on the projection of figures for the future |
| (v) Development | Related to the industrial revolution | Related to the needs of the modern business world |
| (vi) Rules and regulation | Follows certain principles and procedures for recording the costs of different products | Does not follow any specific rules and regulations |
:::

### 2. Cost accounting and financial accounting (SM 1.9–1.10)

"Cost accounting accumulates and ascertains costs for goods sold and inventories. It provides inputs to record costs in financial accounting system" (SM 1.9). So the cost accounts feed the financial accounts: the closing inventory and the cost of goods sold in Tapri's annual report start in Ritu's cost records.

::: compare Difference between Financial Accounting and Cost Accounting (SM 1.9–1.10)
| Basis | Financial Accounting | Cost Accounting |
|---|---|---|
| (i) Objective | Provides information about the financial performance of an entity | Ascertainment of cost for the purpose of cost control and decision making |
| (ii) Nature | Classifies, records, presents and interprets transactions in monetary terms | Classifies, records, presents and interprets costs in a significant manner |
| (iii) Recording of data | Records historical data | Uses both historical and pre-determined costs |
| (iv) Users of information | Shareholders, creditors, financial analysts, government and its agencies etc. | Generally internal management; sometimes regulatory authorities also |
| (v) Analysis of cost and profit | Shows profit or loss of the organisation segment-wise or as a whole | Gives cost details for each cost object: product, process, job, operation, contract etc. |
| (vi) Time period | Financial statements prepared usually for a year | Reports and statements prepared as and when required |
| (vii) Presentation of information | A set format is used | In general, no set format is followed |
:::

## Worked examples

::::: example The cafés segment: one profit, three cafés
Tapri's financial statements for 2025-26 report a profit of ₹26,40,000 for the cafés segment (three cafés). Ritu's cost records for the same year show:

| Café | Revenue (₹) | Total cost (₹) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Indore | 96,00,000 | 72,00,000 |
| Pune | 72,00,000 | 61,20,000 |
| Bhopal | 48,00,000 | 56,40,000 |

**Required:**

(i) Calculate the profit or loss of each café, and check that the cost records agree with the segment profit.

(ii) Using the bases on which ICAI distinguishes financial accounting from cost accounting, explain why the board needs both sets of information.

**(i) Café-wise profit**

| Café | Revenue (₹) | Total cost (₹) | Profit / (Loss) (₹) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Indore | 96,00,000 | 72,00,000 | 24,00,000 |

| **Total** | **2,16,00,000** | **1,89,60,000** | **26,40,000** |

The total agrees with the segment profit of ₹26,40,000 in the financial statements. Here the two agree; in general the profit shown by the cost accounts and by the financial accounts may differ (purely financial items, notional costs, different treatment, stock valuation), which is why they are reconciled ([[cma.sys.recon|reconciling cost and financial profit]]).

**(ii) Why the board needs both**

| Basis | Financial accounting (the segment figure) | Cost accounting (Ritu's records) |
|---|---|---|
| Objective | Financial performance of the entity: the segment earned ₹26,40,000 | Cost for control and decisions: why Bhopal loses, what to do |
| Recording of data | Historical data for the year | Historical and pre-determined (standard) costs per cup |
| Users | Shareholders, creditors, analysts, government | Internal management: the board and café managers |
| Analysis of cost and profit | Profit segment-wise or as a whole | Cost details for each cost object: each café, each cup |
| Time period | Usually a year | As and when required: monthly |
| Presentation | Set format | No set format: café-wise, cup-wise |

The financial statements tell the shareholders that the segment is profitable; only the cost records tell the board that Bhopal needs action. Neither replaces the other.

:::: example Two requests, two disciplines
In the same week Ritu receives two requests:

1. From the Pune café manager: the cost per kulhad cup for September, built up from milk, tea, kulhad, gas and staff, and compared with its standard.
2. From Ananya Rao, Tapri's head of strategy: a five-year plan for entering twenty new cities, with projected sales, budgets and tax implications, and a note on how strongly customers in each city prefer BrewBucks' brand.

**Required:** With reference to the two requests, explain the differences between cost accounting and management accounting.

::: answer Model answer
Though cost accounting and management accounting are used synonymously, there are a few differences (SM 1.8). The first request is cost accounting; the second is management accounting.

| Basis | Cost accounting (request 1) | Management accounting (request 2) |
|---|---|---|
| (i) Nature | Records the quantitative aspect only: rupees per cup | Records both qualitative and quantitative aspects: projected sales and customer preference for BrewBucks' brand |
| (ii) Objective | Records the cost of providing a service: one cup | Provides information to management for planning and co-ordination: the expansion plan |
| (iii) Area | Deals only with cost ascertainment | Wider: budgeting, taxation and planning are all in the request |
| (iv) Recording of data | Uses past and present figures: September's actual cost | Focused on projecting figures for the future: five years ahead |
| (v) Development | Related to the industrial revolution | Related to the needs of the modern business world |
| (vi) Rules and regulation | Follows certain principles and procedures for recording costs | Follows no specific rules and regulations |

Management accounting will itself use the cost accounting figures, along with financial accounts and statistical tools, as its inputs.

## Under the hood

### The view from Paper 6A

The Financial Management Study Material describes the same relationship from its side: accounting is "a necessary input into the financial management function"; the financial manager uses accounting data for decisions; and "financial management begins where accounting ends" (FM Study Material 1.21–1.23). It adds a difference in the treatment of funds: accounting measures funds on the accrual principle, while financial management looks at cash flows, because a firm can be profitable in the accounting sense and still fail to meet its obligations. Financial management's three decisions (investment, financing and dividend) are in [[fm.intro.role|the finance function]].

### Why the two sets of books must be reconciled

Some items appear only in the financial accounts (purely financial expenses and incomes, such as interest on loans or dividends received), some only in the cost accounts (notional expenses, such as rent in lieu of owned premises), some are treated differently, and stock is valued on different bases (SM 7.31–7.32). So the two profits differ, and when the books are kept separately a reconciliation statement is needed, one of the three limitations of cost accounting ([[cma.intro.install|limitations of cost accounting]]). When the accounts are integrated, the reconciliation can be avoided (SM 7.33; [[cma.sys.ledgers|non-integrated and integrated systems]], [[cma.sys.recon|reconciling cost and financial profit]]).

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: "Management accounting is cost accounting with a bigger salary"
Treating the two as the same and writing one table of cost vs financial accounting for a cost vs management accounting question. **Fix:** management accounting records qualitative as well as quantitative information, is wider in area (financial accounting, budgeting, taxation, planning), projects the future and follows no specific rules. Six bases, and none of them is "users" or "time period".
:::

::: mistake Predetermined costs in the financial accounts
Writing that financial accounting uses predetermined costs, or that cost accounting records only historical data. **Fix:** financial accounting records historical data; cost accounting uses both historical and pre-determined costs (standards and budgets are part of it).
:::

::: mistake The yearly cost report in a statutory format
Saying cost reports are prepared annually in a prescribed format for shareholders. **Fix:** cost reports and statements are prepared as and when required, in general with no set format, generally for internal management (sometimes for regulatory authorities also).
:::

## Recap

- Cost accounting provides inputs to financial accounting (costs of goods sold and inventories); management accounting takes inputs from cost accounts, financial accounts, statistical and operation management tools.
- Cost vs management accounting, six bases: nature (quantitative / both), objective (cost of product or service / planning and co-ordination), area (cost ascertainment / wider), recording (past and present / future), development (industrial revolution / modern business), rules (follows / none).
- Financial vs cost accounting, seven bases: objective, nature, recording of data (historical / historical and pre-determined), users (outsiders / internal management), analysis (segment or whole / each cost object), time period (usually a year / as and when required), presentation (set format / no set format).
- Cost and management accounting is an application of financial management for decision making purposes.
- A segment profit can hide a loss-making cost object: only the cost records show it. (The cafés' ₹26,40,000 hid Bhopal's loss of ₹8,40,000.)
