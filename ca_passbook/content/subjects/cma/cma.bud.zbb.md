## In plain words

### The jingle nobody can hum

Budget season at Tapri. Kesar has a proposal for next year's marketing and training budget.

> **Kesar:** Last year's figure plus 10%. Simple. Sab adjust ho jayega.
> **Ritu:** Last year we paid ₹40 lakh for an FM radio jingle in 40 cities. Can anyone in this room hum it?
> **Dev:** *(after a silence)* The present value of that jingle is zero.
> **Ritu:** Then this year it starts at zero too. It gets money when it shows what the money buys.

Kesar's method is **traditional (incremental) budgeting**: start from last year's spending and argue about the increase. Ritu's is **zero-based budgeting (ZBB)**: every activity starts at zero, even one Tapri has run for years. Each activity is written up as a **decision package** (what it is, what it costs, what it delivers), the packages are judged and ranked, and the money goes down the ranking until it runs out. An activity that cannot justify itself gets nothing.

Across town, the Malwa Nagar Nigam (the municipal corporation that licenses Tapri's cafés) has a different problem. Its street-lighting budget says "electricity ₹3.60 crore, salaries ₹1.20 crore, materials ₹0.80 crore, contractors ₹1.40 crore", and no councillor can tell what the city gets for ₹7 crore. A **performance budget** presents the same money by function, programme and activity, with the output each must deliver: how many lights kept burning, how many new lights put up, and what each light costs. The money is tied to results, and somebody is answerable for each result.

::: analogy Three ways to pack for a trip
The incremental packer opens last trip's suitcase, leaves everything in and adds 10% more. The zero-based packer starts with an empty suitcase: every item must argue its way in, most important first, until the bag is full, and the snow boots stay home for a trip to Goa. The performance packer labels each item with the job it will do: "umbrella: dry on three rainy days".
:::

::: key
ZBB: every activity, old or new, must justify its cost from the first rupee; without justification the budget allowance is zero, and justified decision packages are ranked and funded in order of priority. Performance budgeting: the budget is presented in terms of functions, programmes and activities, linking the money (inputs) to the results expected (outputs), with responsibility fixed for those results.
:::

## How it works

### Zero-based budgeting: the definition ICAI marks

::: definition Zero-based budgeting (ZBB)
A method of budgeting which requires each cost element to be specifically justified, though the activities to which the budget relates are not being undertaken for the first time. The cost of each activity has to be justified and ==without justification, the budget allowance is zero==. (SM 15.59)
:::

ZBB differs from the conventional system because it starts from scratch, not from trends or historical levels of expenditure. In the customary system, last year's figures are accepted as they are, or cut back, or increases are granted. ZBB starts from the premise that next period's budget is zero so long as the demand for a function, process, project or activity is not justified for each rupee, from the first rupee spent (SM 15.59). Its other features, in the Study Material's order:

- It is an **emergent form of budgeting** that arises to overcome the limitations of incremental (traditional) budgeting.
- It is an **activity-based budgeting system**: budgets are prepared for each activity rather than for each functional department. Justification in the form of cost-benefit for the activity is required; the activities are then evaluated and prioritised by management on factors like **synchronisation with organisational objectives, availability of funds and regulatory requirement**.
- It suits **corporate and non-corporate entities** alike. Government departments, local bodies and not-for-profit organisations need to justify the benefits of spending on social programmes such as the mid-day meal, street lights or drinking water.
- In a company it is **best suited to discretionary costs**: research and development, training programmes, advertisement.

1. **Identification and description of decision packages.** Decision packages are the programmes or activities on which a decision is required. Each is described for its technical specifications, its financial impact in the form of a cost-benefit analysis, and other issues such as environmental, regulatory and social ones.
2. **Evaluation of decision packages.** Each package is evaluated against factors like synchronisation with organisational objectives, availability of funds and regulatory requirement.
3. **Ranking (prioritisation) of decision packages.** The evaluated packages are ranked by the priority of the activities. Because of this feature ZBB is also known as **priority-based budgeting**.
4. **Allocation of resources.** Resources are allocated to the ranked packages. Budgets are prepared as if for the first time, without reference to previous budgets.

### Why bother: the advantages

The advantages of ZBB, in ICAI's order (SM 15.60–15.61):

1. It provides a **systematic approach** for evaluating different activities and ranking them in order of preference for the allocation of scarce resources.
2. It ensures that the functions undertaken by the organisation are **critical for the achievement of its objectives** and are being performed in the best possible way.
3. It gives management the opportunity to allocate resources only after a **thorough cost-benefit analysis**, so arbitrary cuts and enhancements are avoided.
4. Areas of **wasteful expenditure** can be easily identified and eliminated.
5. Departmental budgets are **closely linked with corporate objectives**.
6. It can be used to introduce and implement **management by objectives**, so it serves the objectives of traditional budgeting and a variety of other purposes too.

The Study Material's separate list of why "zero-based budgeting is superior to traditional budgeting" (SM 15.61) repeats these points in short form: systematic evaluation, critical functions, cost-benefit allocation, wasteful expenditure eliminated with departmental budgets linked to corporate objectives, and management by objectives.

### ZBB against traditional budgeting

::: compare
| Point | Traditional budgeting | Zero-based budgeting |
|---|---|---|
| Orientation | Accounting oriented; stress on the previous level of expenditure | Decision oriented and rational; all programmes, old and new, compete for scarce resources |
| Starting point | Past spending first, then demands for inflation and new programmes | Only the decision packages that enjoy priority over others |
| Padding | Some managers inflate requests so that after the cuts they still get what they want | Rational analysis of proposals; inflated requests are likely to be caught and exposed; approval only for result-oriented packages |
| Clarity | Not as clear and responsive | Clearer and more responsive |
| Who justifies | Top management decides why an amount is spent on a decision unit | The responsibility shifts to the manager of the decision unit |
| Approach | Routine | Straightforward; spotlights the packages with priority |
:::

(SM 15.61–15.62)

### The catch: limitations

ZBB has two limitations in the Study Material (SM 15.62):

1. Creating the decision packages and ranking them has to be done on the basis of new data, a process that is **very tedious** for management.
2. The activities selected for ZBB are based on the traditional functional departments, so the scheme **may not be implemented properly**.

The first is why ZBB is never "the least time-consuming and least costly approach to budgeting".

### Performance budgeting: money tied to results

::: definition Performance budgeting (PB)
Performance budgeting involves evaluation of the performance of an organisation in the context of both specific as well as overall objectives of the organisation. A performance budget is one which presents the purposes and objectives for which funds are required, the costs of the programmes proposed for achieving those objectives, and quantitative data measuring the accomplishments and work performed under each programme. (SM 15.62–15.63)
:::

What follows from it (SM 15.63, 15.71):

- It needs complete clarity about both the short-term and the long-term objectives of the organisation.
- The responsibility of the various levels of management is **predetermined in terms of results** expected from them and the authority vested in them. PB fixes the responsibility of each executive and appraises performance continuously, so it is considered **synonymous with responsibility accounting**.
- It provides ==a meaningful relationship between estimated inputs and expected outputs== as an integral part of the budgeting system.
- It is a technique of presenting budgets for costs and revenues **in terms of functions, programmes and activities**, correlating the physical and the financial aspects of the items in the budget.

**Traditional budgeting against performance budgeting** (SM 15.63):

| Point | Traditional budgeting | Performance budgeting |
|---|---|---|
| Emphasis | The financial aspect, more than the physical aspect or performance | A relationship between inputs and outputs |
| Built around | Objects or items of expenditure: salaries, stores and materials, rates, rents and taxes | Functions, the programmes that discharge them, and the activities those programmes involve |

### The ARC's sequence: function, programme, activity, project

According to the Administrative Reforms Commission (ARC), the basic steps in PB are (SM 15.63–15.64):

1. Establishing a meaningful functional, programme and activity classification of government operations.
2. Bringing the system of accounting and financial management in line with this classification.
3. Evolving suitable norms, yardsticks, work units of performance and unit costs, wherever possible, under each programme and activity, for their reporting and evaluation.

## Worked examples

:::: example Kesar's plus-ten-per-cent budget
Tapri's marketing, training and product-development spending was ₹1.36 crore in 2025-26, spread over a radio jingle, digital ads, staff training and two new-product trials. For 2026-27 Kesar proposes "last year's figure plus 10% for every head". Ritu proposes zero-based budgeting instead. The board asks her for a note.

(a) Explain zero-based budgeting, and how each of its stages would deal with these costs.

(b) State whether ZBB suits these costs, giving reasons.

(c) State one limitation the board should expect.

::: answer Model answer
**(a) Zero-based budgeting.** Zero-based budgeting is a method of budgeting which requires each cost element to be specifically justified, though the activities to which the budget relates are not being undertaken for the first time. The cost of each activity has to be justified; without justification, the budget allowance is zero. Unlike the traditional (incremental) system, in which last year's figures are accepted as they are, cut back or increased, ZBB starts from scratch, not from trends or historical levels of expenditure. It is an activity-based budgeting system: budgets are prepared for each activity rather than for each functional department, with justification in the form of cost-benefit, and the activities are evaluated and prioritised on factors like synchronisation with organisational objectives, availability of funds and regulatory requirement.

ZBB involves the following stages, applied here to Tapri's costs:

1. **Identification and description of decision packages**: the programmes or activities on which a decision is required, described for technical specifications, financial impact (cost-benefit analysis) and other issues such as environmental, regulatory and social ones. The jingle, the digital ads, each training programme and each product trial becomes a separate package stating what it is, what it costs and what it is expected to deliver.
2. **Evaluation of decision packages** against synchronisation with organisational objectives, availability of funds, regulatory requirement, etc. A training programme that the food licences require would pass on regulatory grounds; the jingle must show a benefit for its cost.
3. **Ranking (prioritisation) of decision packages** by the priority of the activities; hence ZBB is also called priority-based budgeting.
4. **Allocation of resources** to the ranked packages, the budget being prepared as if for the first time, without reference to previous budgets: the ₹1.36 crore of 2025-26 is not the starting point, and packages below the cut-off receive nothing.

**(b) Suitability.** Marketing, training and product development are discretionary costs: their level is decided by management, not by the volume of output. In a corporate entity ZBB is best suited to discretionary costs like research and development, training programmes and advertisement. Kesar's method would carry every past activity forward, including ones that no longer serve Tapri's objectives (such as the radio jingle), and add 10% to each. ZBB would require each activity to justify its cost and would fund only the ranked packages, so wasteful expenditure is identified and eliminated and the budget is linked to corporate objectives. ZBB is therefore suitable.

**(c) Limitation.** Creating the decision packages and ranking them has to be done on new data each time, which is very tedious for management. (Alternatively: the activities selected for ZBB are based on the traditional functional departments, so the scheme may not be implemented properly.)
:::

:::: example Malwa Nagar Nigam's street lights
The Malwa Nagar Nigam's traditional budget for street lighting in 2026-27 is:

| Item of expenditure | Amount (₹ lakh) |
|---|---:|
| Electricity charges | 360 |
| Salaries and wages | 120 |
| Materials and spares | 80 |
| Contract charges | 140 |
| **Total** | **700** |

The city has 30,000 street lights. The salaries and ₹30 lakh of the materials maintain them; the electricity lights them; ₹50 lakh of materials and all the contract charges are for 2,000 new LED lights in new colonies.

**Required:**

(a) Recast the budget as a performance budget.

(b) State how it differs from the traditional budget.

(c) State what the Nigam must ensure to adopt performance budgeting.

::: answer Model answer
**(a) Performance budget for street lighting, 2026-27**

Function: safe movement on the city's streets at night. Programme: street lighting.

| Activity or project | Physical target | Cost (₹ lakh) | Unit cost (₹) |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Energy for existing lights | 30,000 lights lit | 360 | 1,200 a light |
| Maintenance of existing lights | 30,000 lights kept working | 150 | 500 a light |
| New LED lights (project) | 2,000 lights installed | 190 | 9,500 a light |
| **Total** | | **700** | |

Working: maintenance = salaries ₹120 lakh + materials ₹30 lakh = ₹150 lakh; new lights = materials ₹50 lakh + contract charges ₹140 lakh = ₹190 lakh. Unit costs: ₹360 lakh ÷ 30,000; ₹150 lakh ÷ 30,000; ₹190 lakh ÷ 2,000. Performance indicators could include the share of lights found working at night inspections and the time taken to repair a reported fault.

**(b) Differences.** The traditional budget gives more emphasis to the financial aspect than to the physical aspect or performance; the performance budget establishes a relationship between inputs and outputs, showing what each lakh is to achieve. The traditional budget is built around objects or items of expenditure (electricity, salaries, materials, contract charges); the performance budget around functions, the programmes that discharge them and the activities those programmes involve.

**(c) For an enterprise to adopt performance budgeting, it is imperative that:**

1. the objectives of the enterprise are spelt out in concrete terms;
2. the objectives are translated into specific functions, programmes, activities and tasks for the different levels of management, within the realities of fiscal constraints;
3. realistic and acceptable norms, yardsticks or standards and performance indicators are evolved and expressed in quantifiable physical units;
4. a style of management based upon a decentralised responsibility structure is adopted; and
5. an accounting and reporting system is developed to facilitate monitoring, analysis and review of actual performance in relation to budgets.

## Under the hood

### Performance reporting: what the reports must pass

The Study Material ends its performance budgeting section with performance reporting (SM 15.64–15.67). Preparing reports for control and planning is a major part of the management accountant's job, and six considerations decide their drawing up and scope:

| Consideration | The question it asks |
|---|---|
| Significance | Are the facts reliable? Do they call for action or show its effect? Is it material enough? |
| Timeliness | How late can the information be and still be of use? How often is it needed? |
| Accuracy | How small must an inaccuracy be not to alter the significance? |
| Appropriateness | Is the recipient the right person to act? Is supporting information needed? |
| Discrimination | Will anything be lost by omitting an item? Is suppressing it acceptable? |
| Presentation | Is the report clear and unbiased, and its form suited to the subject and the recipient? |

**Reports submitted regularly**, by level:

- **Top management** (board and financial managers): balance sheet; profit and loss statement; position of stocks; disposition of funds or working capital; capital expenditure and forward commitments with progress of projects in hand; cash-flow statements; sales, production and other statistics.
- **Sales management**: actual against budgeted sales by products, territories, salesmen and customers; standard profit and loss by product (for fixing selling prices and concentrating on the most profitable products); selling expenses against budget and sales value; bad debts and slow accounts; status reports on new or doubtful customers.
- **Production management**: to the buyer, price variations on purchases by commodity; to the foreman, operational efficiency of operators, labour utilisation and lost time, indirect shop expenses against standard, and scrap; to the works manager, departmental and general works operating statements, plant utilisation, departmental scrap and material usage.
- **Special reports**, at the request of management or on the management accountant's initiative: taxation legislation and its effect on profits; earning capacity of a new project; break-even analysis; replacement of capital equipment; special pricing; make or buy; the surplus available for bonus.

### ZBB and activity-based budgeting

Both budget by activity rather than by department; ZBB's point is that each activity justifies itself from zero. The activity-based version, where each activity's resources follow its planned workload, is in [[cma.abc.abm|activity based management and budgeting]]. The budget committee, the budget manual and the steps of budgetary control into which either technique fits are in [[cma.bud.basics|budgets and budgetary control]].

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: "zero-based means a zero budget"
ZBB does not cut spending to zero. It starts each activity's allowance at zero and funds every package that is justified and ranked within the funds available. **Fix:** "zero" is the starting point of the argument, not the result.
:::

::: mistake ZBB "for new activities only"
Writing that ZBB applies to activities being undertaken for the first time. **Fix:** the definition says the opposite: each cost element is justified **though** the activities are **not** being undertaken for the first time. Continuing activities are the whole point.
:::

::: mistake Calling ZBB quick and cheap
**Fix:** creating decision packages and ranking them on new data is very tedious for management: that is ICAI's first limitation, and the false option in ICAI's own MCQ.
:::

::: mistake Performance budget, performance report, flexible budget
Answering "performance budgeting" with a report comparing actual and budget, or with a budget at several levels of activity. **Fix:** a performance budget presents funds by functions, programmes and activities with the physical results each must achieve, and fixes responsibility for those results.
:::

## Recap

- ZBB (SM 15.59): each cost element specifically justified, though the activities are not being undertaken for the first time; without justification the budget allowance is zero.
- Starts from scratch, not from trends; activity-based; cost-benefit justification; prioritised on objectives, funds and regulatory requirement.
- Suits corporate and non-corporate entities (mid-day meal, street lights, drinking water); in companies, discretionary costs (R&D, training, advertisement).
- Stages: identify and describe decision packages, evaluate, rank (hence priority-based budgeting), allocate resources.
- Six advantages: systematic evaluation, critical functions, cost-benefit allocation, waste eliminated, departmental budgets linked to corporate objectives, management by objectives. Two limitations: tedious, may not be implemented properly.
- Performance budgeting: funds presented by functions, programmes and activities with quantitative data on accomplishments; links inputs to outputs; synonymous with responsibility accounting.
- ARC: function, programme, activity, project; PB is still annual but output-oriented. To adopt it: concrete objectives, translated into functions to tasks, physical norms and indicators, decentralised responsibility, a reporting system.
- The jingle gets money when it shows what the money buys.

## Flashcards

Q:: ICAI's definition of zero-based budgeting?
A:: A method of budgeting which requires each cost element to be specifically justified, though the activities to which the budget relates are not being undertaken for the first time; without justification, the budget allowance is zero.

Q:: The four stages of ZBB, in order?
A:: Identification and description of decision packages; evaluation of decision packages; ranking (prioritisation) of decision packages; allocation of resources.

Q:: What are decision packages in ZBB?
A:: The programmes or activities for which a decision is required, described for technical specifications, financial impact (cost-benefit analysis) and other issues such as environmental, regulatory and social.

Q:: On what factors are ZBB decision packages evaluated?
A:: Synchronisation with organisational objectives, availability of funds, regulatory requirement, etc.

Q:: Why is ZBB also called priority-based budgeting?
A:: Because the evaluated decision packages are ranked by the priority of the activities before resources are allocated.

Q:: For which costs is ZBB best suited in a company?
A:: Discretionary costs such as research and development, training programmes and advertisement.

Q:: ICAI's two limitations of ZBB?
A:: Creating and ranking decision packages on new data is very tedious for management; the activities selected are based on traditional functional departments, so the scheme may not be implemented properly.

Q:: Traditional budgeting versus ZBB: who justifies the spending?
A:: In traditional budgeting top management decides why an amount is spent on a decision unit; in ZBB the responsibility shifts to the manager of the decision unit.

Q:: What does a performance budget present?
A:: The purposes and objectives for which funds are required, the costs of the programmes proposed to achieve them, and quantitative data measuring the accomplishments and work performed under each programme.

Q:: Performance budgeting is considered synonymous with what, and why?
A:: Responsibility accounting: it fixes the responsibility of each executive in terms of results and appraises performance continuously.

Q:: The ARC's three basic steps in performance budgeting?
A:: A meaningful functional, programme and activity classification of government operations; accounting and financial management brought in line with it; norms, yardsticks, work units and unit costs evolved under each programme and activity for reporting and evaluation.

Q:: Five things an enterprise must ensure to adopt performance budgeting?
A:: Objectives spelt out in concrete terms; translated into functions, programmes, activities and tasks within fiscal constraints; realistic norms and performance indicators in quantifiable physical units; a decentralised responsibility structure; an accounting and reporting system to monitor and review actual performance against budgets.
