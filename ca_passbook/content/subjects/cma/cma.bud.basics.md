## In plain words

### Two crore cups, said on a Monday

At the Monday meeting Kesar announces next year's plan.

> **Kesar:** Next year we sell two crore cups, open fifteen cafés and launch a Holi thandai. Sab adjust ho jayega.
> **Ritu:** Per unit, please. How many cups in each city, each quarter? How much milk, how many staff, how many ovens?
> **Dev:** And who pays for fifteen cafés before the first cup is sold?
> **Kesar:** Details. You people do the details.

Ritu's "details" are the budget. A **budget** is a plan turned into numbers (cups, litres, hours, rupees) for a definite future period, written down and approved before that period starts. Kesar's sentence is a wish; Ritu's spreadsheet, once the board approves it, is a commitment.

Then the year starts. Every month Ritu puts the actual figures next to the budget, finds where they differ and asks the person responsible what will be done about it: speed up, cut back, or, if the world has changed, revise the plan. That continuous comparison, with somebody answerable for each line, is **budgetary control**.

A system that many people feed needs a rulebook: who prepares which budget, on which form, by which date, and who approves it. That rulebook is the **budget manual**, and the people who run the system are the **budget committee** (the heads of the functions) acting through a **budget officer**.

One more thing comes before any of the numbers. Ritu checks the dairy contract: Tapri can get milk for only 1.6 crore cups. However loudly Kesar says "two crore", milk is the factor that limits everything else: sales, staff, cups, ovens. A factor that sets a limit to the total activity is the **key factor**, and the budget has to be built around it.

::: analogy The family wedding
A wedding has a budget: guests, plates, decorations and rupees, fixed before the date. It has budgetary control: the aunt with a notebook who checks every bill against the plan and asks who ordered the extra band. It has a budget manual: the file that says who books the caterer, who approves payments and by when the guest list is closed. And it has a key factor: the hall seats 400. However many relatives Kesar wants to invite, the plates, the sweets and the return gifts are all set by 400.
:::

::: key
A budget is a plan in numbers for a definite future period, approved in advance; budgetary control is the continuous comparison of actual results with it, with responsibility fixed for the differences. The key factor sets the ceiling for the whole budget, so it is identified before the budgets are built.
:::

## How it works

### Budget, forecast and budgeting

CIMA defines a **budget** as "A financial and/or quantitative statement, prepared and approved prior to a defined period of time of the policy to be pursued during that period for the purpose of attaining a given objective. It may include income, expenditure and employment of capital" (SM 15.2). It is a blue-print of the projected plan of action expressed in quantitative terms for a specified period of time.

A **forecast** is an assessment of probable future events. A budget is built on forecasts, but it is a commitment or a target which the management seeks to attain: ==a forecast denotes some degree of flexibility while a budget denotes a definite target== (SM 15.3). **Budgeting** is the process of designing, implementing and operating budgets.

The **essential characteristics of a budget**, in ICAI's order (SM 15.3–15.4):

1. A budget is concerned for a definite future period.
2. A budget is a written document.
3. A budget is a detailed plan of all the economic activities of a business.
4. All the departments of a business unit should co-operate for the preparation of a business budget.
5. Budget is a means to achieve business objectives and it is not an end in itself.
6. Budget needs to be updated, corrected and controlled every time circumstances change; therefore, it is a continuous process.
7. Budget helps in planning, coordination and control.
8. Different types of budgets are prepared by industries according to business requirements.
9. A budget acts as a business barometer.
10. Budget is usually prepared in the light of past experiences.
11. Budget is a constant endeavour of the Management.

The **objectives of budgeting** are three (SM 15.5–15.7): **planning** (targets in line with the organisation's objectives; planning precedes the budget), **direction and co-ordination** (of the budgetary units, the **responsibility centres**, each led by a manager answerable for its performance) and **controlling** (actual compared with targets, the feedback used to fine-tune). The main objective is to help achieve the overall objective of the organisation.

The **essential steps for preparing a budget** (SM 15.4):

1. Organisational structure must be clearly defined and responsibility assigned to identifiable units within the organisation.
2. Setting of clear objectives and reasonable targets, in consonance with the long-term plan of the organisation.
3. Objectives and responsibility should be clearly stated and communicated to the management or person responsible.
4. Budgets are prepared for the future periods based on the expected course of actions.
5. Budgets are updated for events not kept in mind while establishing them; hence budgets should be flexible enough for mid-term revision.
6. The entire organisation must be committed to the preparation and implementing of budgeting.
7. Budgets should be quantifiable, and the master budget should be broken down into various functional budgets.
8. Budgets should be monitored periodically; variances of the actual outcomes analysed and responsibility fixed.
9. Budgetary performance needs to be linked effectively to the reward system.

### Budgetary control: the comparison that never stops

::: definition Budgetary control
CIMA: "the establishment of budgets relating to the responsibilities of executives to the requirements of a policy and the continuous comparison of actual with budgeted results, either to secure by individual action, the objective of that policy or to provide a basis for its revision." It is the system of management control and accounting in which all the operations are forecasted and planned in advance to the extent possible and the actual results compared with the forecasted and planned results. (SM 15.7)
:::

Budgetary control **involves** (SM 15.7): (1) establishment of budgets; (2) continuous comparison of actuals with budgets for achievement of targets; (3) revision of budgets after considering the changes in the circumstances; (4) fixation of the responsibility for failure to achieve the budget targets.

For it to work, the firm needs a proper basis of measurement, that is, a system of **standard costing** ([[cma.std.setting|standards and standard costing]]), and an organisation in which every line of authority and responsibility is defined, "from the Managing Director down to the Shop Foreman" (SM 15.9). Its objectives, the steps for establishing it, its advantages and its limitations are asked as 4- and 5-mark lists (Exam angle): the objectives, advantages and limitations are set out point by point in the Answer frame, the five steps in the second worked example.

Whether the comparison happens after the period (feedback control, ex-post corrective) or continuously during it (feedforward control, ex-ante preventive), and how budgets motivate the people who must meet them, are in [[cma.bud.ratios|control ratios, motivation, feedback and feed-forward]].

### Who runs it: the budget committee and the budget officer

The **budget committee** is a group of representatives of the various functions. Because a change in one function's target changes the others', the targets are discussed until a mutually agreed programme is decided: this is **coordination in budget-making** (SM 15.10). The **Chief Executive** is ultimately responsible for the budget programme, but a large part of the supervisory responsibility is better delegated to a **Budget Officer**, who should know the technical side of the business and report to the president or CEO. The responsibility for introducing and implementing the system rests with the Budget Committee acting through the Budget Officer.

The **responsibilities of the Budget Committee and Budget Officer** (SM 15.11):

1. Assist in the preparation of the separate budget for various departments, coordinating the work of the accounts department (which normally compiles the budgets) with the functional departments like Sales, Production and Plant maintenance.
2. Forward the budget to the individual department heads who are responsible to implement it, and guide them in overcoming practical difficulties in its working.
3. Prepare the periodical budget reports for circulation to the individuals concerned.
4. Follow-up action to be taken on the budget reports.
5. Prepare an overall budget working report for discussion at the Budget Committee meetings, and ensure follow-up on the lines of action suggested by the Committee.
6. Prepare periodical reports for the Board meeting, comparing the budgeted Profit and Loss Account and Balance Sheet with the actual results attained.

The efficiency of the Budget Officer, and through him of the committee, is judged by the smooth working of the system and the agreement between the actual and the budgeted figures.

Use the one the question's context fits. In the chapter's own MCQ, the budget organisation is headed by the **budget director or budget controller** (SM 15.72).

### Six steps to prepare the budget, key factor second

ICAI's section on the preparation of budgets (SM 15.16–15.18):

::: steps
1. **Defining business or organisational objectives.** Written down, with the areas of control demarcated and the items of revenue and expenditure to be covered stated clearly.
2. **Identification of the key budget factor.** There are usually one or two key budget factors (sometimes more than two) which set a limit to the total activity. In India, non-availability of power sometimes does not allow production to increase in spite of heavy demand; similarly, lack of demand may limit production. Such a factor is known as the **key factor**; it must be identified and its influence on production or sales estimated properly while preparing the budget.
3. **Appointment of controller or officer.** Formulating a budget usually needs a whole-time senior executive, assisted by the Budget Committee. The Budget Controller or Officer coordinates and develops the budget programmes and prepares the manual of instructions, the **budget manual**.
4. **Budget manual.** A booklet specifying the objectives of the organisation in relation to its strategy; its typical contents are the model answer to the third worked example below.
5. **Budget period.** There is no general rule: the Budget Committee decides the length. Normally a calendar year or a period co-terminus with the financial year, divided into months, quarters or periods that coincide with the trading activity of the business.
6. **Standard of activity or output.** Past statistics cannot be completely relied upon, because the past mixes good and bad factors; apply them only when similar conditions are likely to repeat. In a progressive business a year's achievement should normally exceed the earlier years': what was good in the past is only fair for the current year.
:::

Where the quantity that can be sold is the limit, sales are the **principal budget factor**, the case "in many business undertakings" (SM 15.30). Which product to favour when a factor is short is a marginal costing decision: [[cma.mc.keyfactor|limiting factor and product mix]].

::: definition Budget manual
CIMA: "A document which sets out the responsibilities of the persons engaged in, the routine of, and the forms and records required for, budgetary control." A budget manual is a collection of documents that contains key information for those involved in the planning process. (SM 15.16–15.17)
:::

"Define Budget Manual. What are the salient features of Budget Manual?" was a 5-mark question in November 2022 (old scheme); the fourteen contents (SM 15.17–15.18) are in the third worked example.

### The four families of budgets inside the master budget

The policy of a business for a defined period is represented by the **master budget**, whose detailed components are the **functional budgets**. These are broadly grouped under four heads, the **components of a budgetary control system** (SM 15.14):

| Group | What it contains | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Physical budgets** | Information in quantitative terms | Quantity of sales, quantity of production, inventories, manpower |
| **Cost budgets** | Cost information | Manufacturing, selling, administration, research and development cost |
| **Profit budgets** | The ascertainment of profit | Sales budget, profit and loss budget |
| **Financial budgets** | The financial position | Cash budget, capital expenditure budget, budgeted balance sheet |

- **Long-term budget**: covers more than a year, usually three to ten years; useful where the gestation period is long, as for makers of machinery or electricity.
- **Short-term budget**: generally one or two years, in monetary terms; used by consumer goods industries like sugar, cotton and textile.
- **Current budget**: months and weeks, relating to current activities. CIMA: a budget "established for use over a short period of time and is related to current conditions".

## Formats and formulas

*Reproduce these definitions from memory; ICAI asks for them in 1-mark slices of a 5-mark question.*

::: definition Budget centre
A section of an organisation developed for the purpose of budgetary control, and intended to facilitate formulation of various budgets with the help of the head of the department. (SM 15.70)
:::

::: definition Budget period
The period covered by a budget. There is no general rule governing its selection; the Budget Committee determines the length suitable for the business, normally a calendar year or a period co-terminus with the financial year, divided into months, quarters or periods coinciding with the trading activity of the business. (SM 15.18)
:::

::: definition Key factor
A factor which sets a limit to the total activity: usually one or two (sometimes more than two), such as non-availability of power or lack of demand. It must be identified and its influence on production or sales estimated properly while preparing the budget. (SM 15.16)
:::

::: definition Master budget
CIMA: "the summary budget, incorporating its component functional budgets, which is finally approved, adopted and employed." (SM 15.55)
:::

::: formula Budgeted activity under a key factor
$$\text{Limit} = \frac{\text{Quantity available}}{\text{Quantity per unit}}$$
$$\text{Budgeted output} = \text{the lowest of the limits}$$
Work out the limit of demand and of each resource (in units of output); the factor giving the lowest limit is the key factor. Conditions: one product, or a mix that stays fixed. With several products competing for one scarce resource, rank them by contribution per unit of that resource instead ([[cma.mc.keyfactor|limiting factor and product mix]]).
:::

## Worked examples

::::: example Power cuts at the Dewas snack plant
Tapri is budgeting its new frozen-snack plant at Dewas for 2026-27. Ritu has collected the following:

| Factor | Data for 2026-27 |
|---|---|
| Demand (sales team's estimate) | 9,60,000 packs |
| Fryers | 2 fryers × 20 hours a day × 300 days, at 75 packs an hour per fryer |
| Skilled cooks | 45 cooks × 2,400 hours a year; 6 minutes of cook time per pack |
| Potato filling (supply contract) | 2,09,000 kg; 0.22 kg per pack |
| Power (sanctioned by the state discom) | 12,60,000 kWh; 1.5 kWh per pack |

**Required:**

(i) Identify the key factor and the maximum output the budget can plan for.

(ii) State the contribution the sales budget would overstate if it were set at 9,60,000 packs.

(iii) A rented diesel generator would add 1,80,000 kWh. Identify the key factor after renting it, and suggest how the remaining gap to demand could be met.

Power sets the lowest limit: it is the **key factor**, and the budget can plan for at most **8,40,000 packs**. The sales, production, purchase and labour budgets are all built to this figure.

**(ii)** Setting sales at 9,60,000 packs plans 1,20,000 packs the plant cannot make: contribution overstated by 1,20,000 × ₹18 = **₹21,60,000**. Advertising should also be planned to the 8,40,000 packs: money should be spent on advertisement only when sufficient quantities of the product are ready for sale (SM 15.41).

**(iii)** With the generator: (12,60,000 + 1,80,000) ÷ 1.5 = 9,60,000 packs, equal to demand. The lowest limit is now the fryers at **9,00,000 packs**: removing one key factor reveals the next. The gap of 60,000 packs to demand needs two things:

1. **Fryers** (up to 9,50,000 packs): 50,000 ÷ 75 = about 667 more fryer-hours, from the Study Material's remedies for insufficient production facilities: working overtime, introducing shift working, sub-contracting or buying additional plant (SM 15.32).
2. **Potato filling** (beyond 9,50,000 packs): the contract covers only 9,50,000 packs, so filling becomes the next key factor. The last 10,000 packs need 10,000 × 0.22 = 2,200 kg more filling, from a second supplier, together with another 10,000 ÷ 75 = about 133 fryer-hours (800 in all).

:::: example The chilli that nobody budgeted at Masala Mills
Masala Mills Ltd, a spice supplier to Tapri, works without budgets. Last year its purchase manager bought a full year's red chilli at the season's peak price, while its sales team, chasing volume, ran a 20% discount scheme. Each department thought it was doing well; nobody knew the combined result until the annual accounts showed a loss. Now the bank wants to see the company's budget plans before it renews the cash credit limit. As a consultant:

(a) Identify and explain four objectives of a budgetary control system that would have helped Masala Mills, relating each to the facts.

(b) State the steps you would follow to establish the system, and what else the company needs for it to work.

::: answer Model answer
**(a) Objectives of a budgetary control system** (four of the nine, chosen for these facts):

1. **Laying down the responsibilities** of each executive and other personnel, so that everyone knows what is expected of him and how he will be judged. The purchase manager and the sales head would each have had targets for which they were answerable.
2. **Providing a basis for comparison** of actual performance with the predetermined targets and investigation of deviations of actual performance and expenses from the budgeted figures, which helps in adopting corrective measures. The excess chilli purchases and the discounts would have shown up as deviations in the monthly budget reports, not in the annual accounts.
3. **Ensuring optimum use of available resources** to maximise profit or production, subject to the limiting factors. Funds locked in a year's chilli stock would have been planned against the purchase and cash budgets.
4. **Co-ordinating various activities of the business**, centralising control and yet enabling management to decentralise responsibility and delegate authority in the overall interest of the business. Purchase and sales worked at cross purposes; budgets prepared together would have had to agree.

**(b) Steps for establishing budgetary control:**

1. Determining the objectives to be achieved over the budget period, and the policies that might be adopted to achieve them.
2. Determining the activities that should be undertaken to achieve the objectives.
3. Drawing up a plan or scheme of operation for each class of activity, in quantitative as well as monetary terms, for the budget period.
4. Laying out a system of comparison of actual performance by each person or department with the relevant budget, and determining the causes of variations.
5. Ensuring that corrective action is taken where the plan has not been achieved and, if that is not possible, revising the plan.

For the system to work, Masala Mills also needs a proper basis of measurement or standards with which to evaluate the efficiency of operations (a system of standard costing), and an organisation in which all lines of authority and responsibility are properly defined. The bank's request is also met: management which has developed well-ordered budget plans, and operates accordingly, receives greater favour from credit agencies (SM 15.12).
:::

:::: example Kesar's one-page budget manual
Kesar's draft "budget manual" for Tapri is one page long. It reads, in full:

1. The names of the fifteen functional budgets.

2. "Every department head prepares his own budget."

3. The budgeted profit and loss account for 2026-27, showing a profit of ₹42 crore.

4. "Budgets once approved are final. No revisions."

5. "Rest, sab adjust ho jayega."

Ritu has to tell the budget committee whether this is a budget manual.

**Required:** Examine each item of the draft against the meaning and contents of a budget manual, and state the contents Ritu should add.

::: answer Model answer
**Meaning.** The budget manual is a booklet specifying the objectives of an organisation in relation to its strategy. CIMA defines it as "a document which sets out the responsibilities of the persons engaged in, the routine of, and the forms and records required for, budgetary control." It is a collection of documents containing key information for those involved in the planning process.

**The draft, item by item**

| Item | Examination |
|---|---|
| 1. Names of the budgets | Not enough on its own: the manual shows, in a form of organisation chart, who is responsible for preparing each functional budget and how the budgets are interrelated |
| 2. "Every head prepares his own" | Too vague: the manual states the functions and responsibilities of each executive for both preparation and execution, and the procedure and authority for approval |
| 3. Budgeted profit and loss account | Does not belong: it is part of the master budget, an output of budgeting; the manual may state the key assumptions behind it, such as the rate of inflation |
| 4. "No revisions" | Contrary to the manual: it sets out the manner in which budgets, after acceptance and issue, may be revised or amended, with the approval of top management |
| 5. "Rest, sab adjust ho jayega" | Not a content: the manual lays down the reports, forms and records, the accounts classification and the reporting of remedial action, so that nothing is adjusted informally |

The draft is therefore not a budget manual.

**Contents Ritu should add.** A typical budget manual may include:

1. A statement regarding the objectives of the organisation and how they can be achieved through budgetary control.
2. A statement about the functions and responsibilities of each executive, both regarding preparation and execution of budgets.
3. Procedures to be followed for obtaining the necessary approval of budgets, the authority for granting approval stated in explicit terms, including whether one, two or more signatures are required on each document.
4. A form of organisation chart to show who is responsible for the preparation of each functional budget and the way in which the budgets are interrelated.
5. A timetable for the preparation of each budget.
6. The manner of scrutiny and the personnel to carry it out.
7. Reports, statements, forms and other records to be maintained.
8. The accounts classification to be employed, identical in the accounts and the budget department.
9. The reporting of the remedial action.
10. The manner in which budgets, after acceptance and issue, are to be revised or amended, on which action can be taken only with the approval of top management.
11. Preventing a "bottleneck", the late preparation of one budget holding up the preparation of all others.
12. Copies of all forms to be completed by those responsible for preparing budgets, with explanations concerning their completion.
13. A list of the organisation's account codes, with full explanations of how to use them.
14. Information concerning the key assumptions to be made by managers in their budgets, for example the rate of inflation and key exchange rates.
:::

## Answer frame

### "Discuss the objectives of a budgetary control system"

The nine points ICAI's answer gives, in order (SM 15.7–15.8); a 4-mark question takes any four with their explanation:

1. Portraying with precision the overall aims of the business and determining targets of performance for each section or department.
2. Laying down the responsibilities of each executive, so that everyone knows what is expected and how he will be judged.
3. Providing a basis for comparing actual performance with the predetermined targets and investigating deviations, which helps in adopting corrective measures.
4. Ensuring optimum use of available resources to maximise profit or production, subject to the limiting factors.
5. Co-ordinating the activities of the business, centralising control and yet enabling management to decentralise responsibility and delegate authority.
6. Engendering a spirit of careful forethought, assessment of what is possible and an attempt at it: dynamism without being reckless.
7. Providing a basis for revision of current and future policies.
8. Drawing up long range plans with a fair measure of accuracy.
9. Providing a yardstick against which actual results can be compared.

### "Advantages" and "limitations"

| Advantages (SM 15.12) | One-line explanation |
|---|---|
| 1. Efficiency | Business activities are conducted in an efficient manner |
| 2. Control on expenditure | A yardstick for measuring the performance of individuals and departments |
| 3. Finding deviations | Actual is compared with budget and deviations are communicated to management |
| 4. Effective utilisation of resources | Men, material, machinery and money are planned for |
| 5. Revision of plans | Helps review current trends and frame future policies |
| 6. Implementation of standard costing | Creates suitable conditions for a standard costing system |
| 7. Cost consciousness | Encourages cost consciousness and maximum utilisation of resources |
| 8. Credit rating | Well-ordered budget plans receive greater favour from credit agencies |

| Limitations (SM 15.13–15.14) | One-line explanation |
|---|---|
| 1. Based on estimates | Made on conditions at the time; revision is needed if they change |
| 2. Time factor | Not executed automatically; management must not expect too much in the initial period |
| 3. Co-operation required | Staff co-operation is usually not available initially; success depends on willing co-operation and teamwork |
| 4. Expensive | Needs a proper organisation structure and consumes qualified manpower and equipment |
| 5. Not a substitute for management | Only a managerial tool, to be intelligently applied |
| 6. Rigid document | Should be flexible enough to take in changes in internal and external factors |

### "Distinguish between a forecast and a budget"

| Point | Forecast | Budget |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | An assessment of probable future events | A financial or quantitative plan to be pursued over a period |
| Commitment | Denotes some degree of flexibility | A commitment, a definite target management seeks to attain |
| Order | Made first, on sales, production cost and finance | Prepared on the basis of the forecasts |
| Sales | Projects the available customer demand: reflects the environment | Shows how management intends to react to it (SM 15.31) |
| Common ground | Both relate to a defined period of time | Both relate to a defined period of time |

### Three answers to "What is a key factor?"

::: cols
**Loses marks**

"The most important factor in the budget."

Vague: no limit, no example, no consequence.
---
**Passes**

"A factor which sets a limit to the total activity, such as lack of demand or shortage of power."

The definition with an example, but not what the budget does with it.
---
**Full marks**

The definition; "usually one or two, sometimes more"; examples (power, demand); it must be identified and its influence on production or sales estimated while preparing the budget; where sales are the limit they are the principal budget factor.
:::

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: the 9,60,000-pack sales budget
Building the sales budget on demand when power allows 8,40,000 packs. Every budget downstream (production, materials, labour, cash) then plans for packs that cannot be made. **Fix:** find the limit set by each factor first; the lowest is the key factor and caps every budget.
:::

::: mistake Calling the forecast a budget
"Sales budget = what we expect to sell." **Fix:** a forecast is an assessment of probable events and stays flexible; a budget is the target management commits to and acts to achieve, built on the forecast.
:::

::: mistake The budget manual that is only a list of budgets
**Fix:** a budget manual sets out responsibilities, routines, forms and records for budgetary control (CIMA): approvals, timetable, organisation chart, account codes, revision procedure, key assumptions. The budgets themselves are not the manual.
:::

::: mistake Mixing up the three lists of steps
"Essential steps for preparing a budget" (nine, SM 15.4), "steps for establishing budgetary control" (five, SM 15.8–15.9) and "preparation of budgets" (six, SM 15.16–15.18) are different lists. **Fix:** read the question's verb and noun, then write the matching list; for "budgetary control technique", the five steps plus key factor, manual and period.
:::

= 4000

## Recap

- Budget (CIMA): a financial and/or quantitative statement, prepared and approved before a defined period, of the policy to be pursued to attain a given objective.
- Forecast = assessment of probable events (flexible); budget = commitment, a definite target.
- Budgetary control: establish budgets, compare actual with budget continuously, revise when circumstances change, fix responsibility.
- Budget committee (functional heads) acts through the budget officer; the Chief Executive is ultimately responsible; the board approves the master budget.
- Preparation: objectives, key factor, controller, budget manual, budget period, standard of activity.
- Key factor: limits the total activity (power, demand); identify it first; the lowest limit binds and can move once removed.
- Budget manual: responsibilities, routine, forms and records for budgetary control: 14 typical contents.
- Components: physical, cost, profit, financial budgets; period-wise: long-term (3 to 10 years), short-term (one or two years), current (months, weeks). (The hall seats 400.)

## Flashcards

Q:: CIMA's definition of a budget?
A:: A financial and/or quantitative statement, prepared and approved prior to a defined period of time, of the policy to be pursued during that period for the purpose of attaining a given objective. It may include income, expenditure and employment of capital.

Q:: CIMA's definition of budgetary control?
A:: The establishment of budgets relating to the responsibilities of executives to the requirements of a policy, and the continuous comparison of actual with budgeted results, either to secure by individual action the objective of that policy or to provide a basis for its revision.

Q:: Budgetary control involves which four things?
A:: Establishment of budgets; continuous comparison of actuals with budgets; revision of budgets when circumstances change; fixation of responsibility for failure to achieve the budget targets.

Q:: What is a key factor in budgeting?
A:: A factor which sets a limit to the total activity (usually one or two, sometimes more), such as non-availability of power or lack of demand; it must be identified and its influence estimated while preparing the budget.

Q:: CIMA's definition of a budget manual?
A:: A document which sets out the responsibilities of the persons engaged in, the routine of, and the forms and records required for, budgetary control.

Q:: The six steps in the preparation of budgets, in order?
A:: Defining objectives; identifying the key budget factor; appointing the controller or officer; the budget manual; the budget period; the standard of activity or output.

Q:: The five steps for establishing budgetary control?
A:: Determine objectives and policies; determine the activities; draw up a plan for each class of activity in quantitative and monetary terms; lay out a system of comparison of actual with budget and find causes of variation; ensure corrective action or revise the plan.

Q:: The four components of a budgetary control system?
A:: Physical budgets, cost budgets, profit budgets and financial budgets (for example, cash budget, capital expenditure budget, budgeted balance sheet).

Q:: Who decides the budget period, and what is it normally?
A:: The Budget Committee; normally a calendar year or a period co-terminus with the financial year, divided into months, quarters or trading periods. There is no general rule.

Q:: ICAI's six limitations of budgetary control?
A:: Based on estimates; time factor; co-operation required; expensive; not a substitute for management; rigid document.

Q:: Forecast versus budget in one line?
A:: A forecast is an assessment of probable future events and denotes flexibility; a budget is a commitment, a definite target which management seeks to attain on the basis of the forecasts.

Q:: Long-term, short-term and current budgets: their periods?
A:: Long-term: more than a year, usually three to ten years; short-term: generally one or two years; current: months and weeks, related to current conditions.
