## In plain words

### The ₹27,000 question

Tapri's Pune café sends Kesar its October bills.

> **Kesar:** ₹2,07,000 on milk this month. Is that good or bad?
> **Ritu:** Per unit, please. The café sold 20,000 cups. Our recipe card says 150 ml of milk a cup at ₹60 a litre: ₹9 a cup, ₹1,80,000 for 20,000 cups.
> **Kesar:** So we are ₹27,000 over. Who drank it?
> **Ritu:** That is exactly the question a standard is for.

Ritu's ₹9 is a **standard cost**: what a cup *should* cost, worked out in advance from a quantity (150 ml) and a price (₹60 a litre). With it, the bill splits into what 20,000 cups should have cost and a **variance** of ₹27,000 to be explained. The milk price was exactly the standard, so the extra 450 litres went into cups: the new barista's generous pour.

**Standard costing** is the routine around that idea: set standards, record actual costs, compare, investigate the differences, act. A manager cannot read every bill; with standards, only the significant variances reach the desk. That is **management by exception**.

How strict should it be? A standard for the best barista on her best day is never met; one copied from last year's average approves last year's waste.

::: analogy The map app's arrival time
The map app tells you when you should arrive: a standard. Arrive late and you ask why: a jam on the highway (not your fault) or a wrong turn (yours). The time for empty roads at 3 a.m. is an ideal standard: nobody makes it at 6 p.m. Today's time, with today's traffic, is a current standard. Your first year's time, kept unchanged to see how much worse traffic gets each year, is a basic standard.
:::

::: key
A standard cost is a planned cost, set in advance in quantities and prices, for the actual output. Standard costing compares actual cost with it, reports the variances and investigates the significant ones. The standard that controls cost best is one that good, efficient work can attain.
:::

## How it works

### Two definitions to write word for word

::: definition Standard costing
"Control technique that reports variances by comparing actual costs to pre-set standards so facilitating action through management by exception." (CIMA Official Terminology, SM 13.2)
:::

::: definition Standard cost
"The planned unit cost of the product, component or service produced in a period. The standard cost may be determined on a number of bases. The main use of standard costs is in performance measurement, control, stock valuation and in the establishment of selling prices." (CIMA Official Terminology, SM 13.2) So a standard cost is a **planned cost**, **determined on a base or number of bases**.
:::

Standards are established to evaluate the performance of a **responsibility centre**. They are also used to value inventory where actual figures are not reliably available, and to determine selling prices, particularly for quotations (SM 13.2). Standard costing is preferred for these reasons (SM 13.2–13.3):

1. **Prediction of future cost for decision making**: set after considering present conditions and future possibilities, a standard cost is the future cost for estimating a proposed project, order or activity.
2. **Provide target to be achieved**: a cost the responsibility centre should not cross; performance is measured against it and variances are reported for action.
3. **Used in budgeting and performance evaluation**: budgets are set from standard costs, so managers do not compromise quality to fulfil the budgeted quantity, and variances can be traced to the responsible department or person.
4. **Interim profit measurement and inventory valuation**: for interim reports, before the accounts are closed, standard costs are deducted from revenue to arrive at the profit.

### The process of standard costing (SM 13.5)

::: steps
1. **Setting of standards** to be achieved.
2. **Ascertainment of actual costs** (books, invoices, wage sheets).
3. **Comparison of actual cost with standard cost**: variances are determined.
4. **Investigate the reasons for variances**; evaluate performance and act.
5. **Disposition of variances**: transferred to the relevant accounts (the Costing Profit and Loss Account) as per the accounting method adopted.
:::

### Four types of standards (SM 13.3–13.4)

1. **Ideal standards**: the level of performance attainable when prices for material and labour are most favourable, when the highest output is achieved with the best equipment and layout, and when maximum efficiency in the utilisation of resources results in maximum output with minimum cost. They are criticised on three grounds: (a) being unattainable, no one would take them seriously; (b) the variances disclosed, being variances from ideal standards, would not indicate the extent to which they could have been reasonably and practically avoided; (c) there would be no logical method of disposing of these variances.
2. **Normal standards**: standards that may be achieved under normal operating conditions. Normal activity is "the number of standard hours which will produce at normal efficiency sufficient good to meet the average sales demand over a term of years". They are difficult to set because they require a degree of forecasting. Their variances are deviations from normal efficiency, normal sales volume or normal production volume; if actual performance is abnormal, large variances may result and necessitate revision of standards.
3. **Basic or bogey standards**: used only when they are likely to remain constant over a long period; a base year is chosen for comparison, as statisticians use price indices. They are set on a long-term basis, seldom revised, and well suited to businesses with a small range of products and long production runs. As they do not represent what should be attained in the present period, current standards should also be prepared. Variances are not calculated: the actual cost is expressed as a percentage of basic cost, the current cost likewise, and the two percentages are compared, then compared with those of previous periods to establish the trend.
4. **Current standards**: management's anticipation of what actual costs will be for the current period: the costs the business will incur if the anticipated prices are paid and the usage corresponds to that believed necessary to produce the planned output. Their variances represent the degree of efficiency in usage of the factors of production, variation in prices paid for materials and services, and difference in the volume of production.

The standard most effective in the control of costs represents ==an attainable level of good performance== (SM 13.52). In the Study Material's MCQs, the standard "attainable under favourable conditions" is the **theoretical standard** and the one "most suitable from cost control point of view" the **expected standard** (SM 13.55, key 13.67); its current-standards paragraph speaks of variances "arising from expected standards" (SM 13.4).

### Setting up standard costs (SM 13.5–13.11)

Standard cost is set on the basis of management's estimation, from the technical specifications of the engineering department or another expert such as the production engineer, considering historical data, the current production plan and expected future conditions. Standards are set for each element of cost (material, labour, variable and fixed overheads) and for sales quantity and value (budgeted sales), **in both physical and monetary terms**.

#### Physical standards (SM 13.6–13.9)

Standard quantities and hours, to secure economies in scale of production and to set selling prices for quotations. The industrial engineering department sets them, considering:

- the company's operating plan, i.e. budgets;
- the final output to be produced;
- material specification, in quantity and quality, provided by the engineering department;
- the proportion of materials to be used where there are multiple inputs;
- the method of production: fully automated, semi-automated or manual;
- the skill set and availability of workers;
- working conditions and internal factors;
- external factors (Labour Law, Factories Act, Government policy).

**Procedure of setting material quantity standards** (SM 13.6–13.7):

1. **Standardisation of products**: what to produce, which type, and how much, from the production plan and customers' orders.
2. **Product study**: by the engineering department or product consultants: how to produce it, the pre-requisites, which materials, market acceptance.
3. **Preparation of specification list**: types (quality), quantities, substitutes and proportions of materials, the process and conditions, allowing for expected wastage.
4. **Test runs**: sample runs under specified conditions; deviations from the specification are noted and the list updated.

**Procedure of setting labour time standards** (SM 13.7–13.8):

1. **Standardisation of product and product study**, as for material.
2. **Labour specification**: the types of labour and the labour time, based on past records, allowing for normal wastage of time.
3. **Standardisation of methods**: selection of proper machines, and the proper sequence and method of operations.
4. **Manufacturing layout**: a plan of operations for each product.
5. **Time and motion study**: the best way to do the job and the standard time an average worker takes, allowing for the learning effect.
6. **Training and trial**: workers are trained; the time in the trial run is noted.

**Overheads time or quantity standards** (SM 13.8): variable overheads may be based on direct material quantity or labour hours, generally labour time worked; fixed overhead time is based on budgeted production volume.

**Problems faced while setting physical standards** (SM 13.8–13.9):

1. A new product line needing inexperienced workers: the standard time must allow for inexperience.
2. New technology and sophisticated machines: output and efficiency cannot be estimated precisely until conditions settle.
3. Product diversification: new tools (a copper part now made in bronze) need new standard times.
4. Materials not available as per specification: the standards may not be achievable.
5. The choice of the type of standard: maximum efficiency, attainable good performance or average past performance; likewise for waste standards, unavoidable wastage only or the past average (the first worked example below).

#### Price or rate standards (SM 13.9–13.11)

Set on (a) the actual average or mean price expected during the coming period, say one year, or (b) the normal prices expected during a cycle of seasons, possibly several years.

**Material price standards** (purchasing department), if price fluctuations are small, are based on:

- (a) the stock of materials on hand and the prices at which it is held;
- (b) the prices at which orders for future deliveries have already been placed;
- (c) the minimum support price fixed by the appropriate authority;
- (d) anticipated fluctuation in price levels.

For unsystematic fluctuations:

- (a) month to month: a year's average price, corrected for known secular changes;
- (b) seasonal: the weighted average of the likely prices if the year's requirement is bought at one time, or of the whole year's prices if buying is spread;
- (c) year to year: an estimate of next year's price from a statistical study.

**Wage rate standards**: the type of labour is the most important factor; rates are set on the time taken per unit of production, the time or piece rate prevailing in the industry, the wage agreement with the workers' union, and the law of the area (minimum wages, bonus).

**Overhead expense standards**: a budgeted output (from practical capacity and anticipated sales demand), fixed and variable budgets for it, and a [[cma.bud.flexible|flexible budget]] if production is seasonal or fluctuates.

### Types and classification of variances (SM 13.11–13.13)

**Controllable variances** can be controlled under normal operating conditions if a responsibility centre takes preventive measures and acts prudently; **uncontrollable variances** occur due to conditions beyond its control and cannot be controlled even with all preventive measures in place. Responsibility centres are answerable for all adverse variances which could have been controlled. Controllability is a subjective matter and varies from situation to situation; if uncontrollable variances are significant and persistent, the standard may need revision.

**Favourable** variances are profitable to the company, **adverse** ones cause loss. For costs, favourable means actual below standard; for sales, actual above budget. They are written F and A, whatever the mathematical sign.

Variances are broadly classified into **revenue variances** (actual sales against budgeted sales) and **cost variances**. The cost variances, as in the Study Material's Fig 13.1 (SM 13.13), each with its own lesson:

::: formula Standard cost of actual output and the variance
Standard cost of actual output = standard cost per unit × actual output.

Cost variance = standard cost of actual output − actual cost.

Positive: F; negative: A. For 6,000 trays: 6,000 × ₹270 = ₹16,20,000, whatever the budget said.

## Worked examples

:::: example Three wastage allowances for one samosa dough
Every 1,000 samosas from Tapri's Indore factory hold 40 kg of finished dough. Kneading, sheeting and trimming waste some dough, and the production head offers Ritu three standards of dough input per 1,000 samosas:

1. Only the wastage that cannot be avoided with the best sheeter and a fully trained team: 41 kg.
2. The wastage a trained team achieves with good effort on the present sheeter: 42 kg.
3. The average wastage of the last three years: 44 kg.

Dough costs ₹60 per kg, and November's price paid was the same. In November the factory made 2,50,000 samosas and used 10,600 kg of dough.

**Required:**

(i) Calculate the standard dough cost per samosa on each basis.

(ii) Calculate November's material usage variance on each basis, indicating whether it is favourable (F) or adverse (A).

(iii) Kesar prices a canteen contract for 5,00,000 samosas at the standard dough cost plus ₹3.50 a samosa for all other costs and profit. Show the price per samosa on each basis, and what each price would lose or leave on the table if November's dough usage repeats.

(iv) Advise Ritu which basis to adopt.

::: answer Show the working
**(i) and (ii)** Standard quantity for 2,50,000 samosas = 250 × kg per 1,000. Actual dough cost = 10,600 × ₹60 = ₹6,36,000, or ₹2.544 a samosa. The price paid equals the standard, so the whole variance is a usage variance = (SQ − AQ) × SP.

| Basis | SQ (kg) | Standard cost (₹) | Per samosa (₹) | Usage variance (₹) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Unavoidable wastage only | 10,250 | 6,15,000 | 2.46 | 21,000 (A) |
| Attainable good performance | 10,500 | 6,30,000 | 2.52 | 6,000 (A) |
| Past average wastage | 11,000 | 6,60,000 | 2.64 | 24,000 (F) |

**(iii) The canteen contract**

| Basis | Price (₹) | Actual vs standard (₹) | On 5,00,000 (₹) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Unavoidable wastage only | 5.96 | 0.084 over | 42,000 lost |
| Attainable good performance | 6.02 | 0.024 over | 12,000 lost |
| Past average wastage | 6.14 | 0.096 under | 48,000 extra |

At November's usage the dough actually costs ₹2.544 a samosa. "Lost" is dough cost the price does not recover, which comes out of the ₹3.50 meant for other costs and profit; "extra" is cost built into the price that November's kitchen did not incur.

**(iv) Advice**

- **Unavoidable wastage only (an ideal standard)**: the lowest cost for pricing (₹2.46), but a large adverse variance whenever that wastage is missed, and a contract priced on it under-recovers ₹42,000; it does show how far performance is off the best (SM 13.9).
- **Attainable good performance**: the variance is small (₹6,000 (A)), close to nil as the Study Material expects of this basis (SM 13.9), and it is the type of standard most effective in the control of costs (SM 13.52).
- **Past average wastage**: the ₹24,000 "favourable" variance only says November beat a past average that includes past waste, and the ₹6.14 price may lose the contract to a rival.

Ritu should adopt **attainable good performance**: 42 kg of dough per 1,000 samosas.
:::
::::

:::: example Kesar's perfect samosa standard
Kesar insisted the samosa factory's standards be "the best we have ever done: best prices, best machines, zero waste", and wants the production head to explain every adverse variance. November's report shows:

1. Material price variance ₹1,20,000 (A): the purchase department bought potatoes in small lots at the last minute instead of under the annual contract.
2. Labour rate variance ₹45,000 (A): the State Government revised statutory minimum wages from 1 November.
3. Labour efficiency variance ₹38,000 (A): workers hired for the Diwali rush had not been trained.
4. Idle time variance ₹22,000 (A): a flood in the industrial area cut power for a day.

**Required:**

(i) Comment on Kesar's choice of standards.

(ii) Classify each variance as controllable or uncontrollable and state who is responsible.

(iii) Advise whether any standard should be revised.

::: answer Model answer
**(i)** Kesar has set **ideal standards**: the level of performance attainable when prices for material and labour are most favourable, the highest output is achieved with the best equipment and layout, and maximum efficiency in the utilisation of resources results in maximum output with minimum cost. Such standards are criticised because (a) being unattainable, no one takes them seriously; (b) the variances do not indicate the extent to which they could have been reasonably and practically avoided; and (c) there is no logical method of disposing of them. The type of standard most effective in the control of costs represents an attainable level of good performance, and standards should be set on that basis.

**(ii)** Controllable variances can be controlled under normal operating conditions if a responsibility centre takes preventive measures and acts prudently; uncontrollable variances occur due to conditions beyond its control. Responsibility centres are answerable for all adverse variances which could have been controlled.

| Variance | Nature | Responsibility |
|---|---|---|
| Material price ₹1,20,000 (A) | Controllable: the buying method is within the purchase department's control | Purchase department |
| Labour rate ₹45,000 (A) | Uncontrollable: a minimum wage fixed by law, beyond the organisation's control | Not the production head's; the personnel manager negotiates rates, not statutory minimums |
| Labour efficiency ₹38,000 (A) | Controllable: an untrained team of workers | Production manager or foreman |
| Idle time ₹22,000 (A) | Uncontrollable: abnormal idle time from a flood | None; shown separately as abnormal idle time |

The production head is answerable only for the efficiency variance.

**(iii)** If uncontrollable variances are significant and persistent, the standard may need revision. The higher minimum wage is permanent, and wage rate standards are set with regard to the law of the area, so the wage rate standard should be revised; the flood is a one-time event. The ideal standards should be replaced by standards of attainable good performance.
:::

## Answer frame

### The Study Material's own theory questions (SM 13.57)

- **"Discuss the types of standards"**: the four types in ICAI's order (ideal, normal, basic, current), each with its meaning and the points listed under it above; end with the type most effective for cost control.
- **"Discuss the process of setting standards"**: (1) set on management's estimation, for each element of cost, in physical and monetary terms; (2) physical standards: the factors considered, the material quantity procedure (four steps), the labour time procedure (six steps), the overhead time standards, and the problems faced; (3) price or rate standards: the two bases, material price standards, wage rate standards, overhead expense standards.
- **"How is the material usage standard set?"**: the four steps of the material quantity procedure, each with a line of explanation.
- **"Discuss the process of standard costing"**: the five steps of SM 13.5, each with a line of explanation.

### "Distinguish between controllable and uncontrollable variances"

| Point | Controllable | Uncontrollable |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Can be controlled under normal operating conditions if the responsibility centre takes preventive measures and acts prudently | Due to conditions beyond the responsibility centre's control; cannot be controlled even with all preventive measures |
| Responsibility | Answerable for adverse variances | Not answerable |
| Standard | Corrective action on performance | If significant and persistent, the standard may need revision |

### "Calculation of variances is not an end in itself, but a means to an end"

ICAI's MTP answer, in order: standard costing stresses what the cost should be; the variance may be favourable or unfavourable; knowing the figures is not enough, their origin and causes must be traced; a detailed probe, particularly into controllable variances, helps management ascertain (i) the amount of the variance, (ii) the factors or causes of its occurrence, (iii) the responsibility to be laid on executives and departments, and (iv) the corrective actions to obviate or reduce it; success depends on how quickly and effectively corrective action is taken; ==information is the means and the action taken on it is the end==.

### Advantages of standard costing (SM 13.49–13.51)

1. A basis for **measuring operating performance and cost control**: only exceptional variances are reported, a signal for prompt action.
2. It **aids price fixing**: stable standard costs are a basis for the selling price.
3. It facilitates **evaluation of jobs and introduction of incentives**.
4. It facilitates the **estimation of the cost of new products** with greater accuracy.
5. A basis for **inventory valuation**; material stock can be recorded in quantities only.
6. **Measurement of profit**: it eliminates variations in profit due to changes in stock values from one period to another.
7. Use in **planning, budgeting and decision making**.
8. **Standardisation of products, operations and processes**.
9. **Objectives and targets for each level of management**, defining managers' responsibilities.
10. A **uniform basis for comparison**; the standard hour compares dissimilar products.
11. **Maximum use of working capital, plant facilities and current assets**, as wastage and idle time are closely controlled.

### "Discuss the criticism of standard costing" (SM 13.51–13.52)

The Study Material answers each criticism but the last, so they do not invalidate the usefulness of the system (SM 13.51). Write both halves:

| Criticism | Answer |
|---|---|
| (i) Variation in price: prices are hard to estimate precisely | Forecasting covers fluctuations to some extent; uncontrollable variances are isolated and dealt with separately |
| (ii) Varying levels of output: standard costs not realised if the standard output is missed | True only in some jobbing industries; forecasting estimates output reasonably; prime cost is unaffected; variance analysis measures idle time |
| (iii) Changing standard of technology | Does not affect the system: standards are revised; cost reduction and control remain its cardinal feature |
| (iv) Attitude of technical people: used to physical standards, misled by standard costs | Educated through orientation courses: not insurmountable |
| (v) Mix of products: a pre-determined combination is presupposed | Standards are set for a short period and revised |
| (vi) Level of performance: too strict or too liberal (theoretical maximum, attainable good, average past performance) | Select a suitable type: attainable good performance |
| (vii) Cannot reflect the true value in exchange | True standards analyse economic and technical factors into quantities and prices reflecting optimum operations: realistic measures of the sacrifices involved |
| (viii) Fixation of standards may be costly: high skill needed, hard for small concerns | No answer given |

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: the standard cost of the budgeted output
The café budgeted 24,000 cups (₹2,16,000 of milk) and sold 20,000: "₹9,000 favourable" hides a ₹27,000 overspend. **Fix:** the variance is always against the standard cost of the **actual** output, 20,000 × ₹9.
:::

::: mistake Calling every adverse variance someone's fault
A variance from a flood or a change in law is uncontrollable: blaming the production head is wrong, and so is ignoring it. **Fix:** classify first; centres answer only for adverse variances they could have controlled; significant, persistent uncontrollable ones may mean revising the standard.
:::

::: mistake Basic standards producing variances
"The basic standard variance is ₹x." With basic standards, variances are not calculated (SM 13.4). **Fix:** express actual and current costs as percentages of basic cost and compare; keep current standards for the variances.

## Recap

- Standard cost: "the planned unit cost of the product, component or service produced in a period"; set in quantities and prices.
- Standard costing: compare actual costs with pre-set standards; report variances; manage by exception.
- Process: set standards, ascertain actual costs, compare, investigate, dispose of variances (Costing P&L).
- Types: ideal (three criticisms), normal, basic or bogey (no variances; percentages of basic cost), current. Attainable good performance controls cost best.
- Material steps: standardisation, product study, specification list, test runs. Labour steps: product study, labour specification, methods, layout, time and motion study, training and trial.
- Price standards: the average price expected in the coming period, or the normal price over a cycle of seasons. Material price (small fluctuations): stock held and its price, orders placed, minimum support price, anticipated fluctuation.
- Controllable vs uncontrollable; F means actual cost below standard; significant, persistent uncontrollable variances: the standard may need revision. (The map app does not blame you for the flood.)

## Flashcards

Q:: CIMA definition of standard costing (SM)?
A:: "Control technique that reports variances by comparing actual costs to pre-set standards so facilitating action through management by exception."

Q:: CIMA definition of standard cost (SM)?
A:: "The planned unit cost of the product, component or service produced in a period. The standard cost may be determined on a number of bases. The main use of standard costs is in performance measurement, control, stock valuation and in the establishment of selling prices."

Q:: The five steps in the process of standard costing?
A:: Setting of standards; ascertainment of actual costs; comparison of actual cost with standard cost; investigating the reasons for variances; disposition of variances.

Q:: The four types of standards, in ICAI's order?
A:: Ideal, normal, basic (bogey) and current standards.

Q:: Three criticisms of ideal standards?
A:: Unattainable, so no one takes them seriously; the variances do not show how far costs could reasonably and practically have been avoided; there is no logical method of disposing of the variances.

Q:: How are basic standards used, since variances are not calculated?
A:: Actual cost and current standard cost are expressed as percentages of basic cost and compared with each other and with previous periods to show the trend.

Q:: Which type of standard is most effective for cost control (SM)?
A:: One which represents an attainable level of good performance.

Q:: Four steps in setting material quantity standards?
A:: Standardisation of products; product study; preparation of specification list; test runs.

Q:: Six steps in setting labour time standards?
A:: Standardisation of product and product study; labour specification; standardisation of methods; manufacturing layout; time and motion study; training and trial.

Q:: Four factors for material price standards when price fluctuations are small?
A:: Stock on hand and its prices; prices of orders already placed for future deliveries; minimum support price fixed by the appropriate authority; anticipated fluctuation in price levels.

Q:: Controllable vs uncontrollable variance (SM)?
A:: Controllable: can be controlled under normal operating conditions if the responsibility centre takes preventive measures and acts prudently. Uncontrollable: due to conditions beyond its control, not controllable even with all preventive measures.

Q:: What is a revision variance?
A:: The difference between the original standard cost and the revised standard cost of actual production.
