## In plain words

### One factory, three teas, one bill

Tapri's tea factory near Jorhat buys green leaf from the gardens around it. Every kilo goes through the same withering, rolling, fermenting and drying. Only at the end, at the sorting sieve, does the made tea fall into three grades: whole **Leaf**, **Brokens** and **Dust**. In March 2026 the factory spent ₹1,20,00,000 and made 10,000 kg of Leaf, 20,000 kg of Brokens and 30,000 kg of Dust, all sold at the Guwahati auction.

> **Kesar:** Ritu, the accounts say Dust lost ₹30 lakh. Shut the Dust line. Sab adjust ho jayega.
> **Ritu:** There is no Dust line, Kesar-ji. Same leaf, same dryer. Dust falls through the sieve whether we like it or not.
> **Kesar:** Then who decided it lost money?
> **Ritu:** Whoever split the ₹1.2 crore by weight.

The three grades are **joint products**: several products of real value that come out of one process at the same time. The ₹1,20,00,000 is their **joint cost**, spent before the grades can be told apart. The point where they become separately identifiable (here, the sieve) is the **split-off point**.

Nobody can say how much of the dryer's gas was "for" the Dust, so the joint cost cannot be traced; it can only be **apportioned** on some rational basis. Shared by weight, every kilo carries ₹200, and Dust, which fetches only ₹100 a kilo, shows a ₹30 lakh loss. Shared by what each grade sells for (₹60 lakh, ₹60 lakh and ₹30 lakh, together ₹1.5 crore), every grade carries 80% of its own sales value, and every grade makes a 20% profit. The factory's total profit, ₹30 lakh, is the same both ways.

::: analogy The wedding biryani
A caterer cooks one giant handi of biryani; leg pieces, rice and gravy all come out of the same pot. Charge the cost of the handi by weight and the plain rice costs as much per kilo as the leg pieces, so the rice counter looks like a loss-maker. Charge it by what each portion sells for and every counter carries cost in proportion to what it earns. The handi cost the same either way; only the slicing changed.
:::

::: key
A joint cost cannot be traced to any one joint product; it can only be apportioned on a rational basis. The method (by quantity, by sales value, by net realisable value) changes each product's cost, profit and inventory value, never the total joint cost, so a product's "loss" after apportionment proves nothing by itself.
:::

## How it works

### The vocabulary, in ICAI's words

::: definition Joint products
Two or more products separated in the course of the same processing operation usually requiring further processing, each product being in such proportion that no single product can be designated as a major product. In other words, two or more products of equal importance, produced simultaneously from the same process, with each having a significant relative sale value. Example: gasoline, fuel oil, lubricants, paraffin, coal tar, asphalt and kerosene from crude petroleum. (SM 11.2)
:::

::: definition Split-off point
A point in a production process where joint products emerging from the process get separately identifiable. Joint cost is incurred up to this point only; any cost incurred after the split-off point is a product-specific cost, borne by the product concerned. (SM 11.3)
:::

::: definition Joint costs
The expenditures incurred up to the point of separation, i.e. the split-off point: the resources spent for producing more than one product from processing a common input. They include raw material, labour, power, fuel, depreciation and overhead costs towards the production of the joint products. (SM 11.4)
:::

A product of small value that comes out incidentally (molasses beside sugar) is a **by-product**, with its own treatment: [[cma.joint.byprod|treatment of by-products]]. **Co-products** are two or more products which are contemporary but do not necessarily emerge from the same material in the same process, such as wheat and gram grown on two separate farms (SM 11.3); each co-product is treated as a separate cost object for costing purposes (SM 11.21–11.22, MCQ 9).

### Why the apportionment matters

ICAI gives three reasons (SM 11.4): it affects (a) the **valuation of closing inventory**; (b) the **pricing of products**; and (c) the **profit or loss on the sale of different products**. Because the relations between materials, processes and joint products are complex and unobservable, the cost of each joint product cannot be correctly identified; ==it can only be apportioned by using some rational method==.

### ICAI's seven methods, in ICAI's two groups

In ICAI's order, first the three commonly used methods, then the four others:

::: steps
1. **Physical units.** Share the joint cost in the ratio of output at split-off in a common unit (kg, litres, numbers). Any loss in the joint process is shared on the same basis. Products in different units must first be converted to a common unit; if they cannot be, the method cannot be applied. Its main defect: it gives equal importance and value to all the joint products (SM 11.5).
2. **Net realisable value (NRV) at split-off.** From each product's final sales value deduct its post split-off processing cost and its directly attributable selling and distribution expenses (freight, royalty, commission), and a profit margin where one is given; share the joint cost in the ratio of what is left (SM 11.6–11.7).
3. **Technical estimates.** Apportion the joint cost on the basis of technical estimates; used when the result of the other methods does not match the resources consumed by the joint products, or their realisable values are not readily available (SM 11.8).
4. **Market value at the point of separation** (sales value at split-off). Share the joint cost in the ratio of each product's quantity produced × its selling price at split-off. ICAI's shortcut is the **multiplying factor**: joint cost ÷ total sales value × 100, applied to each product's sales value (SM 11.8).
5. **Market value after further processing.** Share it in the ratio of final sales values of the finished products (SM 11.9). Unfair when further processing costs are disproportionate or not all products are processed further (SM 11.10).
6. **Average unit cost.** Divide the joint cost by the total units produced and charge every unit that average. It gives the same answer as physical units (SM 11.10).
7. **Contribution margin.** Split the joint cost into variable and fixed. Share the variable part on physical units; add any variable cost after split-off; deduct the total from each product's sales to get its contribution; share the fixed part in the ratio of contributions (SM 11.11).
:::

### Which method when: ICAI's circumstances table

| Method | When it is used (SM 11.11) |
|---|---|
| Physical unit | Sale price of all the products is uniform |
| NRV at split-off | Realisable value at split-off is not known |
| Technical estimates | NRV result does not match the resources consumed |
| Market value at separation | Further processing costs are disproportionate |
| Market value after processing | Further costs proportionate; all products processed |
| Average unit cost | Units produced have the same unit |

## Formats and formulas

*Reproduce the NRV statement and the formulas from memory; they are the working notes ICAI's answers start with.*

::: formula Physical units (and average unit cost)
$$\text{Share of a product} = \frac{\text{Joint cost}}{\text{Total units}} \times \text{Units of the product}$$
Units are the quantities **produced** at split-off, in one common unit.
:::

::: formula Sales value at split-off: the multiplying factor
$$\text{Multiplying factor} = \frac{\text{Joint cost}}{\text{Total sales revenue}} \times 100$$
Total sales revenue = Σ (quantity produced × market price at split-off). Joint cost of a product = its sales value at split-off × multiplying factor (SM 11.8).
:::

::: formula Net realisable value
$$\text{NRV} = \text{Final sales} - \text{Profit margin} \qquad - \text{S\&D costs} - \text{Post split-off cost}$$
Joint cost of a product = total joint cost × its NRV ÷ total NRV. Final sales value = units after processing × selling price. S&D = directly attributable selling and distribution costs.
:::

**NRV statement** (ICAI's pro-forma, SM 11.7):

| Particulars | Product A (₹) | Product B (₹) | Product C (₹) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Sales value (units × final price) | xxx | xxx | xxx |
| Less: Profit margin | (xxx) | (xxx) | (xxx) |
| Less: Selling and distribution costs | (xxx) | (xxx) | (xxx) |
| Less: Post split-off cost | (xxx) | (xxx) | (xxx) |
| **Net realisable value** | **xxx** | **xxx** | **xxx** |
| Joint cost apportioned (NRV ratio) | xxx | xxx | xxx |

The profit margin line is used only when the question gives a margin; most questions give none, and the line drops out.

::: formula Contribution margin method
$$\text{Contribution} = \text{Sales} - \text{Variable cost}$$
Variable cost = variable joint cost shared on physical units + variable cost after split-off. Fixed joint cost of a product = total fixed joint cost × its contribution ÷ total contribution. Its joint cost = its variable share + its fixed share.
:::

::: formula Constant gross-margin percentage NRV method
$$\text{Gross margin \%} = \frac{\text{Final sales} - \text{All costs}}{\text{Final sales}} \times 100$$
All costs = joint cost + separable (post split-off) costs; final sales = final sales value of the whole production.

Joint cost of a product = its final sales value × (1 − gross margin %) − its separable costs, so every product earns the same gross margin percentage (SM 11.38–11.39, Case Scenario 1).
:::

:::: example Cocoa butter and powder: when the butter price falls
Tapri's hot chocolate has to beat BrewBucks, so Tapri presses its own cocoa. In June 2026 its cocoa unit pressed cocoa mass into 8,000 kg of cocoa butter and 12,000 kg of cocoa powder, both sold at the split-off point: butter at ₹850 a kg and powder at ₹350 a kg. The joint costs were ₹50,00,000 of variable cost (cocoa mass, labour and power, which vary with the kilos pressed) and ₹10,00,000 of fixed overheads.

**Required:**

(i) Apportion June's joint cost by the physical units method and by the contribution margin method, and show the profit of each product under each.

(ii) In July a glut of cocoa butter from BrewBucks' supplier pushes the butter price down to ₹700 a kg; quantities, costs and the powder price do not change. Repeat (i) for July and explain why the powder's profit changes under one method but not under the other.

**(i) June**

*Physical units method* (8,000 : 12,000 kg = 2 : 3; joint cost ₹60,00,000, ₹300 a kg)

| Particulars (₹) | Butter | Powder | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Sales value (8,000 × ₹850; 12,000 × ₹350) | 68,00,000 | 42,00,000 | 1,10,00,000 |
| Joint cost (2 : 3) | 24,00,000 | 36,00,000 | 60,00,000 |
| **Profit** | **44,00,000** | **6,00,000** | **50,00,000** |

*Contribution margin method*

| Particulars (₹) | Butter | Powder | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A. Sales value | 68,00,000 | 42,00,000 | 1,10,00,000 |
| B. Variable joint cost (2 : 3) | 20,00,000 | 30,00,000 | 50,00,000 |
| C. Contribution (A − B) | 48,00,000 | 12,00,000 | 60,00,000 |
| D. Fixed joint cost (48 : 12 = 4 : 1) | 8,00,000 | 2,00,000 | 10,00,000 |
| **Profit (C − D)** | **40,00,000** | **10,00,000** | **50,00,000** |

Joint cost apportioned: butter ₹28,00,000 and powder ₹32,00,000 (against ₹24,00,000 and ₹36,00,000 by physical units).

**(ii) July** (butter sales 8,000 × ₹700 = ₹56,00,000)

| Particulars (₹) | Butter | Powder | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Physical units: joint cost (unchanged, 2 : 3) | 24,00,000 | 36,00,000 | 60,00,000 |
| **Physical units: profit** | **32,00,000** | **6,00,000** | **38,00,000** |
| Contribution (₹56,00,000 − ₹20,00,000; ₹42,00,000 − ₹30,00,000) | 36,00,000 | 12,00,000 | 48,00,000 |
| Fixed joint cost (36 : 12 = 3 : 1) | 7,50,000 | 2,50,000 | 10,00,000 |
| **Contribution margin: profit** | **28,50,000** | **9,50,000** | **38,00,000** |

**Explanation.** Total profit falls by ₹12,00,000 under both methods, because butter's sales fall by that much and the joint cost does not change. The physical units method shares the joint cost by kilos only, so the whole fall stays with butter and the powder's profit is unchanged at ₹6,00,000. The contribution margin method shares the fixed cost by contribution: butter's contribution falls from ₹48,00,000 to ₹36,00,000, so it takes a smaller share of the fixed overheads (₹7,50,000) and ₹50,000 more moves to the powder, whose profit falls to ₹9,50,000 although nothing about the powder changed. Neither apportionment should decide whether to keep a joint product: the joint cost is incurred for both together.

## Under the hood

### A loss inside the joint process

Under the physical units method a normal loss in the joint process is shared on the same basis as the cost (SM 11.5). If 5,000 kg goes in and 4,800 kg of two products comes out in the ratio 3 : 1, the 200 kg loss is shared 150 : 50, and each product's share of the cost is the same as sharing it on the output 3,600 : 1,200. When the joint products come out of a process with normal loss and abnormal gain, first find the cost of good output in the process account ([[cma.proc.accounts|process accounts]]), then apportion it (RTP May 2025 MCQ 6).

### The profit-margin line of the NRV statement

When final prices are fixed to give a stated margin, the margin is deducted too. Example: a product sells at ₹30 a unit, priced to earn 20% on cost, with ₹9 a unit of post split-off cost. Cost of sales = ₹30 ÷ 1.20 = ₹25, so its joint cost = ₹25 − ₹9 = ₹16 a unit. Watch the base: 20% on cost is 16.67% on sales, and ICAI's own working writes "25% on cost or 20% on sales" (SM 11.34). This is how the January 2025 MCQ 12 is solved.

### What ICAI's wording about NRV means

The Study Material says sales value at split-off "is the preferred technique" when selling prices for all products exist at split-off (SM 11.7), and that the NRV method "is used when the realisable value of joint products at split-off is not known" (SM 11.8, 11.11). Read together: use split-off prices when they exist; when a product has no market at split-off, estimate its value at split-off backwards from the final price. The Study Material's Illustration 4 does both on the same data (SM 11.13–11.15).

### Total profit and the closing stock

When everything produced is sold, every method gives the same total profit, because the same total joint cost is charged. With closing stock, the method decides how much joint cost sits in the inventory and how much reaches cost of goods sold, so the period's profit moves too. That is reason (a) in ICAI's list.

### Market value after further processing

Using final sales values is fair only when further processing costs are proportionate and all products are processed further (SM 11.11). If one product carries heavy post split-off costs, its final sales value overstates what the joint process gave it; the NRV method corrects that by deducting those costs first (SM 11.10).

::: exam The traps ICAI set
- Quantities **sold** given with opening and closing stocks: the base is quantity **produced** = sold + closing − opening (RTP May 2026 MCQ 5; RTP January 2025 MCQ 6, whose wrong option (a) uses units sold).
- Joint cost not given as one figure: add material, conversion cost and overheads first (May 2026 Q3(a): raw material plus Department I conversion cost), or apportion factory overheads to the departments first (MTP May 2025 Series II Q5(a)).
- Selling price "assuming a net margin of 25% on cost": deduct the margin before the post split-off cost (January 2025 MCQ 12).
- Contribution margin method: variable cost on physical units, fixed cost on contribution (May 2024 Q1(b); September 2025 MCQ 12).
:::

::: trick Check the total
Whatever the method, the shares must add back to the total joint cost, and (with no closing stock) the product profits must add to the same total profit. Condition: the same joint cost, and no by-product credit taken in one method but not the other.
:::

## Answer frame

### "Describe briefly how joint costs are apportioned under …"

The chapter's own theory question (SM 11.23, Q4). Write each method as a definition, the basis and one limitation, in this order:

1. **Average unit cost**: total process cost up to separation ÷ total units produced; all products get a uniform cost per unit; if used for pricing, all products may have more or less the same price.
2. **Contribution margin**: joint cost split into variable and fixed; variable apportioned on physical quantities, plus variable cost after split-off; contribution = sales − variable cost; fixed apportioned in the ratio of contributions.
3. **Market value at the point of separation**: joint cost apportioned in the ratio of sales value at split-off (multiplying factor = joint cost ÷ total sales value × 100); difficult when the market value at separation is not available; useful when further processing costs are disproportionate.
4. **Market value after further processing**: ratio of final sales values; unfair when further processing costs are disproportionate or not all products are processed further.
5. **Net realisable value**: from final sales value deduct estimated profit margin, selling and distribution expenses and post split-off costs; joint cost apportioned in the ratio of NRV.

### "Mention the circumstances …"

Reproduce ICAI's circumstances table from How it works, one line a method, in ICAI's words.

## Common mistakes

::: mistake Hall of Shame: the joint cost that followed the sales invoice
Apportioning on units **sold** when the question gives stocks. The joint cost was spent on everything produced, including what still sits in the cold room. **Fix:** produced = sold + closing stock − opening stock, then apportion.
:::

::: mistake NRV that forgot to subtract anything
Taking the final sales values as the "NRV" (that is the market value after further processing method). In the tea factory it would give Dust ₹48,00,000 ÷ ₹1,67,20,000 of the joint cost instead of ₹24,00,000 ÷ ₹1,20,00,000. **Fix:** NRV = final sales value − post split-off cost − attributable selling and distribution costs (− profit margin if given).
:::

::: mistake The contribution margin method done in one step
Sharing the whole joint cost in the ratio of contributions, or working contribution without the variable joint cost. **Fix:** variable joint cost on physical units first; contribution = sales − variable cost; only the fixed joint cost follows the contribution ratio.
:::

::: mistake Kilos and litres added together
Adding 4,000 kg and 3,000 litres for the physical units method. **Fix:** convert to a common unit first (litres to kg through the density, for example); if no common unit exists, the method cannot be applied (SM 11.5).
:::

## Practice

::: quiz Check yourself
Q: Joint cost of ₹9,60,000 is apportioned on units produced. Product P: sold 30,000 kg, opening stock 2,000 kg, closing stock 6,000 kg. Product Q: sold 44,000 kg, no opening stock, closing stock 2,000 kg. What joint cost (₹) goes to P?
= 408000
> Produced: P = 30,000 + 6,000 − 2,000 = 34,000 kg; Q = 44,000 + 2,000 = 46,000 kg; total 80,000 kg. P's share = ₹9,60,000 × 34,000 ÷ 80,000 = ₹4,08,000. Using units sold gives ₹3,89,189.

Q: Case scenario. Ritu is choosing a method for Tapri's spice unit. Market prices at the split-off point are known for all three products, and their further processing costs differ hugely: one is only bagged, another is roasted, ground and packed. According to ICAI's circumstances, which method suits this case?
- [ ] Physical units method
- [ ] Market value after further processing
- [x] Market value at the point of separation
- [ ] Average unit cost method
> ICAI uses market value at the point of separation "where further processing costs are incurred disproportionately" (SM 11.11). Final market values would favour the product with heavy processing; physical units and average unit cost suit uniform prices or a common unit.

Q: Joint cost is ₹6,00,000. Product M has a final sales value of ₹6,00,000 and further processing cost of ₹3,00,000; product N ₹4,00,000 and ₹1,00,000. What joint cost goes to M under the NRV method?
- [ ] ₹3,60,000
- [x] ₹3,00,000
- [ ] ₹2,40,000
- [ ] ₹4,50,000
> NRV: M = ₹3,00,000, N = ₹3,00,000, so 1 : 1 and ₹3,00,000 each. ₹3,60,000 shares on final sales values (6 : 4), the market value after further processing method; ₹4,50,000 shares on further processing costs.

Q: Joint variable cost ₹2,40,000 and joint fixed cost ₹1,20,000 produce X 3,000 kg (sold at ₹100) and Y 5,000 kg (sold at ₹60). Under the contribution margin method, what total joint cost (₹) goes to X?
= 160000
> Variable cost on kg 3 : 5: X ₹90,000, Y ₹1,50,000. Contribution: X ₹3,00,000 − ₹90,000 = ₹2,10,000; Y ₹3,00,000 − ₹1,50,000 = ₹1,50,000; ratio 7 : 5. Fixed to X = ₹1,20,000 × 7/12 = ₹70,000. Total ₹90,000 + ₹70,000 = ₹1,60,000.

Q: Which of these methods charge every unit of every joint product the same joint cost per unit? Select all that apply.
- [x] Physical units method
- [x] Average unit cost method
- [ ] Net realisable value method
- [ ] Market value at the point of separation
> Both divide the joint cost by total units, so every unit carries the same amount; ICAI notes they give the same outcome (SM 11.10). The value-based methods charge more to the products that earn more.
:::

## Recap

- Joint products: two or more products of equal importance, produced simultaneously from the same process, each with a significant relative sale value (SM 11.2).
- Joint cost is everything up to the split-off point; after it, costs belong to the product concerned.
- Apportionment affects inventory valuation, pricing and product profit; the cost cannot be identified, only apportioned on a rational basis.
- Commonly used: physical units, NRV at split-off, technical estimates. Other: market value at separation, market value after further processing, average unit cost, contribution margin.
- Multiplying factor = joint cost ÷ total sales value at split-off × 100.
- NRV = final sales − profit margin − selling and distribution costs − post split-off cost.
- Contribution margin method: variable joint cost on units, fixed joint cost on contribution.
- Base is units produced (sold + closing − opening). The handi cost the same; only the slicing changed.

## Flashcards

Q:: ICAI's definition of joint products?
A:: Two or more products separated in the course of the same processing operation usually requiring further processing, each in such proportion that no single product can be designated as a major product.

Q:: What is the split-off point, and what happens to costs after it?
A:: The point in a production process where joint products become separately identifiable. Joint cost is incurred up to it; any cost after it is product-specific and borne by the product concerned.

Q:: Three things affected by the apportionment of joint cost (ICAI)?
A:: (a) Valuation of closing inventory; (b) pricing of products; (c) profit or loss on the sale of different products.

Q:: ICAI's three commonly used methods of apportioning joint cost?
A:: (i) Physical units method; (ii) net realisable value at split-off point; (iii) using technical estimates.

Q:: ICAI's four "other methods" managers may use for decisions?
A:: (i) Market value at the point of separation; (ii) market value after further processing; (iii) average unit cost method; (iv) contribution margin method.

Q:: Formula: multiplying factor (market value at the point of separation)?
A:: Joint cost ÷ total sales revenue at split-off × 100; each product's joint cost = its sales value × the factor.

Q:: What is deducted from final sales value to get NRV at split-off?
A:: Profit margin (if given), directly attributable selling and distribution costs (freight, royalty, commission), and post split-off processing cost.

Q:: Contribution margin method: what is apportioned on what basis?
A:: Variable joint cost on physical units; fixed joint cost in the ratio of contributions (sales − variable cost, including variable cost after split-off).

Q:: Main defect of the physical units method (ICAI)?
A:: It gives equal importance and value to all the joint products; it also cannot be used if the products cannot be converted to a common unit.

Q:: When is the market value at the point of separation method used (ICAI's circumstances)?
A:: Where further processing costs are incurred disproportionately.

Q:: Co-products: definition and example?
A:: Two or more products which are contemporary but do not necessarily emerge from the same material in the same process, e.g. wheat and gram grown on two separate farms.
