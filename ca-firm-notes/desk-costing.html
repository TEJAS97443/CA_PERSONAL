Unit 1 — Overview of Cost and Management Accounting§

Principal

This factory made 40,000 units last month and sold them for ₹4,50,000. Did it make money?

You

Their profit and loss account says yes, ₹60,000.

Principal

That tells you the factory made money. I asked whether the units did. Which of them, and how much each?

Margin note

That gap is the entire reason cost accounting exists. Financial accounting answers "how did the entity perform"; cost accounting answers "what did this thing cost, and why". Every definitional question in this unit is really about that distinction.

What cost and management accounting is for§

The syllabus objective is worth reading as a list of verbs: establish the cost of products and services, apply it to determine prices, understand cost statements, and apply information for cost ascertainment, planning, control and decision making.

Four disciplines get confused in exam answers, so fix them now:

DisciplineQuestion it answersAudienceGoverned by
Financial AccountingHow did the entity perform and what does it own and owe?External — shareholders, lenders, regulatorsStatute and accounting standards
Cost AccountingWhat did this product, service, job or process cost?Internal managementManagement's own needs
Management AccountingWhat should we do?Internal managementNothing mandatory
Financial ManagementWhere do funds come from and where should they go?Internal, and the capital marketJudgement plus market discipline

Users of cost and management accounting information are internal: management at every level, plus employees, and in a regulated or cost-audited environment, outside parties. The functions of management accounting run from collecting and classifying data through planning, control, performance measurement and decision support.

The cost accounting department does not sit alone. It draws from stores, personnel and payroll, production, and financial accounting, and it feeds back to all of them. Exam questions on "relation with other departments" want that two-way flow, not a list.

Installation of a costing system is examined as a set of considerations rather than a procedure: the objective and the entity's own needs, the nature of the product and process, the organisational structure, the level of detail worth its cost, and the cooperation of the people who will feed it. The recurring theme is that the system must be worth more than it costs to run and must fit the business it is installed in.

Remember this Cost accounting is not a smaller financial accounting. It has a different question, a different audience, and no statutory format to hide behind.

Answers that describe cost accounting as "accounting for costs in more detail" lose the mark. The distinction the examiner wants is the purpose: ascertainment, control and decision making for internal management, against a chosen cost object.

Cost terms you must be exact about§

  • Cost — the amount of expenditure incurred on, or attributable to, a specified thing.
  • Cost object — the thing being costed: a product, service, job, process, batch, customer, department.
  • Cost centre — a location, person, or item of equipment for which cost is ascertained and used for control. Subdivided into personal and impersonal, and into production and service cost centres.
  • Cost unit — the unit of quantity in which cost is expressed. Get the unit right and the answer follows: per tonne-kilometre for transport, per patient-day for a hospital, per kilowatt-hour for power, per student-year for education.
  • Cost driver — the factor that causes a change in the cost of an activity. This is the term Activity Based Costing is built on, later in Unit 2.

Cost control versus cost reduction is asked almost every attempt, and the contrast is clean:

Cost controlCost reduction
AimKeep cost within a predetermined standard or budgetReduce cost permanently below the present level

That last row is the mark most people drop: cost reduction must be real and permanent, and must not reduce the utility or quality of the product. A saving obtained by cheapening the product is not cost reduction.

Elements of cost, and how cost behaves§

The three elements are material, labour (employee cost) and expenses, and each splits into direct and indirect. The three indirect elements together are overheads.

                       Material        Labour          Expenses
   Direct           Direct material  Direct wages   Direct expenses   -> PRIME COST
   Indirect         Indirect mat.    Indirect lab.  Indirect exp.     -> OVERHEADS

Everything in Paper 4 hangs off that grid. Prime cost is the total of the direct row; overheads are the total of the indirect row, then classified functionally as factory, administration, selling, distribution, and research and development.

Behavioural classification is the other axis, and Unit 4's marginal costing depends on it:

  • Fixed cost — unchanged in total over a period within a relevant range, so it falls per unit as volume rises. Factory rent.
  • Variable cost — changes in total in direct proportion to volume, so it is constant per unit. Direct material.
  • Semi-variable cost — has both a fixed and a variable component. A telephone line rental plus call charges; supervisory salary plus overtime.
  • Step cost — fixed over a range, then jumps to a new level. One supervisor per shift: add a shift and the cost steps up, it does not slope.

Separating the fixed and variable halves of a semi-variable cost is examined numerically, usually by the high-low method: take the highest and lowest activity levels, divide the change in cost by the change in volume to get the variable rate per unit, then substitute back to get the fixed portion.

Exam trap Calling a step cost a variable cost because it rises with volume

A variable cost rises proportionately and is constant per unit. A step cost is fixed within each range and jumps between ranges, so it is neither constant in total nor constant per unit. The exam tests this with supervision, inspection and delivery-vehicle costs. If the cost only changes when you cross a threshold, it steps.

Methods of costing answer "what kind of thing are we costing" — unit, job, batch, contract, process, operation, service — and are the whole of Unit 3. Techniques of costing answer "how are we using the cost" — standard costing, marginal costing, absorption costing, budgetary control — and are the whole of Unit 4. Do not mix the two words in an answer; the syllabus keeps them separate and so does the marking.

Digital costing is explicitly in the syllabus. Treat it as the use of integrated digital systems — ERP, real-time data capture, analytics, and in Unit 2 the Government e-Marketplace (GeM) and digital inventory control — to collect and analyse cost data continuously rather than periodically. The benefits examined are accuracy, timeliness, traceability and lower cost of collection; the concerns are data quality, implementation cost and control over access.

Four things about that statement are where marks are actually won:

  1. Freight inward is part of material cost; freight outward is distribution overhead. The words are nearly identical and the treatments are opposite.
  2. Work-in-progress is adjusted at works cost stage, not at prime cost, because WIP has already absorbed factory overheads.
  3. Finished goods stock is adjusted after cost of production, because that is the value at which finished goods are carried.
  4. Sale of scrap is deducted from factory overheads or works cost, not treated as income.

Verify: the treatment of administration overheads — how much is absorbed into cost of production versus shown after cost of goods sold — is a convention that ICAI states in the Study Material. Confirm the layout your attempt's material uses before the exam.

For the service sector the same logic applies with a different cost unit and no material or WIP to speak of. A service cost statement builds up operating cost by element, then divides by the composite cost unit. The examinable skill is choosing that unit: a tonne-kilometre for goods transport, a passenger-kilometre for passenger transport, a bed-day or patient-day for a hospital, a room-day for a hotel, a kilowatt-hour for power.

Raw material consumed = 20,000 + 1,80,000 + 8,000 − 5,000 − 25,000 = ₹1,78,000.

Prime cost = 1,78,000 + 90,000 + 12,000 = ₹2,80,000.

Works cost = 2,80,000 + 60,000 + 15,000 − 18,000 − 3,000 = ₹3,34,000.

Cost of production = 3,34,000 + 6,000 = ₹3,40,000.

Cost of goods sold = 3,40,000 + 22,000 − 30,000 = ₹3,32,000.

Cost of sales = 3,32,000 + 25,000 + 33,000 = ₹3,90,000.

Profit = 4,50,000 − 3,90,000 = ₹60,000.

Check

Two checks. First, the two percentages must differ and the cost-based one must be the larger, because the denominator is smaller — if yours came out equal or the other way round, you have divided by the wrong figure. Second, read the question's wording: "profit on cost" and "profit on sales" are different questions, and a question that gives you a margin and asks for sales is testing exactly this. When the question says the entity earns a profit of 20% on cost, sales are 120% of cost; when it says 20% on sales, cost is 80% of sales.

Practise it Without looking above, write the cost sheet skeleton from opening raw material down to sales, marking at which line WIP is adjusted and at which line finished goods stock is adjusted.

A full-mark skeleton has the six named totals in this order — raw material consumed, prime cost, works or factory cost, cost of production, cost of goods sold, cost of sales — with sales reached by adding profit.

The two adjustment points are the whole test:

  • Work-in-progress is adjusted between prime cost and works cost. Opening WIP added, closing WIP deducted. It sits there because WIP already carries factory overheads, so adjusting it before overheads are added would understate it.
  • Finished goods stock is adjusted between cost of production and cost of goods sold. Opening added, closing deducted. It sits there because finished goods are valued at cost of production.

If you wrote the two adjustments in the wrong places, the totals still add up and every subsequent figure is wrong — which is exactly why examiners like this question. Also check that you deducted sale of scrap rather than adding it as income, and that carriage inward went into material rather than into distribution.

Unit 4's marginal costing and CVP analysis is built entirely on splitting cost into fixed and variable, so the high-low method you learn here is a prerequisite there rather than a separate topic.

Where it stops

Marginal costing treats the fixed/variable split as a decision tool and deliberately excludes fixed cost from product cost. The cost sheet in this unit is an absorption costing statement and includes fixed factory overheads in cost of production. The same classification serves two methods that give different product costs and different profits — reconciling them is itself an examinable topic.

Unit 2 — Ascertainment of Cost and Cost Accounting System§

Material cost: procurement, receipt, issue§

Procurement runs requisition, tender or quotation above the value limit, purchase order, goods received note, stores ledger, requisition on issue. GeM, the Government e-Marketplace, discovers rates online and makes tendering auditable; ERP costing unifies purchase, stores, production and costing so that material requirement analysis is continuous; digital inventory control fires re-order triggers off tagged movements.

Material cost is purchase price plus non-recoverable taxes, freight inward, insurance in transit and handling, less trade discount, rebates and refundable taxes; cash discount is financial and excluded. Normal transit shortage is absorbed by the good units, raising the rate. Issues use FIFO, LIFO, average or standard price — weighted average recomputes the rate on every receipt, never on an issue.

Inventory control§

Formula Economic Order Quantity
Formula

EOQ = sqrt( (2 x A x O) / C )

Terms

A = annual consumption in units. O = ordering cost per order. C = carrying cost per unit per annum.

Watch out

Convert carrying cost given as a percentage of unit price into rupees per unit a year first.

Formula Stock levels and safety stock
Formula

Re-order level = Maximum consumption x Maximum re-order period Minimum level = Re-order level − (Normal consumption x Normal re-order period) Maximum level = Re-order level + Re-order quantity − (Minimum consumption x Minimum re-order period)

Terms

Safety stock is the cushion against variation in consumption and in lead time.

Watch out

Re-order level takes the maximum of both figures, minimum level the normal of both.

Techniques differ by basis: ABC by annual value of consumption, FSN by movement (which flags obsolescence), HML by unit price, VED by criticality. JIT produces against demand only, so carrying cost approaches zero. Inventory turnover is material consumed over average inventory.

Because ABC classifies by annual value of consumption while VED classifies by criticality to production. A cheap gasket whose absence halts the line is vital, so the control policy follows the V, not the C. The techniques are complementary, not alternatives.

Employee cost§

Time keeping records presence, time booking what the time was spent on; the gap is idle time. Elements of wages are basic pay, dearness allowance, overtime, bonus, holiday and leave wages, allowances and perquisites, leave wages being recovered through an inflated hourly rate. Normal idle time is absorbed in the wage rate or in factory overhead and abnormal idle time goes to Costing P&L; the overtime premium goes to the job for customer urgency, to factory overhead where overtime is general, to the department whose delay caused it, or to Costing P&L when abnormal.

Formula Employee turnover rates
Formula

Separation rate = (Separations / Average employees) x 100 Replacement rate = (Replacements / Average employees) x 100 New recruitment rate = (New recruitments / Average employees) x 100 Flux rate = ((Separations + Accessions) / Average employees) x 100

Terms

Accessions = replacements + new recruitments; average employees = (opening + closing) / 2.

Watch out

Expansion is not turnover, so keep replacements and new recruitments apart; some editions define flux on separations plus replacements only. On 1,800 at the start and 2,200 at the close, with 90 separations, 60 replacements and 430 new recruitments: average 2,000, so 4.5%, 3%, 21.5%, flux 29%.

Causes are avoidable (pay, supervision, conditions) and unavoidable (retirement, death, illness, dismissal); effects are costed as preventive and replacement cost — recruitment, training, low output while learning. Employee cost control works through standard times, incentives tied to output, and watching idle time, overtime and turnover.

Formula Halsey and Rowan premium bonus
Formula

Halsey wages = (Time taken x Rate) + (50% x Time saved x Rate) Rowan wages = (Time taken x Rate) + ((Time saved / Standard time) x Time taken x Rate)

Terms

Time saved = standard time allowed − time taken; Halsey's share is 50% unless stated.

Watch out

Rowan's bonus is a proportion of wages earned, not of time saved. On standard time 40 hours, taken 30, rate ₹50: Halsey pays 1,500 + 250 = ₹1,750, Rowan 1,500 + 375 = ₹1,875. Rowan pays more while time saved is under half the standard time and Halsey more beyond it; at exactly half — 20 hours taken of 40 — the plans coincide, each paying ₹1,000 of wages plus ₹500 of bonus.

Direct expenses§

Direct or chargeable expenses are wholly attributable to one job, product, service or process — a royalty per unit, a tool hired for one job. They form the third line of prime cost; anything shared on a basis is an overhead.

Overheads§

Overheads are the three indirect elements, classified functionally (factory, administration, selling, distribution, research and development) and behaviourally (fixed, variable, semi-variable). Allocation charges a whole item to the centre where it arose, apportionment splits a common item on an equitable basis (floor area, capital value, headcount, kilowatt-hours), absorption charges a centre's total onto its cost units.

Primary distribution spreads factory overhead over all departments, service ones included; secondary distribution clears the service departments onto production, by the direct method, the step ladder where servicing runs one way, or — for reciprocal services — repeated distribution or simultaneous equations (S1 = 4,500 + 0.10 S2 with S2 = 4,000 + 0.20 S1 gives ₹5,000 each).

Absorption rates are a percentage of direct wages or prime cost, a rate per unit, a labour hour rate, or a machine hour rate where work is machine-paced. Predetermined, they leave overhead under- or over-absorbed, cleared by a supplementary rate, by carry forward where seasonal, or to Costing P&L. Administration overheads are apportioned between production and selling, absorbed into production cost, or charged wholly to Costing P&L.

Verify: which treatment of administration overheads your attempt's Study Material treats as the standard, against the current Study Guidelines.

Selling and distribution overheads are absorbed on sales value, units sold or weight, and marketing strategy moves them directly: the cost effectiveness of a promotion method is judged on incremental contribution against incremental cost. R&D cost follows purpose: existing product to its production overhead, new-product development charged when production begins, an abandoned project to Costing P&L.

Activity Based Costing§

Overhead is traced to activities — set-ups, purchase orders, inspections, handling — each with the cost driver that causes its cost, and charged to products on the drivers each consumes, from unit level up to facility level. ABC pays where overheads are large and products differ in complexity, since one labour-hour rate cross-subsidises them.

Integration of cost and financial data§

Financial data is recorded once, then segregated by element and function. A non-integrated system keeps a separate self-balancing cost ledger through a General Ledger Adjustment Account, with control accounts for stores, work-in-progress, finished goods, wages and overhead; two sets of books mean two profits, so reconciliation is required. An integrated system keeps one set, so there is no reconciliation to do.

Exam trap Absorbing abnormal cost into the cost unit

Abnormal idle time, wastage, process loss, an overtime premium caused by a department's own delay and under-absorption from an abnormal cause all leave product cost for the Costing P&L. Candidates lose it by never asking the question of an item like "idle time ₹8,000".

Unit 3 — Methods of Costing§

The method follows the shape of the output: identical continuous units give unit costing, customer orders give job costing, identifiable lots give batch costing, a continuous sequence of operations whose output cannot be told apart gives process or operation costing, and an intangible output gives service costing.

Single output or unit costing§

One product, one cost unit — a tonne of cement, a thousand bricks, a litre of paint. The statement is the cost sheet of Unit 1 carried down to cost of sales, with a cost-per-unit column beside the total column. Where output comes in grades, it is converted into equivalent units of the standard grade before dividing.

Job costing§

Each order is a cost object with its own job cost card or record in a job database: material from stores requisitions at the issue price in force, wages from job cards, direct expenses as incurred, and overhead at the absorption rate of each department the job passes through. The card totals to a job cost, compared with the quotation to give profit on the job. It suits printing, repairs, one-off construction and professional assignments — work done to customer specification, so no two cost objects are alike.

Batch costing§

A batch is the cost unit while it is made, accumulated on a batch cost sheet and then divided by good units produced to give cost per unit. Spoiled work within the normal rate is absorbed by the good units of the batch; beyond it, the abnormal loss goes to Costing P&L. Rectification cost of defectives is added to the batch when normal, and charged to Costing P&L when abnormal.

Formula Economic Batch Quantity
Measures

EOQ logic applied to production: the batch size minimising set-up plus carrying cost.

Formula

EBQ = sqrt( (2 x D x S) / C )

Terms

D = annual demand in units. S = set-up cost per batch. C = carrying cost per unit per annum.

Watch out

S is set-up cost per batch, not per unit. On D = 24,000 units, S = ₹360 and C = ₹3, EBQ = sqrt(57,60,000) = 2,400 units, so 10 batches a year: set-up cost 10 x 360 = ₹3,600 and carrying cost 1,200 x 3 = ₹3,600. Equal, as at the EOQ.

Process and operation costing§

Cost is collected process by process in a process account and transferred on. Three loss situations, and the whole topic turns on telling them apart:

  • Normal loss — the expected, unavoidable loss. Its units are entered on the credit side at scrap or realisable value only, never at cost, so the cost of the lost units is borne by the good units. This is what raises cost per unit.
  • Abnormal loss — actual loss beyond normal. Valued at the full cost per good unit and taken out of the process to the Abnormal Loss Account, which recovers scrap value and sends the balance to Costing P&L.
  • Abnormal gain — actual loss less than normal. Valued at the same full cost per good unit, debited to the process and credited to the Abnormal Gain Account; that account is debited with the scrap value the entity failed to realise on the shortfall in normal loss, and the balance goes to Costing P&L.
Formula Cost per good unit in a process
Formula

Cost per unit = (Total process cost − Scrap value of normal loss) / (Units introduced − Normal loss units)

Terms

Total process cost is material introduced plus the transfer from the previous process plus wages plus production overhead. Units introduced means the whole input to that process, so for a second or later process it is the units transferred in plus any material added there.

Watch out

The denominator uses expected output, not actual. Compute the rate once and value output, abnormal loss and abnormal gain at that same rate; a rate recomputed on actual output is the standard error here.

Equivalent units convert part-finished work into finished-unit terms so that cost can be split between output and closing work-in-progress, degree of completion being taken separately for material, labour and overhead. With 8,000 units completed and 2,000 in closing WIP that are complete for material but half complete for labour and overhead, equivalent units are 10,000 and 9,000; material of ₹1,00,000 gives ₹10 a unit and labour with overhead of ₹45,000 gives ₹5, so output is 8,000 x ₹15 = ₹1,20,000 and closing WIP is (2,000 x 10) + (1,000 x 5) = ₹25,000. The two add back to ₹1,45,000.

Verify: whether your attempt's material works equivalent units on the FIFO or the weighted average basis where opening WIP exists — the two give different answers and the question usually names one. Confirm against the current Study Guidelines.

Inter-process profit arises where each process transfers output at cost plus a margin, so that management can compare a process against an outside quotation. It complicates the accounts in one specific way: the closing stock held in a later process contains unrealised profit, which must be removed to state stock at cost. On a transfer price of ₹1,20,000 that includes ₹20,000 profit, closing stock of ₹30,000 carries 30,000 x 20,000 / 1,20,000 = ₹5,000 of unrealised profit.

Joint products and by-products§

Joint products are two or more products of significant sales value produced together from the same process; a by-product is of minor value beside the main product. The line between them is relative sales value, and it decides the accounting.

Joint cost is apportioned up to the point of separation by physical units, average unit cost, a survey or weighted points method scoring products by effort or technical difficulty, market value at separation, net realisable value (market value after further processing less that processing and selling cost), or contribution margin, splitting variable joint cost on physical units and fixed joint cost on contribution. Cost incurred after separation is never joint; it belongs to the product that incurred it.

By-products are handled by non-cost methods — net realisable value credited to the main process cost, or by-product income shown as other income — or by cost methods, giving the by-product a share of joint cost at standard cost, opportunity cost, or a reverse-cost calculation from its selling price.

Costing of service sectors§

Service costing has no material or work-in-progress to speak of; the skill is choosing the cost unit and building operating cost by element. Composite units are the core: tonne-kilometre, passenger-kilometre, patient-day, room-day, kilowatt-hour. Cost is grouped into standing charges, maintenance charges and running charges, and price is cost per unit plus the required margin.

✦Quick recallA lorry carries 10 tonnes for 40 km, then 6 tonnes for 60 km. What are the absolute and the commercial tonne-kilometres?Answer it in your head first

Absolute tonne-km weights each leg by its own load: (10 x 40) + (6 x 60) = 400 + 360 = 760 tonne-km. Commercial tonne-km uses the average load over the whole distance: ((10 + 6) / 2) x 100 = 800 tonne-km. The absolute figure is the more accurate and gives the higher cost per tonne-km; using the wrong one is a favourite one-mark trap.

Practise it Write the credit side of a process account where output is less than expected, naming every item and how each is valued.
✦Model answerCompare against what a full-mark answer containsWrite above to unseal

Three items, each with its own valuation rule:

  1. Normal loss — at scrap or realisable value only. Its units appear, its cost does not, which is how the loss gets absorbed by good output.
  2. Abnormal loss — at full cost per good unit, from (total cost − normal loss scrap) / (input − normal loss units).
  3. Output transferred — the balance of units, at that same rate.

Then check that units and rupees agree with the debit side. If they do not, the cause is usually normal loss valued at cost, or a rate recomputed on actual rather than expected output. Where output exceeds expectation there is no abnormal loss line and an abnormal gain appears on the debit side at the same rate.

Remember this Normal loss is valued at scrap value; everything abnormal is valued at full cost per good unit.

That single sentence decides the process account, and the rate must come from expected output. Say it before you start writing, and the account balances itself.

Exam trap Reducing the normal loss entry when there is an abnormal gain

An abnormal gain means fewer units were lost than expected, so less scrap was realised than the normal-loss line assumes. The normal loss entry is not reduced; instead the Abnormal Gain Account is debited with the scrap lost on the shortfall, and only the net balance reaches Costing P&L. Netting it off inside the process account gives the right total in the wrong account.

Unit 4 — Cost Control and Analysis§

Standard costing: setting standards§

A standard cost is predetermined on a standard cost card — standard quantity at standard price for each element — set from specifications, time study, wage agreements and expected activity. Four types: ideal (perfect conditions, so demotivating), normal (average over a trade cycle), basic (a fixed trend base) and current or expected, which control uses. As performance measurement it asks what the actual output should have cost and splits the gap into variances that each point at one manager.

Remember this A positive variance is favourable and a negative one adverse. Label every answer F or A.

In every material, labour and variable overhead variance the standard-based figure is written first and the actual subtracted, and so it is for fixed overhead expenditure (budgeted − actual) and volume (standard for actual output − budgeted). The exception is fixed overhead capacity, written (actual hours worked − budgeted hours). Take the order from the formula as printed; only the sign convention never changes.

One card, one period, so every variance ties into one reconciliation.

Formula Material and labour variances
Formula

Material cost = Std cost of actual output − Actual cost Material price = Actual quantity x (Std price − Actual price) Material usage = Std price x (Std quantity for actual output − Actual quantity) Material mix = Std price x (Actual input in std proportion − Actual quantity) Material yield = Std cost per unit of input x (Std input for actual output − Actual input) Labour cost = Std cost of actual output − Actual wages Labour rate = Hours paid x (Std rate − Actual rate) Idle time = Idle hours x Std rate (always adverse) Labour efficiency = Std rate x (Std hours for actual output − Actual hours worked)

Terms

Price + usage = material cost; mix + yield = usage; rate + idle + efficiency = labour cost.

Watch out

Rate is on hours paid, efficiency on hours worked; use paid hours for efficiency and idle time counts twice, so the three will not sum.

Formula Overhead variances
Formula

Variable OH cost = (Std hours for actual output x Std rate) − Actual variable OH Variable OH expenditure = (Actual hours worked x Std rate) − Actual variable OH Variable OH efficiency = Std rate x (Std hours for actual output − Actual hours worked) Fixed OH cost = (Actual output x Std fixed OH per unit) − Actual fixed OH Fixed OH expenditure = Budgeted fixed OH − Actual fixed OH Fixed OH volume = (Actual output x Std fixed OH per unit) − Budgeted fixed OH Fixed OH capacity = Std rate per hour x (Actual hours worked − Budgeted hours) Fixed OH efficiency = Std rate per hour x (Std hours for actual output − Actual hours worked)

Terms

Expenditure + efficiency = variable overhead variance; expenditure + volume = fixed overhead variance; capacity + efficiency = volume.

Watch out

Only fixed overhead has a volume variance, because only fixed overhead is spread over a budgeted volume. Capacity is the one measured against budgeted hours, so it turns adverse when fewer hours are run than planned.

Verify: whether your attempt's material splits volume into capacity and efficiency only, or also expects a calendar variance where actual working days differ from budgeted.

Marginal costing§

Marginal costing charges only variable cost to the product and treats fixed cost as a period cost, so contribution = sales − variable cost carries every short-term decision. Finished goods and work-in-progress are valued at variable cost only — the whole source of the profit difference against absorption costing.

Formula Contribution, break-even and margin of safety
Formula

P/V ratio = Contribution / Sales Break-even (units) = Fixed cost / Contribution per unit Break-even (value) = Fixed cost / P/V ratio Margin of safety = Actual sales − Break-even sales Profit = (Sales x P/V ratio) − Fixed cost = Margin of safety x P/V ratio Sales for a target profit = (Fixed cost + Target profit) / P/V ratio

Terms

On the break-even chart the angle of incidence is the angle at which the sales line cuts the total cost line — a wide angle means profit accrues quickly beyond break-even. A profit-volume chart plots profit against sales instead, cutting the axis at break-even.

Watch out

For multiproduct break-even work in a composite unit — contribution per bundle of the given mix. Break-even moves when the mix changes, so state the mix assumed.

Reconciling the methods: the profit gap is the fixed overhead in the stock movement. On 2,000 units produced against 1,800 sold, with fixed overhead of ₹100 a unit, the 200 units of closing stock carry ₹20,000, so absorption profit is ₹20,000 higher; where stock falls it is lower, and where stock is unchanged the two agree.

Short-term decisions reduce to contribution. Make or buy compares the buying price with the variable cost of making, plus avoidable fixed cost and contribution lost on displaced work. Discontinuation: keep a product while its contribution is positive. With a limiting factor, rank by contribution per unit of that factor, not per unit of product.

Budget and budgetary control§

A budget is a quantitative plan for a defined future period; budgetary control monitors actual against it, isolates variances and acts. Essentials: prepared in advance, tied to an objective, expressed in money or quantity, owned by the managers held to it. The budget manual records responsibilities, forms, timetable and procedures, and budgeting begins from the principal or key budget factor — the constraint, usually sales, that limits everything else.

Functional budgets — sales, production, material, labour, overhead, capital expenditure and non-operating functions such as administration and research — feed the cash budget, and all consolidate into the master budget. Zero-based budgeting justifies every rupee from zero each period through ranked decision packages instead of extrapolating last year: thorough, expensive in time. A performance budget links spending to output achieved. Budgets also motivate: attainable targets lift performance, ideal or imposed ones invite budgetary slack.

Feedback control compares actual with budget after the event and corrects next period; feed-forward control compares a forecast outcome with the budget and corrects before it. A cash budget prepared three months ahead so an overdraft can be arranged is feed-forward; a variance report is feedback.

✦Model answerCompare against what a full-mark answer containsWrite above to unseal

Each compares a different pair, so take the order from the formula rather than from a general rule:

  • Cost = (actual output x standard fixed OH per unit) − actual fixed overhead.
  • Expenditure = budgeted − actual fixed overhead: did it spend its allowance?
  • Volume = (actual output x standard fixed OH per unit) − budgeted fixed overhead: did the volume materialise?
  • Capacity = standard rate x (actual hours worked − budgeted hours); efficiency = standard rate x (standard hours for actual output − actual hours worked).

Capacity and efficiency add to volume, because volume falls short for two reasons only: fewer hours run than budgeted, or hours producing less than standard. Expenditure plus volume gives the total, and only fixed overhead has a volume variance.

✦Quick recallWhy can a product showing a loss under absorption costing be worth keeping?Answer it in your head first

Because that loss includes apportioned fixed overhead which continues whether or not the product is made. While sales exceed variable cost, the contribution reduces the fixed cost the rest of the business carries, so discontinuing cuts profit by that contribution less any fixed cost saved.

Exam trap Reading the fixed overhead volume variance as overspending

Overspending the allowance is the expenditure variance; failing to produce the budgeted volume, so that fixed overhead is under-recovered, is the volume variance. A department can spend exactly its allowance and still show a large adverse volume variance because output fell.
