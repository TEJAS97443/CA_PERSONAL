Unit 1 — Financial Management and Financial Analysis§

Principal

Profit is up 18%. The bank has refused to renew the working capital limit. Explain that.

You

If profit is up, the bank is being unreasonable.

Principal

The bank lends against cash and the balance sheet, not against profit. Find where the profit went.

Margin note

Unit 1 in one exchange. Profit maximisation and wealth maximisation are not the same objective, and ratios say which one the entity is achieving.

The finance function and its objective§

Financial management plans, raises, controls and administers funds. Its scope is three decisions and one constraint: the financing decision (how much, from where, in what mix — Unit 2), the investment and dividend decisions (Unit 3), and the liquidity decision (Unit 4).

Profit maximisationWealth maximisation
MeasureAccounting profit or EPSMarket value of the owners' stake
TimingIndifferent to when profit arrivesDiscounts for timing
RiskIgnoredPriced in the discount rate
BasisAccrual profit, an accounting figureCash flow, an economic figure
VaguenessWhich profit — pre-tax or post, short or long run?One number, quoted daily
VerdictA yardstick, not an objectiveThe accepted objective

The three objections to profit maximisation compress to one phrase: timing, risk and vagueness.

Value creation is wealth maximisation made operational — value is created when return on capital employed exceeds the cost of that capital. Unit 2 measures that cost, Unit 3 tests projects against it, Unit 4 asks the same of money tied up in stock and debtors.

Remember this Value is created only when return on capital employed exceeds the cost of capital. Growth financed above its own return destroys wealth while still raising profit.

Funds ploughed into projects earning 9% while capital costs 12% raise profit and lower market value. That asymmetry is the mark when a question contrasts the two objectives.

Financial manager against financial controller, examined as a contrast:

Financial ManagerFinancial Controller

Functions of finance executives: estimating fund requirements; deciding capital structure; selecting sources; investment of funds; maintaining liquidity; disposal of surplus and the dividend decision; financial negotiation; evaluating performance; and tracking the company's own share price.

The financial management environment is where those decisions are taken: the money market for short-term instruments, the capital market for long-term funds, the intermediaries channelling savings into investment, and the regulatory, tax and interest-rate framework. The firm is largely a price-taker here — the cost of capital in Unit 2 is set in these markets, not chosen.

Financial distress and insolvency§

Financial distress is the condition in which cash flows are insufficient to meet obligations. It is a matter of degree, running from mild liquidity strain through stretched creditors and breached covenants to insolvency. Insolvency takes two forms and questions turn on the distinction: cash-flow (technical) insolvency, where debts cannot be paid as they fall due even though assets exceed liabilities; and balance-sheet insolvency, where liabilities exceed the realisable value of assets.

The costs of distress split the way marks do. Direct — legal, administrative and professional fees, and management time spent on creditors. Indirect — customers and suppliers withdraw, credit tightens, staff leave, assets are sold below value, new finance costs more. The indirect usually exceed the direct.

Verify: Section A treats distress and insolvency as a financial condition and its costs. The statutory resolution machinery sits elsewhere — check the Study Guidelines for your attempt before adding legal detail to an FM answer.

Financial analysis through ratios§

Users, each paired with their question: management (control and decisions), investors (return and risk), lenders (security and servicing capacity), suppliers (short-term solvency), employees (stability), government (compliance and tax), analysts and rating agencies.

Sources of financial data: the balance sheet and statement of profit and loss, the cash flow statement, notes and accounting policies, the directors' report and management discussion; and internally, budgets, cost records and management reports, plus industry data to compare against.

Formula Return on Capital Employed
Measures

The return earned on all long-term funds in the business, before deciding who gets it.

Formula

ROCE = (EBIT / Capital employed) x 100 Capital employed = Shareholders' funds + Long-term debt = Total assets - Current liabilities

Terms

EBIT = earnings before interest and tax. Shareholders' funds = equity + preference capital + reserves, less accumulated losses.

Watch out

The numerator is before interest, because interest is a return to one of the suppliers of the capital in the denominator. PAT over capital employed mixes a post-interest return with a pre-interest base — the commonest error here.

Formula Return on equity, decomposed (DuPont)
Measures

Splits the owners' return into a trading, an efficiency and a gearing effect.

Formula

ROE = PAT / Shareholders' funds = (PAT / Sales) x (Sales / Total assets) x (Total assets / Shareholders' funds) = Net profit margin x Total asset turnover x Equity multiplier

Terms

Where preference shares exist, return on equity funds uses PAT less preference dividend over equity shareholders' funds only.

Watch out

The three factors must multiply back to the direct ROE; if not, you have mixed average and closing bases. An ROE rising only through the third factor is a gearing story, not an improvement.

Formula Interest coverage and debt service coverage
Formula

Interest coverage = EBIT / Interest DSCR = (PAT + Depreciation + Interest on term loan) / (Interest on term loan + Principal instalment)

Watch out

Both are "times", never percentages. DSCR adds principal repayment, which is not an expense and so is absent from profit, and adds back non-cash depreciation. Comfortable interest coverage with a DSCR below one is the company in the dialogue.

Check

Three checks. The DuPont product must return the direct ROE to the decimal — it does. ROCE (30%) exceeds the pre-tax cost of debt (12%), so gearing works for the owners, consistent with ROE landing close to ROCE despite tax. And collection (29 days) is faster than payment (46 days), so suppliers finance the firm — if yours reversed, check which turnover you divided by which.

Exam trap Mixing closing balances with averages inside one answer

Turnover ratios are a flow over an average stock, so averages are preferable and questions often say "average inventory". Given only a closing balance sheet, closing figures are the only honest basis — state the assumption once and hold it across every ratio. The mark is for the disclosure and the consistency. Same for 365 against 360 days: pick one, say so, never switch.

✦Quick recallDebtor velocity is 29 days but the stated credit policy is 60 days. Is the ratio wrong?Answer it in your head first

No, and the finding is favourable. Always compare a ratio against policy, prior year and industry — alone it is a number, not a diagnosis. The reverse case is the examinable one: collection beyond policy points to weak credit control and possibly unrecognised bad debts.

Limitations of ratio analysis is a stock question: ratios are historical and say nothing about the future; no single correct standard exists to compare against; accounting policy differences on depreciation and inventory destroy comparability; price-level changes distort trends; window dressing flatters liquidity; a balance sheet ratio is a single-date snapshot hiding seasonality; aggregation conceals the mix inside a total; qualitative factors are invisible; and a ratio shows a symptom, never a cause.

Practise it Net profit ratio has fallen from 10% to 8% while ROE has risen from 24% to 27%. Explain how both can be true, and state which ratios you would compute next.
✦Model answerCompare against what a full-mark answer containsWrite above to unseal

Write the identity first — ROE = net profit margin x total asset turnover x equity multiplier — and the rest is mechanical: with margin down, ROE still rises if assets turn faster, or if the equity multiplier has risen through more debt or a return of capital. Then name the next computations:

  • Total asset turnover and the individual turnover ratios test the efficiency story; a genuine improvement shows as higher inventory and receivable turnover.
  • Debt–equity, interest coverage and DSCR test the gearing story. If the multiplier did the work, ROE rose because risk rose.
  • ROCE is the referee, being unaffected by financing mix. Rising ROCE supports efficiency; ROCE flat while ROE rises is gearing.

Close with the sentence examiners look for: an ROE gain achieved purely through leverage is not value creation, because the equity holder's required return rises with gearing.

Margin note

The equity holder is paid last and so demands the most, and interest is deductible while dividend is not. That is the spine of the unit.

Methods of raising long-term finance: public issue, rights issue, private placement and preferential allotment, offer for sale, debentures or bonds, term loans and deferred credit, and internal accruals.

Short-term sources: trade credit and accrued expenses (spontaneous, and free unless a discount is forgone), bank finance (cash credit, overdraft, bills discounting, working capital demand loan, letter of credit), commercial paper, certificates of deposit, inter-corporate deposits, public deposits, factoring, and advances from customers.

Contemporary sources of funding are examined descriptively, so carry one clause each. P2P lending — a platform matches individual lenders directly with borrowers, cutting out the bank. Crowd funding — many small contributors fund a venture through a platform, in donation, reward, debt or equity form. Equity funding — a stake issued to angel investors, private equity or strategic investors. Start-up funding — the staged ladder from bootstrapping and family money through incubators, accelerators and angel rounds to venture capital, with government schemes alongside.

Internal funds are retained earnings plus funds released by depreciation: no issue cost, no dilution — but not free, because retained earnings carry an opportunity cost equal to what shareholders could have earned, so their cost is the cost of equity.

International sources: American and Global Depository Receipts, Foreign Currency Convertible Bonds, External Commercial Borrowings, foreign currency term loans, Euro issues, export credit, and foreign direct or portfolio investment.

Other sources. Lease financing gives use without ownership; a finance lease transfers substantially all risks and rewards, an operating lease does not. Sale and lease back — the owner sells an asset and immediately leases it back, freeing locked-up capital while keeping the use of it. Convertible debt — debt carrying a right to convert into equity: cheap while debt, dilutive once converted. Venture capital — equity or quasi-equity in unlisted high-risk ventures, with active involvement and a planned exit. Grants — non-repayable, usually purpose-restricted.

Cost of capital§

The cost of capital is the minimum return a project must earn to leave shareholders no worse off. It is the discount rate in Unit 3, the benchmark for value creation in Unit 1, and the quantity minimised in capital structure design. Factors: the general level of interest rates, the tax regime, capital structure and financial policy, business and financial risk, dividend policy, and issue costs.

Say this once and never break it. In WACC, debt is taken after tax, because interest is deductible in computing taxable profit, so the firm parts with only I(1 - t). Equity and preference are not, because dividends are an appropriation of post-tax profit and carry no shield. Multiplying Ke or Kp by (1 − t) is a guaranteed lost mark.

Formula Cost of equity — dividend growth model and CAPM
Formula

Ke = D1/P0 + g with issue cost: Ke = D1/NP + g D1 = D0 x (1 + g) Ke = Rf + Beta x (Rm - Rf)

Terms

g = constant dividend growth = retention ratio (b) x return on equity (r).

Watch out

D1, not D0, goes in the numerator. If the risk premium is given, do not subtract Rf from it again — the commonest CAPM slip. CAPM needs no dividend and no growth, so use it for a non-payer.

Formula Cost of debt and of preference shares
Formula

Kd irredeemable = I(1 - t)/NP (drop the (1 - t) for the pre-tax cost) Kd redeemable = [ I(1 - t) + (RV - NP)/n ] / [ (RV + NP)/2 ] Kp irredeemable = PD/NP Kp redeemable = [ PD + (RV - NP)/n ] / [ (RV + NP)/2 ]

Terms

NP = net proceeds after discount and expenses; RV = redemption value; n = years to redemption.

Watch out

Interest is computed on face value but divided by net proceeds. No (1 − t) appears anywhere in Kp. The redeemable formulas spread the capital gain or loss over the life and over average funds — an approximation to the true after-tax IRR, so say which route you used.

Formula Weighted average and marginal cost of capital
Formula

WACC (Ko) = (We x Ke) + (Wp x Kp) + (Wd x Kd after tax) + (Wr x Kr) Marginal cost of capital = weighted cost of the ADDITIONAL funds, at new costs and new weights only.

Watch out

Weights (book or market) must total exactly 1.0 and every cost must be on the same tax basis. Market weights are preferred because each component cost is itself derived from market prices. Marginal cost is not the new average cost of the enlarged pool — a different figure the question may also want.

Exam trap Giving retained earnings a market value of their own

Retained earnings have a book value but no market quotation — the share price already capitalises them, which is why the market column shows a dash. Some Study Material questions instead ask you to split the market value of equity between capital and reserves in the ratio of their book values; that convention exists and yields the same WACC, both slices carrying the same cost. State which you followed, never both.

Capital structure decisions§

Capital structure is the mix of long-term debt and equity. It moves both WACC and the risk borne by equity; the optimum capital structure is the mix at which WACC is lowest and firm value highest.

Determinants: cost of capital and the tax shield; business risk and stability of earnings; the need to retain control; flexibility and unused debt capacity; lenders' covenants; size and stage of the company; asset structure and available security; market conditions; and the purpose of the finance. Designing an optimum structure means maximising EPS or value while keeping fixed charges serviceable — carry FRICT: flexibility, risk, income, control, timing.

The NOI mechanism deserves one arithmetic line, because it is the argument. With EBIT ₹10,00,000 and Ko 12.5%, value = 10,00,000/0.125 = ₹80,00,000. Add ₹20,00,000 of 10% debt: equity is worth ₹60,00,000, so Ke = (10,00,000 − 2,00,000)/60,00,000 = 13.33%. Recheck the WACC — 0.25 x 10% + 0.75 x 13.33% = 12.5%, unchanged. Cheap debt bought nothing because equity repriced. MM defends the result by arbitrage: an investor can create or undo gearing personally, so two identical firms cannot sell at different prices.

EBIT–EPS analysis chooses between financing plans by comparing EPS at the expected EBIT. The indifference (break-even EBIT) point is the EBIT at which two plans give equal EPS. The financial break-even EBIT is a different thing — the EBIT at which EPS is exactly zero, equal to interest plus preference dividend grossed up by (1 − t).

Remember this Above the indifference EBIT, gear up; below it, issue equity. Debt magnifies EPS in both directions, so the choice is really a judgement on how firm the EBIT forecast is.

Worked: to raise ₹40,00,000 on 2,00,000 existing shares, Plan A issues 40,000 shares at ₹100 and Plan B issues 10% debt; tax 30%. Setting EBIT(1−t)/2,40,000 = (EBIT − 4,00,000)(1−t)/2,00,000 gives EBIT = ₹24,00,000, where both yield EPS of exactly ₹7.00. Plan B's financial break-even EBIT is ₹4,00,000.

Over- and under-capitalisation compare earnings with capital employed, and candidates reverse them. Over-capitalised: capital employed exceeds what earnings justify, the earning rate is below normal, book value of shares exceeds real value — caused by over-issue of capital, assets bought at inflated prices, promotion in a boom, or inadequate depreciation; remedied by reorganising capital, redeeming debt and writing down assets. Under-capitalised: earnings are high relative to capital employed, the earning rate is above normal, real value exceeds book value — caused by under-estimating requirements, conservative dividends building reserves, or high efficiency; remedied by bonus shares, share splits or a higher dividend.

Leverages§

Formula The three leverages and the identity between them
Formula

DOL = Contribution / EBIT = %change in EBIT / %change in Sales DFL = EBIT / PBT = %change in EPS / %change in EBIT DFL with preference shares = EBIT / [ EBIT - I - Dp/(1 - t) ] DCL = DOL x DFL = %change in EPS / %change in Sales DCL = Contribution / PBT <-- ONLY when there is no preference dividend DCL with preference shares = Contribution / [ EBIT - I - Dp/(1 - t) ]

Terms

Contribution = Sales − Variable cost. PBT = EBIT − Interest. Dp = preference dividend.

Watch out

Operating leverage comes from fixed operating cost, financial leverage from fixed financial charges. A firm with no fixed cost has DOL of 1 and one with no debt has DFL of 1 — leverage is a multiplier, so its neutral value is one, never zero. DCL is a product, never a sum.

The shortcut Contribution / PBT is only valid when Dp = 0, and this is the trap most revision sheets get wrong. It comes from cancelling EBIT out of (Contribution/EBIT) x (EBIT/PBT) — but the moment a preference dividend exists, DFL is no longer EBIT/PBT, so there is no EBIT to cancel and the shortcut silently understates DCL. Use %change in EPS / %change in Sales as your check; it is the one route that is always right, because DCL is defined by the effect on EPS and preference dividend reduces earnings available to equity.

Operating leverage. DOL = 4,00,000/2,00,000 = 2.00. Check: EBIT is up 20% on a 10% sales rise, so 20/10 = 2 ✓.

Financial leverage — and this is where the preference dividend bites. The dividend is paid out of post-tax profit, so it must be grossed up before it can sit alongside pre-tax interest: 21,000/0.70 = ₹30,000. Then

DFL = 2,00,000 / (2,00,000 − 50,000 − 30,000) = 2,00,000/1,20,000 = 1.6667

not EBIT/PBT = 2,00,000/1,50,000 = 1.3333. Check it against EPS: EPS rises 33.33% while EBIT rises 20%, and 33.33/20 = 1.6667 ✓. The grossed-up figure is right and EBIT/PBT is wrong here.

Combined leverage. DCL = DOL x DFL = 2.00 x 1.6667 = 3.3333. Check against the percentages: EPS is up 33.33% on a 10% sales rise, so 33.33/10 = 3.3333 ✓.

Now notice what you must not do. Contribution/PBT = 4,00,000/1,50,000 = 2.6667, and that is not the answer to this question. The shortcut assumes DFL is EBIT/PBT so that EBIT cancels out of the product — which stops being true the moment a preference dividend exists. The correct direct route here is

DCL = Contribution / [ EBIT − I − Dp/(1 − t) ] = 4,00,000/1,20,000 = 3.3333 ✓

Three routes, one answer. The 26.67% rise in PBT is a red herring: it gives you nothing, because DCL is defined on EPS, and between PBT and EPS sits the preference dividend.

  Neutral value of every leverage is 1.0, not 0. DCL is a product, never a sum.

Unit 3 — Capital Investment and Dividend Decisions§

Capital investment decisions commit large sums, are largely irreversible, and bind the firm for years, so the objective is to accept only projects returning more than the capital funding them costs, and to rank projects when funds are short. Two rules govern everything here: work with incremental cash flows after tax, never accounting profit, and ignore sunk costs while including opportunity costs. Depreciation enters only through its tax saving, not being a cash outflow.

Formula Payback, discounted payback and Accounting Rate of Return
Formula

Payback period = Years fully recovered + (Unrecovered cost / Cash flow of next year) Discounted payback = the same, on cash flows already discounted at the cost of capital ARR = (Average annual accounting profit after tax / Investment) x 100 Average investment = (Initial outlay + Salvage value) / 2

Terms

Accounting profit = cash flow − depreciation. With even flows, payback = Outlay / Annual inflow.

Watch out

Payback uses cash flows, ARR uses profit after depreciation — mixing the two is the commonest error in this unit. Payback ignores everything after the cut-off and ignores the time value of money; discounted payback fixes the second defect, not the first.

Verify: the ARR denominator is a genuine split — some editions divide by average investment, others by initial investment, and the answers differ by a factor of two. Average investment is more commonly expected in ICAI solutions. State your base, and confirm it for your attempt.

Formula Net Present Value and Profitability Index
Formula

NPV = Sum of [ Cash inflow of year t / (1 + k)^t ] - Initial outlay PI (Benefit-Cost Ratio) = PV of future cash inflows / Initial outlay

Terms

k = cost of capital. PI = 1 + (NPV / Initial outlay).

Watch out

Accept if NPV is positive. PI and NPV always agree on accept-or-reject, PI exceeding 1 exactly when NPV exceeds 0 — but they can disagree on ranking, NPV measuring rupees created and PI rupees created per rupee invested.

Strengths of NPV: uses all cash flows, discounts them properly, is in absolute rupees and so is additive across projects, and uses the cost of capital directly. Limitations: needs an estimate of the cost of capital; is unintuitive to non-finance managers; ignores the size of the outlay (₹10,000 of NPV counts the same on a ₹1 lakh project as on a ₹1 crore one); and assumes intermediate flows are reinvested at the cost of capital.

The working capital adjustment in NPV has three rules: the working capital is an outflow when committed, it is not depreciated, and it is recovered as an inflow at the end of the life, untaxed, because releasing stock and debtors is not income.

Verify: conventions vary. Some questions inject the whole amount at time zero and release it in the terminal year; others add increments year by year as sales grow; a few do not recover it at all. Follow the question, say so in one line, and check the Study Material for your attempt.

Formula Internal Rate of Return and Modified IRR
Formula

IRR = the rate at which NPV = 0 By interpolation: IRR = L + [ NPV at L / (NPV at L - NPV at H) ] x (H - L) MIRR: Terminal Value = Sum of [ Cash inflow of year t x (1 + k)^(n - t) ] MIRR = (Terminal Value / Initial outlay)^(1/n) - 1

Terms

L = lower rate (positive NPV), H = higher rate (negative NPV), n = life, k = reinvestment rate.

Watch out

The interpolation denominator is the difference between the two NPVs, which becomes a sum in arithmetic when NPV at H is negative. Interpolation is an approximation — a straight line drawn through a curved NPV profile — so the error shrinks as L and H close in. Bracket the answer within a few percentage points and call the figure approximate.

Dividend decisions§

A dividend distributes earnings to shareholders, so the decision is really about how much to retain — every rupee paid out is a rupee not reinvested. Keep the four instruments apart:

What happensEffect
Cash dividendCash leaves the companyReserves and cash fall; number of shares unchanged
Stock dividend (bonus issue)Reserves are capitalised into new fully paid sharesNo cash moves; shares rise, reserves fall, EPS and price per share fall proportionately, total wealth unchanged
Stock splitThe face value of each share is subdividedShares rise, capital unchanged, reserves unchanged; price falls proportionately; improves marketability
Share buy-backThe company purchases its own sharesCash and shares both fall; EPS rises on the reduced base; a way of returning surplus cash and signalling confidence

Determinants of dividend: level and stability of earnings; liquidity, since profit is not cash; access to and cost of external finance; investment opportunities and their return relative to Ke; shareholders' expectations and the shareholder base; the desire for a stable payout; contractual and statutory restrictions; signalling; and shareholders' tax position.

Formula Walter's model and Gordon's model
Formula

Walter: P = [ D + (r/Ke)(E - D) ] / Ke Gordon: P0 = E1 x (1 - b) / (Ke - b x r) where growth g = b x r

Terms

P = price per share, D = dividend per share, E = earnings per share, r = return on retained earnings, Ke = cost of equity, b = retention ratio.

Watch out

Both models make payout relevant and both reach the same conclusion. When r > Ke (growth firm) price is highest at zero payout; when r < Ke (declining firm) at 100% payout; when r = Ke payout is irrelevant. Gordon's formula breaks down once b x r reaches Ke, the denominator turning zero or negative — say so rather than reporting an absurd price.

Remember this With E = ₹10, r = 15%, Ke = 12%, Walter gives ₹104.17 at zero payout, ₹91.67 at a ₹6 dividend and ₹83.33 at full payout. Reverse r and Ke and the ordering reverses exactly.

Check the middle figure: [6 + (0.15/0.12)(10 − 6)]/0.12 = 11/0.12 = ₹91.67. Gordon on the same firm at b = 40%: g = 6%, P0 = 6/(0.12 − 0.06) = ₹100; at b = 60%: g = 9%, P0 = 4/0.03 = ₹133.33 — higher retention, higher price, because r beats Ke. Note that Walter's E is the current EPS while Gordon's E1 is next year's; a question giving a single earnings figure means both, as here, and if it gives E0 and a growth rate, take E1 = E0(1 + g) for Gordon.

The traditional approach (Graham and Dodd) also treats dividends as relevant, weighting the dividend more heavily than retained earnings in the market price. MM's hypothesis says the opposite: in a perfect market with no taxes or transaction costs and a given investment policy, dividend policy is irrelevant — a shareholder wanting cash can sell shares, one who does not can reinvest. Pay a dividend and the firm must raise the same sum elsewhere, the fall in terminal price exactly offsetting the dividend.

Practise it A company has 10,000 shares at ₹100, Ke 10%, expected earnings ₹1,00,000 and planned investment ₹1,50,000. Prove MM's irrelevance by computing the value of the firm when it pays a ₹10 dividend and when it pays nothing.
✦Model answerCompare against what a full-mark answer containsWrite above to unseal

Use the two MM relations: the year-end price P1 = P0(1 + Ke) − D1, and the funds to be raised by a fresh issue = Investment − (Earnings − Total dividend), so the number of new shares is that amount divided by P1. Then value the existing shares as

n x P0 = [ (n + Δn) x P1 − (Investment − Earnings) ] / (1 + Ke)

Case 1, dividend ₹10 per share. P1 = 100(1.10) − 10 = ₹100. Dividend paid = ₹1,00,000, so retained earnings are nil and the whole ₹1,50,000 must be raised: Δn = 1,50,000/100 = 1,500 shares. n x P0 = [(10,000 + 1,500) x 100 − (1,50,000 − 1,00,000)]/1.10 = (11,50,000 − 50,000)/1.10 = ₹10,00,000.

Case 2, no dividend. P1 = 100(1.10) − 0 = ₹110. Earnings of ₹1,00,000 are retained, so only ₹50,000 need be raised: Δn = 50,000/110 = 454.55 shares. n x P0 = [(10,000 + 454.55) x 110 − 50,000]/1.10 = (11,50,000 − 50,000)/1.10 = ₹10,00,000.

Identical, and the identity is the answer: paying the dividend transferred ₹1,00,000 of value from the terminal share price into the shareholders' pockets and forced the company to sell 1,500 shares to strangers instead of 455. Total wealth did not move.

Close by naming the assumptions, where the remaining marks are: perfect capital markets with costless information, no taxes, no transaction or floatation costs, a fixed investment policy, and certainty of future earnings. Relax any one — taxes and issue costs especially — and dividend policy becomes relevant again, which is why Walter and Gordon exist.

✦Quick recallA company announces a bonus issue and its share price halves the next day. Have shareholders lost money?Answer it in your head first

No. A bonus issue capitalises reserves into shares: the share count rises, the price per share falls proportionately, each holder's total value is unchanged, and no cash has left the company — which is why a bonus is no substitute for a cash dividend, and why a stock split produces the same price effect by a different route (face value, not reserves).

Unit 4 — Management of Working Capital§

Principal

The client is profitable, has a full order book, and could not pay salaries on the 7th. How?

You

Their debtors are slow.

Principal

Debtors are slow, stock is high and the suppliers have stopped waiting. Put a number on it.

Margin note

The number is the cash conversion cycle. This unit is examined as arithmetic, not opinion.

Gross working capital is total current assets; net working capital is current assets less current liabilities. Split it another way and the financing question appears: permanent (core) working capital is the minimum level always tied up, and temporary (fluctuating) working capital rises and falls with the season or the order book.

Liquidity against profitability is the trade-off the unit turns on. Holding more current assets, and financing them long term, makes the firm safe and less profitable: idle cash earns little, generous credit buys sales at a cost, large stocks avoid stock-outs and tie up funds. Holding less does the opposite. Three financing strategies name the positions:

StrategyHow it financesEffect
ConservativeLong-term funds cover permanent needs and part of the fluctuating needLow risk, low return; surplus funds in slack season
AggressiveShort-term funds cover the fluctuating need and part of the permanent needHigh return, high refinancing and interest-rate risk
Moderate (matching or hedging)Long-term funds match permanent needs, short-term funds match fluctuating needsMaturity of the finance matches the life of the asset

Primary and secondary sources of liquidity must be kept apart. Primary sources are the ordinary, low-cost, day-to-day ones: cash and near-cash balances, cash generated by trading, trade credit taken, and undrawn bank limits. Secondary sources are reached for when the primary ones are exhausted: renegotiating or rescheduling debt, factoring or securitising receivables, liquidating inventory at a discount, selling long-term assets, and ultimately insolvency proceedings. The distinction is not merely descriptive — using a secondary source changes the firm's operations or capital structure and signals distress; using a primary source does not.

The operating cycle and the cash conversion cycle§

Formula The operating cycle, the cash conversion cycle and the funds they need
Formula

Raw material period = (Average raw material stock / Annual RM consumption) x 365 Work-in-progress period = (Average WIP / Annual cost of production) x 365 Finished goods period = (Average finished goods / Annual cost of goods sold) x 365 Receivables period = (Average debtors / Annual credit sales) x 365 Payables period = (Average creditors / Annual credit purchases) x 365

Gross operating cycle = RM period + WIP period + FG period + Receivables period Net operating cycle (cash conversion cycle) = Gross operating cycle - Payables period Number of operating cycles a year = 365 / Net operating cycle Working capital required = Total operating cost / Number of operating cycles a year

Terms

Each numerator is a stock (a balance), each denominator a flow (a year's turnover). Use averages where opening and closing figures are given, closing balances otherwise.

Watch out

Every ratio must pair a stock with its own flow — finished goods against cost of goods sold, not sales; debtors against credit sales; creditors against credit purchases, not consumption. Pairing debtors with cost of sales, or stock with sales, loses the whole answer.

Verify: conventions differ. Many texts define the operating cycle as inventory period plus receivables period and reserve "cash conversion cycle" for that figure minus payables — the same arithmetic under different names, as above. Some questions use 360 days rather than 365. Follow the question's basis, state it, and check the Study Material for your attempt.

Assessment of the working capital requirement§

Three approaches are examined. The estimation of components method builds current assets and liabilities up line by line, valuing stock and debtors at cost and adding a stated safety margin — noting that debtors may be taken at cost or at selling price depending on the question, and that cash cost excludes depreciation. The percentage of sales method applies a historical ratio of working capital to sales: quick and crude. The operating cycle method is the one just worked. Whichever is used, state whether you worked on a total cost or a cash cost basis.

Receivables, payables and credit management§

Credit granting assesses the applicant — character, capacity, capital, collateral, conditions — then sets the credit period, any early-payment discount and the credit limit. Monitoring uses the average collection period, an ageing schedule of receivables by days outstanding, and receivables as a proportion of sales. Collection escalates from statements and reminders to telephone follow-up, withdrawal of credit, an agency, and legal action. The governing rule: relax credit only while the incremental contribution exceeds the incremental cost of bad debts, discounts, administration and the funds tied up.

Formula The cost of forgoing a cash discount
Formula

Cost (% per annum) = [ d / (100 - d) ] x [ 365 / (Credit period - Discount period) ] x 100

Terms

d = discount percentage offered for early payment.

Watch out

This is the implicit annual interest in not taking a discount, usually far above any borrowing rate — on 2/10, net 45 it is (2/98) x (365/35) = 21.28% a year, so a firm that can borrow more cheaply should always take the discount. Read the same formula the other way to price the discount you offer your own customers.

Managing payables is the mirror image: trade credit is free while no discount is forgone, so take the full period offered — but stretching beyond it costs goodwill, invites supply interruption and eventually cash-on-delivery terms. Compare the cost of forgoing the discount with the firm's own cost of short-term finance and choose the cheaper.

Cash and treasury management§

Three motives for holding cash: transaction (routine payments), precautionary (a buffer against uncertainty) and speculative (to exploit an opportunity). The control tool is the cash budget, projecting receipts and payments period by period and exposing deficits before they arrive. Cash is then managed by speeding collections (prompt billing, decentralised collection, concentration banking, lock boxes) and controlling disbursements (centralised payment, use of the cheque float, paying on the last permissible day), with genuine surpluses invested short term.

Treasury management owns all of this: liquidity and cash-flow management, banking relationships and the negotiation of facilities, arranging short-term funding, investing surpluses, monitoring the firm's financial exposures and reporting on them. Its aim is the right money in the right place at the right time — no idle surplus, no missed payment.

Verify: the Study Material develops optimum cash balance models — associated with Baumol and with Miller and Orr — under management of cash. Whether they are examinable, and in what depth, is set by the Study Guidelines for your attempt; confirm before spending revision time on them.

Banking norms of working capital finance§

A bank does not finance the whole working capital gap. The borrower contributes a margin from its own long-term funds and the bank funds the balance. Against the security charged — usually stocks and book debts — the bank computes a drawing power: eligible security value, less the stipulated margin, less unpaid creditors for those stocks. Drawings are allowed up to the lower of drawing power and the sanctioned limit, which is why a borrower with an adequate limit can still be unable to draw. The underlying idea is a maximum permissible bank finance — a ceiling derived from the working capital gap after the borrower's own contribution — policed by periodic stock and book-debt statements and an annual review.

Verify: the specific margin percentages, the method by which permissible bank finance is computed, and the eligibility rules for security are matters of banking regulation and lender policy, and they change. This note deliberately states no percentage. Confirm the treatment expected in the Study Material and Study Guidelines for your attempt.

Remember this A shorter cash conversion cycle is free finance. Every day removed releases funds permanently and costs no interest, which is why the cycle is the first thing to compute and the last thing to report.

On the figures above one day is about ₹1,507, so cutting the 48.67-day receivables period to 40 days releases roughly ₹13,000 of the ₹1,60,992 requirement with no new borrowing. Benefit against the cost of achieving it is the form every working capital answer should take.

Decision rules, and where they fight each other.

  • Accept a project if NPV > 0, IRR > cost of capital, PI > 1, payback is within the cut-off, or ARR beats the target. For one conventional project the discounted measures agree.
  • On ranking, prefer NPV — it maximises absolute wealth. Prefer PI only when capital is rationed and projects are divisible; with indivisible projects, test feasible combinations, because PI ranking can leave funds idle and money on the table.
  • Prefer MIRR to IRR where the reinvestment assumption matters, and abandon IRR altogether when cash flows change sign more than once, because multiple IRRs exist.
  • Never rank on payback (ignores everything after the cut-off and, undiscounted, the time value of money) or ARR (uses profit, not cash, and ignores timing) — quote them as supplementary.
  • In financing, accept the geared plan only above the indifference EBIT; in dividends, follow Walter and Gordon's single rule — retain when r > Ke, distribute when r < Ke.

Ratios and what each one diagnoses. Liquidity — current, quick, cash: can short-term obligations be met. Solvency — debt–equity, proprietary, capital gearing, interest coverage, DSCR: how much risk sits with lenders and whether they can be serviced. Activity — inventory, receivables, payables, fixed asset, working capital turnover: how hard the assets are worked and how long cash is locked up. Profitability — gross, operating and net profit ratios, ROCE, ROE, return on assets: whether the trading and the capital both pay. Market — EPS, price–earnings, earnings and dividend yield, payout: what the market will pay for the earnings. Remember that ROCE is the referee between an efficiency story and a gearing story, and that every ratio is a symptom, not a cause.

Traps that cost marks. Multiplying Ke or Kp by (1 − t). Adding leverages instead of multiplying them. Using EBIT/PBT for DFL when preference shares exist. Giving retained earnings a separate market value. Comparing raw present values of assets with unequal lives instead of EAC. Valuing debtors at sales value when the question says cost. Pairing a stock with the wrong flow in a cycle computation. Treating a bonus issue or a stock split as a gain to shareholders.
