Unit 1 — Introduction to Strategic Management§

Section B is 50 marks and the shortest syllabus in Group II. It is also the easiest half-paper in this group to score well on, for one reason: almost every answer is a named framework applied to the facts of a scenario. Learn to draw the frameworks, learn the three-move answer shape below, and the marks follow.

How this paper is marked — apply this to every unit§

  1. Name the framework and say why it fits. One sentence. "The company is choosing between existing and new products in existing and new markets, so Ansoff's Matrix is the tool."
  2. Apply it to the facts you were given. Take the specifics — the growth rate, the market share, the loss-making unit, the promoter's remark — and place each inside the framework. Quote the fact, then say what the framework makes of it. Most of the marks live here.
  3. Conclude with a recommendation. Which option does the analysis point to, and why. An answer that analyses and never decides is unfinished.
Exam trap Describing the framework instead of applying it to the scenario

The single largest source of lost marks in Section B. A question gives a company with four business units and asks you to advise; the answer explains what the BCG Matrix is, names the four cells, and stops. The examiner has no fact of the case to award against. Write the framework in a line or two, spend the rest on the company in front of you — naming units, figures and stages — and finish with the recommendation.

Meaning and nature of strategic management§

Strategic management is the process by which management decides the organisation's long-term direction and scope, chooses the businesses and markets it will compete in, allocates resources to that choice, and puts the structures and controls in place to make it happen — in an environment it does not control.

Four questions define the process, and they are the spine of the section:

QuestionWhere it is answered
Where are we now?Units 2 and 3 — external and internal analysis
Where do we want to be?Unit 1 — strategic intent
What are our options, and which do we take?Unit 4 — strategic choices
How do we make it happen, and how do we know it is working?Unit 5 — implementation, control, measures

Its nature, as an answer should state it:

  • It concerns the whole organisation and the long term, not a department and not a year.
  • It is about choice: what to do, and equally what to give up.
  • It is continuous and iterative, not an annual document. Strategy is partly deliberate and partly emergent — what a firm does is rarely exactly what it planned.
  • It is done under uncertainty, so it deals in judgement, not computation.
  • It is both analytical (frameworks, data) and behavioural (leadership, culture, resistance), which is why Unit 5 exists.
  • It is top management's responsibility, but needs the whole organisation to execute.

Importance. It gives a direction against which smaller decisions can be tested; makes the firm proactive rather than reactive; provides the basis for allocating scarce resources; improves coordination, because units pull the same way; anticipates change instead of improvising; gives management something to control against; and builds advantage that lasts.

Limitations. The environment is uncertain, so the forecasts it rests on can be wrong; it costs money and senior management time; over-formalised, it produces plans nobody uses; a plan treated as sacred creates rigidity in a market that has moved; good strategies still fail in execution; and the benefits are long-term and hard to attribute, so management under-invests in them.

Remember this Strategy is a choice about direction and scope made under uncertainty — never a forecast, and never a plan that binds.

Describing strategic management as "long-term planning" understates it and loses the mark. The distinguishing features are choice (options were rejected), scope (the whole organisation, and which businesses it is in) and environment fit (internal capability matched to external conditions).

Strategic intent — vision, mission, goals, objectives, values§

Strategic intent is the umbrella term for what the organisation stands for and stretches towards. It is expressed through five things, and the exam separates them relentlessly.

ElementQuestion it answersHorizonTest of a good one
VisionWhat do we want to become?Long, aspirationalShort, ambitious, future-tense, memorable, stretches the firm
MissionWhy do we exist, what business are we in, for whom?PresentStates the customer group, the need served and the means used
GoalsWhat broad outcomes must we achieve to get there?Medium, open-endedDerived from the mission; qualitative direction
ObjectivesWhat exactly, by how much, by when?Specific and datedMeasurable, time-bound, assigned to someone
ValuesHow will we behave while doing it?PermanentFew, non-negotiable, actually constrain decisions

The hierarchy runs vision → mission → goals → objectives, with values cutting across all four as a constraint on means. A mission that does not narrow the business is useless: "to serve humanity" tells nobody which products to launch. An objective with no measure in it is a goal wearing the wrong label.

Verify: the goals-versus-objectives boundary is drawn slightly differently across editions of the Study Material — some treat goals as open-ended and objectives as close-ended and time-bound, others use the words more loosely. Use your attempt's Study Material wording.

Strategic levels in organisations§

Four levels, each with its own question. Network is the level candidates omit, and it is named first in the syllabus.

   NETWORK LEVEL        the firm plus its alliances, joint ventures, franchisees,
        |               outsourcing partners, platform and supply-chain partners
        |               Q: with whom do we cooperate, and what stays inside the firm?
        v
   CORPORATE LEVEL      the whole group / the multi-business firm
        |               Q: which businesses should we be in, and how do we allocate
        |                  resources between them?
        v
   BUSINESS LEVEL       one strategic business unit, one product-market
        |               Q: how do we compete and win in this particular business?
        v
   FUNCTIONAL LEVEL     marketing, operations, finance, HR, IT, procurement
                        Q: how does each function deliver the business strategy?
  • Network strategy operates across organisational boundaries. The firm is one node in a web of partners, so the decisions are which partners to hold, what to keep in-house and what to source, how value and risk are shared, and how control works without ownership. Franchising, joint ventures, contract manufacturing and platform ecosystems are network-level questions.
  • Corporate strategy is the portfolio: which businesses, entry and exit, diversification, resources shared across units. Unit 4's BCG and GE matrices are corporate tools.
  • Business strategy is about competing in one business; Porter's generic strategies are the business-level tool.
  • Functional strategy converts that into functional decisions and targets, and is where implementation bites.

Separate all five on question answered, horizon and testability, and illustrate each rather than defining it twice.

  • Vision — what we want to become. "To be the city's first choice for daily travel." No measure.
  • Mission — why we exist and for whom. "To move commuters across the city safely, on schedule and at a fare they can afford, on a clean fleet." Customer, need, means.
  • Goals — broad outcomes required. "Improve schedule reliability." Direction, no number.
  • Objectives — the same goal made specific. "Raise on-time departures from 82% to 95% within eighteen months." Measurable, dated, owned.
  • Values — how we behave regardless. "Passenger safety is never traded against schedule."

Two marks sit on the relationships: objectives come from goals, goals from the mission, the mission serves the vision, values constrain the means at every level. Marks are lost by writing a vision in the mission row, or an objective with no number in it.

Unit 2 — Strategic Analysis: External Environment§

External analysis answers one question: what is happening outside the firm that it does not control but must respond to. It works in three layers, and the tool changes with the layer.

PESTLE — the macro and international environment§

Six heads. Name them, then attach the question's facts to each; an empty head is better left out than padded.

HeadWhat it coversGeneric illustration
PoliticalGovernment stability and stance, policy direction, taxation and subsidy, trade and industrial policyA state announcing an incentive scheme for electric fleets
EconomicGrowth, inflation, interest and exchange rates, incomes, employment, credit, commodity pricesRates rising for a business that sells on consumer finance
SocialDemographics, urbanisation, education, lifestyle, health awareness, attitudes to brandsSmaller households shifting demand to single-serve packs
TechnologicalNew products and processes, automation, digital platforms, obsolescence, R&D intensityA distributor facing platform aggregators

EnvironmentalClimate and weather, resource scarcity, emissions and waste rules, sustainability expectationsA monsoon-dependent input for an agri-processor

Political against Legal is the distinction examiners test: political is the stance and direction of government, legal is enacted law you must comply with. A proposed policy is political; a notified statute is legal.

For the international environment, PESTLE is run country by country, plus the factors that exist only across borders: trade policy and tariffs, exchange-rate exposure, limits on repatriating profits, local-content and ownership rules, cultural distance, and differences in infrastructure and enforcement.

What the manager does with the output. Classify each factor as an opportunity or a threat, rank it by impact and probability, and carry only the material ones into the O and T of the SWOT in Unit

  1. A PESTLE that ends as a list of headings has done nothing.
Remember this PESTLE describes the environment; Five Forces explains the profit in it. They are not substitutes and the exam expects the right one.

Government policy, demographics, technology or exchange rates: PESTLE. Why margins in this industry are thin, who captures the value, whether to enter: Five Forces. An industry facing a new regulation may need both — PESTLE to identify the change, Five Forces to work out what it does to industry profitability.

Defining the industry for analysis — the value chain§

The value chain (Michael Porter) splits the firm's activities into those that create the product and those that support them, so that value added and cost can be traced activity by activity.

  SUPPORT      Firm infrastructure  (planning, finance, legal, MIS)        \
  ACTIVITIES   Human resource management                                    |
               Technology development                                       |  M
               Procurement                                                  |  A
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------   |  R
  PRIMARY      Inbound  ->  Operations  ->  Outbound  ->  Marketing  ->     |  G
  ACTIVITIES   logistics                    logistics     and sales  ->     |  I
                                                          Service          /   N

Five primary activities — inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, service. Four support activities — firm infrastructure, human resource management, technology development, procurement. What the buyer's price leaves after the cost of all of them is the margin.

Used in external analysis it sets the industry boundary: extend the chain back into suppliers and forward into channels and buyers and you can see which activities the industry actually owns, where value is added, and therefore whose profit pool you are analysing. Two firms that claim the same industry may occupy different spans of the same chain. The internal use of the tool — cost and differentiation drivers inside the firm — belongs with resources and capabilities in Unit 3.

What the manager does with the output. Decide which activities to own and which to outsource, where to attack cost, and which activity is the real source of differentiation.

The PLC describes a product or industry, not a firm. The five forces collectively determine how much profit is available in the industry, not how well one firm is doing. Strong forces compress margins for everyone; weak forces leave a pool worth entering.

Draw the five with rivalry in the centre, give two credible determinants each, and close on purpose rather than repeating the diagram.

  • New entrants — height of entry barriers (capital, scale, brand, licences); expected retaliation.
  • Suppliers — supplier concentration relative to the industry; uniqueness of the input and the cost of switching.
  • Buyers — buyer concentration and volume per buyer; a standardised product with low switching cost, making price the only variable.
  • Substitutes — relative price and performance; the buyer's propensity to switch.
  • Rivalry — number and size of competitors; industry growth rate.

The closing point earns the last mark: PESTLE tells you what is changing; Five Forces tells you what that change does to this industry's profitability and who captures the value. Only the second answers "should we be in this business, and how do we position?"

✦Quick recallA firm says its industry has "only two competitors, so competition is low." What has it missed?Answer it in your head first

Four of the five forces. Buyers and suppliers can compress its margin without any competitor acting, a substitute product can take the demand away entirely, and a concentrated, profitable industry invites entrants. Rivalry is one force of five.

Understanding customers and markets§

The industry sets the profit pool; market analysis says whose money it is.

  • Segment on a basis that changes buying behaviour — geography, demographics, income, usage rate, benefit sought, industrial buyer size or application.
  • Separate the roles: who decides, who uses and who pays are often three parties, and the marketing follows whoever decides.
  • Establish the buying criteria per segment, and which of them wins the order rather than merely qualifying the firm — price, availability, quality, service, brand, credit.
  • Size the segment and check the channel: size, growth and profitability; who controls the route to the customer and what it takes of the price.

Competition in the industry§

Competitor analysis is specific, not general. For each significant rival establish four things: its objectives (share, margin, survival), its assumptions about the industry, its current strategy and the capabilities behind it. Together they predict how it will respond to what you plan to do, which is the point.

Then place the rivals: direct competitors serving the same segment with the same product, substitute competitors meeting the same need differently, and potential entrants. Firms with similar strategies and resources form a strategic group, and rivalry inside a group is sharper than between groups, so the group constrains a firm's options more than the industry average. Note the basis of competition too: where rivals compete on price, margins fall; on differentiation and service, margins can hold.

Unit 3 — Strategic Analysis: Internal Environment§

Internal analysis asks what the firm has, whom it must satisfy, and what it can do better than anyone else. It ends by joining Unit 2's external findings to the internal ones in a SWOT, then converting that into a competitive position.

Understanding key stakeholders — Mendelow's model§

A stakeholder is any party with an interest in what the organisation does: shareholders, lenders, employees, customers, suppliers, government and regulators, communities. Their expectations conflict, so the firm must know whose bind. Mendelow's model maps them on two dimensions — power (ability to influence the strategy) and interest (how much they care about it).

                        LEVEL OF INTEREST
                    LOW                    HIGH
              +----------------------+----------------------+
              |                      |                      |
        LOW   |   A                  |   B                  |
              |   MINIMAL EFFORT     |   KEEP INFORMED      |
   P          |   monitor only       |   explain, consult;  |
   O          |                      |   they lobby, they   |
   W          |                      |   cannot compel      |
   E          +----------------------+----------------------+
   R          |                      |                      |
              |   C                  |   D                  |
        HIGH  |   KEEP SATISFIED     |   KEY PLAYERS        |
              |   do not provoke;    |   involve in the     |
              |   they can act if    |   decision; strategy |
              |   roused             |   must be acceptable |
              |                      |   to them            |
              +----------------------+----------------------+
  • A — low power, low interest: minimal effort; monitor in case they move.
  • B — low power, high interest: keep informed. They cannot stop the strategy but they can make noise, and an informed supporter is useful — a community group, a small shareholder body.
  • C — high power, low interest: keep satisfied. Dangerous precisely because they are currently indifferent: one decision touching their interest moves them to D. A large lender is the standard case.
  • D — high power, high interest: key players. The strategy must be acceptable to them, so they are involved before it is settled, not informed after.

What the manager does with the output. Choose an involvement strategy per quadrant and test any proposed strategy for acceptability to the key players before committing. Positions are not permanent — a decision can raise a stakeholder's interest, and management can move a stakeholder deliberately by sharing information or granting representation.

Exam trap Mapping stakeholders by how important they feel rather than by power and interest

Employees are always "very important", so they land in the key-player box by default. Power is the ability to influence or block the strategy; interest is how much this decision affects them. Unorganised employees facing a plant closure have high interest and low power — "keep informed" is the answer, and saying so earns the mark. The axes are power and interest, not power and influence.

Strategic drivers§

Five drivers determine where a strategy can go. Treat each as a question about the firm's position:

  • Industry and markets — which industries and segments is the firm in, how attractive are they, and are they growing or consolidating?
  • Customers — which groups does it serve, how concentrated and loyal are they, and how profitable is each?
  • Channels — how does it reach them, who controls the channel, what does the channel cost, and can the firm reach the customer directly?
  • Products and services — what is the range, where is each item in its life cycle, and how differentiated is it?
  • Competitive advantage — what does the firm do better than rivals, and will that hold?

Their use is diagnostic: changing one driver silently changes the others. Moving to direct online distribution changes the channel, and with it the customer relationship, the viable product range and the basis of advantage.

Resources and capabilities§

Resources are what the firm has; capabilities are what it can do with them.

LayerMeaningExamples
ResourcesStock of assets, tangible and intangiblePlant, cash, patents, brand, licences, data, people
CompetencesActivities the firm performs reliablyRunning a plant efficiently; settling a claim in a day
Core competencesThe few competences central to the strategy that distinguish the firmAn engineering skill rivals cannot match; unreplicable distribution reach

Resources are classified as tangible (physical, financial), intangible (brand, reputation, intellectual property, data, culture) and human (skills, experience, relationships). The intangible ones are usually the durable source of advantage, being hardest to buy.

A resource or capability produces a sustainable advantage only if it is valuable to customers, rare among competitors, hard to imitate and hard to substitute — and the firm is organised to exploit it. A superb capability the firm is not structured to use is a wasted asset, which is the Unit 5 problem.

✦Quick recallA firm's advantage is that it owns the only cold-storage network in a region. Is that sustainable?Answer it in your head first

Only until someone builds another one. It is valuable and currently rare, but imitability decides sustainability — if a rival can fund and construct the same network, the advantage lasts as long as the construction period. A capability built on accumulated skill, relationships or data lasts longer than one built on an asset that money can buy.

Three disciplines separate a SWOT that scores from a list that does not. Strengths and weaknesses are internal and comparative — measured against competitors, not in the abstract; a 20% margin is a strength only if rivals earn less. Opportunities and threats are external and outside the firm's control; "we should launch a new product" is a strategy, not an opportunity. And the same fact can appear twice: reliance on one large customer is a strength in volume and a weakness in dependence.

What the manager does with the output. Pair the cells to generate candidate strategies, then evaluate them with the Unit 4 tools. A SWOT that stops at four lists earns half the marks.

Remember this A SWOT is a bridge, not a list. Its output is the pairs — SO, ST, WO, WT — and each pair is a candidate strategy.

In a scenario the four lists earn the first half of the marks; the strategies drawn from pairing them, and the recommendation, earn the second. Tie every item to a fact in the question.

Gaining competitive advantage — Michael Porter's generic strategies§

Porter argues advantage has only two sources — being the lowest cost producer or being differentiated — pursued either across a broad market or in a narrow segment. Two dimensions, four boxes.

✦Answer ladderA regional garment maker sells mid-priced clothing through its own stores. It has no scale advantage against national brands, and its prices are above the local unbranded tailors. Sales are flat. Advise on competitive positioning.Answer it yourself first, then see the three tiers
Would lose marks

The company should reduce its costs and also improve its product quality so that it can compete with both the national brands and the local tailors.

Passing

Identifies the firm as stuck in the middle under Porter's generic strategies — neither lowest cost, since it lacks scale against the national brands, nor differentiated, since it sells mid-priced clothing with no distinguishing feature. It must choose one of the four cells rather than sit between them.

Full marks

The same diagnosis with the facts named, then a reasoned choice. Cost leadership is unavailable: the national brands hold the scale and the unbranded tailors the lower overhead, so a firm this size cannot win on cost across the industry, which rules out both broad-market cells. Its real assets — its own stores and its regional presence — are segment assets, so the viable cells are the focused ones, most plausibly differentiation focus: a defined regional segment served with fit, local styling, alteration and exchange service that neither a national brand nor a tailor can match at that price. This is also why "cut cost and raise quality" is wrong; it deepens the stuck position by spending on both dimensions at once. Recommendation: adopt differentiation focus on the regional segment, realign the value chain to service and fit rather than volume, and accept a smaller addressable market as the price of a defensible position.

Practise it Draw Mendelow's matrix, label the four quadrants with the strategy for each, and explain what management should do when a high-power stakeholder's interest suddenly rises.
✦Model answerCompare against what a full-mark answer containsWrite above to unseal

Draw the grid with power on one axis and level of interest on the other, and name the quadrants correctly: low/low minimal effort, low power–high interest keep informed, high power–low interest keep satisfied, high/high key players.

The second half is the discriminating part. A high-power, low-interest stakeholder who becomes interested moves from "keep satisfied" to "key player", so the treatment must change with the position: that stakeholder must now be involved in the decision and the strategy must be acceptable to them, not explained afterwards. Hence the model is dynamic — stakeholders are mapped for a specific decision, not once for the organisation, and a strategy touching a lender's security or a regulator's remit itself moves stakeholders into the key-player box. If a strategy is unacceptable to a key player it must be modified, dropped, or the stakeholder brought along before commitment.

Unit 4 — Strategic Choices§

Units 2 and 3 tell the firm where it stands. This unit is the menu of what it can do about it and the four matrices that generate and screen the options. Each matrix is drawable in a minute, and in a scenario question the drawing plus correct placement of the facts is most of the mark.

The menu of strategic choices§

ChoiceWhat it isAppropriate when
Market developmentExisting products into new markets — geography, segment or channelThe home market is saturating
Product developmentNew or modified products for existing customersThe customer relationship is strong, the range dated
InnovationSomething materially new — product, process or business modelA shift extension will not hold
Concentric diversificationA related new business sharing technology, market, channel or skillThere is a real link to exploit
Conglomerate diversificationAn unrelated new business; the logic is financialRisk spreading, or surplus cash
Horizontal integrationA firm at the same stage of the same industryScale, share or capacity
Vertical integrationA different stage of the same chain — back to suppliers, forward to channelsSupply or access is unreliable, or that margin is worth capturing
TurnaroundKeep it and fix it — cost cuts, asset reduction, refocus, new managementDecline is recent and reversible
DivestitureSell or spin off a unit; the rest continuesWorth more to another owner
LiquidationClose and sell the assets; exitNo buyer and no viable fix

Four pairs get conflated, and the marks are in the distinction:

  • Concentric versus conglomerate — relatedness. Concentric shares something operational (technology, customers, channel, brand, skill), so synergy can be claimed; conglomerate shares nothing operational, so its only case is risk spreading or surplus funds, and its typical failure is management that does not understand the new business.
  • Horizontal versus vertical — direction. Horizontal moves sideways at the same stage, so the firm does more of what it already does; vertical moves along the chain into work it has never done. A biscuit maker buying another biscuit maker is horizontal; buying its flour mill is backward vertical; buying its distributor is forward vertical.
  • Turnaround, divestiture, liquidation — escalating severity, each with a test. Viable if fixed? Worth more to someone else? Any buyer or fix at all? The answer follows from the test.
  • Market development, product development, diversification — which of market and product is new. That is Ansoff's entire point.
Remember this Every strategic-choice question reduces to "what is new here — the product, the market, both, or the stage of the value chain?"

Classify the situation first; the label follows. Then give the condition that makes the choice appropriate and the risk that makes it dangerous. The examiner is testing classification and judgement, not ten definitions.

Exam trap Using absolute market share, or the company's own growth rate, on the BCG axes

Both axes are specific. The horizontal axis is relative share — the unit's divided by the largest rival's — so 20% is high against a leader at 12% and low against a leader at 45%. The vertical axis is the market's growth, not the unit's sales growth. Get either wrong and every unit lands in the wrong cell, which costs the question rather than a mark. And a low-share unit in a growing market is a question mark, not a dog.

✦Quick recallTwo units both hold a weak position — one in an embryonic industry, one in an ageing one. Why does ADL separate them and BCG does not?Answer it in your head first

Because ADL's horizontal axis is maturity, which distinguishes "too early to tell" from "too late to fix". The embryonic unit is asked to prove viability — the industry has not settled and a position is still winnable; the ageing one is told to withdraw, since neither industry nor position will improve. BCG separates them only by market growth.

✦Answer ladderA mid-sized paint company sells to retail customers in three states. Volume growth has fallen to 2% as those states saturate; the firm has cash, a recognised brand and a strong dealer network. Recommend a growth strategy.Answer it yourself first, then see the three tiers
Would lose marks

Ansoff's Matrix has four cells — market penetration, market development, product development and diversification — of which penetration is least risky and diversification most risky. The company should choose whichever suits it best.

Passing

Applies the matrix to the firm. Penetration is nearly exhausted, since the three states are saturating and growth has fallen to 2%. Market development means the existing paints in new states; product development means new products for the existing dealers and customers; diversification would be riskiest. Both middle options look feasible given the cash and the brand.

Full marks

The same, decided on the firm's own assets and closed with the risk. Penetration is ruled out by the facts — 2% growth in saturating states. The transferable strengths are the brand and the dealer network; a dealer network is state-specific and must be rebuilt in a new state, whereas new products travel through the network the firm already controls, so product development exploits the existing asset and market development recreates it. Recommendation: lead with product development — adjacent lines such as waterproofing for the same buyers through the same dealers — and phase market development into one contiguous state as a funded pilot rather than three at once. Reject diversification: cash without capability in an unrelated business is the standard reason conglomerate diversification destroys value. Risk: a wider range dilutes management attention and a quality failure damages the brand carrying the whole portfolio, so each new line needs a stage-gate, not an open commitment.

Case study The unit that pays for everything
Symptom

A listed group has three businesses. Packaging earns 70% of group profit and holds 34% share, with its largest competitor at 30%, in a market growing 3% a year. Speciality chemicals has 6% share against a leader at 28%, in a market growing 22%, and has consumed cash for four years. Printing consumables has 4% share against a leader at 26%, in a market shrinking 5% a year, and has broken even for three years.

Signals

The board proposes to fund the chemicals expansion by cutting packaging's maintenance and marketing budget, and to keep printing consumables because "it covers its own costs and the plant is written down."

Diagnose it

Place the three units on the BCG matrix. Is the board's funding proposal sound, and what would you recommend for each unit?

✦The answerPlace the three units on the BCG matrix. Is the board's funding proposal sound, and what would you recommend for each unit?Commit to an answer, then open this
Root cause

Packaging is a cash cow — relative share 34/30 exceeds one, so it is the market leader, and growth of 3% is low. Speciality chemicals is a question mark — relative share 6/28 is well below one, growth of 22% is high, and the cash absorption is what the cell predicts. Printing consumables is a dog — relative share 4/26 is far below one and market growth is negative.

The board is half right. Funding a question mark out of a cash cow is the cash logic the matrix prescribes, so the direction of the money is correct; the source is not. Cutting a cow's maintenance and marketing does not release free cash — it converts the cow into a dog over a few years, because share in a low-growth market is held by exactly that spending. A cow is harvested by taking its surplus, not by starving the asset that generates it. The unexamined "keep" on printing consumables is the second flaw: breaking even in a shrinking market still consumes attention and working capital while terminal value falls, and the written-down plant is irrelevant to a decision that turns on what the assets and customer list would realise now.

Recommendation. Hold and harvest packaging, funding chemicals from its surplus rather than its cost base. On chemicals, set a share and margin milestone at which further investment is justified and treat divestiture as the live alternative while the market is growing and buyers exist — "build selectively or divest", since the group cannot fund a share war against a rival nearly five times its size. Divest or liquidate printing consumables. Finally, state what the matrix cannot see: whether the units share customers, channels or technology, since a dog carrying a cow's customer relationship may be worth keeping for a reason the grid does not capture.

Practise it Draw Ansoff's Matrix and the BCG Matrix from memory, label all eight cells, and state what a manager decides with each matrix's output.
✦Model answerCompare against what a full-mark answer containsWrite above to unseal

Ansoff — products (existing, new) against markets (existing, new): market penetration, product development, market development, diversification. Output: growth options ranked by risk, rising away from penetration.

Unit 5 — Strategy Implementation and Evaluation§

A strategy that exists only on paper has produced nothing. This unit turns the Unit 4 choice into structure, behaviour and measurement — and asks, while it runs, whether it is still the right strategy.

Linkages and issues. The forward linkage runs from formulation to implementation: the strategy chosen dictates the structure, systems, resource allocation and skills to be put in place, so a diversification and a cost-leadership strategy need different organisations. The backward linkage runs the other way: existing structure, culture and capability constrain which strategies are realistic. The recurring issues are resource allocation to the new priorities rather than the old, fit of structure and systems to the strategy, leadership, culture and resistance, sequence and timing, and rewards still pointing at the previous objectives.

Remember this Formulation asks what to do; implementation asks how, by whom, with what and by when. Most strategies fail in the second question.

Where a question asks you to discuss implementation issues, the marks are on internal mechanics — structure, systems, resources, people, culture, timing, control — not on re-justifying the strategy. Re-arguing the choice is the commonest way to answer the wrong question.

Strategic change through digital transformation§

Three words are not interchangeable. Digitisation converts information to digital form. Digitalisation uses digital technology to run existing processes better. Digital transformation changes the business model, the value proposition and the customer experience — what the firm sells, to whom, and how it captures value. That is why it sits here: it is strategic change, managed as change.

  1. Establish the case and the urgency — what makes the current model unsustainable.
  2. Set the vision for the changed business, in customer outcomes, not technology.
  3. Assess the capability gap: data, skills, systems, processes, partners.
  4. Pilot narrow, learn, then scale — evidence beats resistance more cheaply than argument.
  5. Realign the hard elements: structure, roles, decision rights, targets, reward.
  6. Build the soft side until the new way survives without sponsorship.

Enablers: leadership sponsorship, usable data and integrated systems, skills, iterative working, and controls over privacy and cyber risk. Barriers: legacy systems and hard-wired processes, skill gaps, culture and resistance, cost against an uncertain return, unclear ownership, security exposure, and change fatigue.

Verify: this vocabulary is a recent addition and the terms and stage lists differ between editions. Use your attempt's Study Material wording for any list you must reproduce.

Organisation structure (hard) and culture (soft)§

Structure — the hard sideCulture — the soft side
What it isReporting lines, departments, decision rights, systems, procedures, targetsShared beliefs, values, assumptions, habits, informal networks
VisibilityVisible; can be drawnInvisible; inferred from behaviour
How it changesBy management decision, quicklySlowly, by consistent behaviour and example; cannot be mandated
ImitabilityEasily copiedVery hard to copy, so a source of advantage
Failure modeA structure that no longer fits the strategyA culture that quietly defeats the structure

The structural forms, one line each: functional (by activity — efficient, weak across products); divisional (by product, market or geography — accountable, duplicative); strategic business unit (related divisions under one head, to make a large portfolio governable); matrix (dual reporting — flexible, conflicting authority); network (a small core coordinating outsourced partners — flexible, dependent, hard to control).

Both sides must move together. Redrawing the chart while leaving culture untouched produces a new structure operated by old behaviour; announcing values while targets and rewards still point at the old strategy produces cynicism. Where the two conflict, culture usually wins, because it decides what people do when nobody is checking.

Exam trap Treating culture as a detail that "will follow" once the structure changes

Culture is the slowest element to change and the one that decides whether the structure functions. An implementation answer that lists structure, systems and resources but never reaches culture, resistance and reward alignment leaves marks on the table — and this is precisely where the hard/soft distinction is examined. Say which changes are hard (fast, by decision) and which are soft (slow, by behaviour and example), and that the soft work must start earliest.

Strategic leadership§

Strategic leadership is the capacity to set direction and get an organisation to move in it. Its tasks: communicating the direction, building the capability the strategy needs, allocating resources consistently with the choice made, shaping culture by example and by what gets rewarded, sustaining the change past the point where enthusiasm fades, and developing successors. Two styles are contrasted: transformational, which changes what people aspire to and suits major strategic change, and transactional, which works through targets, monitoring and reward and suits executing an agreed strategy efficiently. Strategy and situation decide which fits.

Strategic control steers the strategy while it is being executed, because the assumptions it rested on can change before it is finished. Four types:

TypeQuestion it asksTrigger and pace

The distinction that earns the mark: operational control corrects performance against the plan; strategic control is willing to conclude that the plan itself is wrong.

Strategic performance measures§

Measures exist so that control has something to work with. A good set is derived from the objectives, few enough to focus attention, a mix of financial and non-financial and of lagging and leading indicators, within someone's control, and comparable over time and against competitors. Financial: profitability, revenue growth, return on capital employed, margins, cash generation. Non-financial: market share, customer satisfaction and retention, quality and defect rates, delivery reliability, employee retention and skills, innovation, sustainability. The non-financial ones usually lead — satisfaction falls before revenue does, which is why a purely financial set reports problems too late to act on. When the strategy changes the measures must change with it.

The company should implement the new structure properly and instruct staff to follow it, and the managing director should stop interfering in divisional matters.

Identifies a hard change without a matching soft change. The structure was altered but culture, decision rights and rewards were not, so behaviour stayed centralised. Full marks

The same diagnosis in the unit's own terms, decided on the facts given. This is a forward linkage failure: differentiation required fast decentralised decisions and the structure was changed to provide them, but the hard change was left incomplete — decision rights, budgets and rewards still sit centrally — and the soft change was never begun. The evidence is in the facts: referral upward is a decision-rights problem, late launches are the consequence, and "nothing has really changed" is culture reporting that behaviour has not moved. Recommendation: complete the hard side first, because it is fast and visible — write down what each divisional head may decide unaided, move budget authority down, reset targets and incentives to divisional outcomes. Then work the slow soft side: the managing director must visibly stop accepting referred decisions, since one reversal teaches more than any announcement. Add implementation control with a six-month milestone review — and if divisional results show the differentiation strategy itself is failing, the answer is premise control, not more restructuring.

Practise it Distinguish strategic control from operational control, name the four types of strategic control, and give one situation each type would detect that the others would miss.
✦Model answerCompare against what a full-mark answer containsWrite above to unseal

Separate the two on object, horizon, question, data and the kind of correction, and state the core difference: operational control corrects performance against the existing plan, while strategic control is prepared to conclude that the plan is wrong.

  • Premise control — the strategy assumed a tariff would stay and it has been withdrawn. No internal report shows this; only a check of the premises does.
  • Implementation control — two years into a five-year capacity programme the first two plants run at half the assumed utilisation, which raises whether to continue at all, not how to catch up.
  • Strategic surveillance — an unrelated industry's technology becomes a substitute. No premise mentioned it and no milestone catches it; broad scanning does.
  • Special alert control — a fire at the sole supplier's plant, or a sudden regulatory ban, needing immediate reassessment rather than a scheduled review.

The last mark is for the point that these are complementary: a firm running only implementation control will execute an obsolete strategy competently.

Revision checklist

Frameworks you must be able to draw and label from memory. For each, the axes are the mark:

  • PESTLE — six heads: political, economic, social, technological, legal, environmental. Run country by country for the international environment.
  • Porter's Five Forces — new entrants, buyers, suppliers, substitutes, rivalry in the centre; gives the profit available in the industry.
  • Value chain (Porter) — five primary activities (inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, service), four support activities (firm infrastructure, HRM, technology development, procurement), plus margin.
  • Product life cycle — introduction, growth, maturity, decline, with the strategy for each stage.
  • Mendelow's model — power against level of interest: minimal effort, keep informed, keep satisfied, key players.
  • SWOT — internal/external against helpful/harmful, and the four pairings SO, ST, WO, WT.
  • Porter's generic strategies — advantage (cost, differentiation) against scope (broad, narrow): cost leadership, differentiation, cost focus, differentiation focus, and stuck in the middle.
  • Ansoff's Matrix — products against markets: market penetration, product development, market development, diversification.
  • BCG Matrix — relative market share against market growth: stars, question marks, cash cows, dogs; build, hold, harvest, divest.
  • GE Matrix — industry attractiveness against business strength, nine cells, three zones: invest/grow, selectivity/earnings, harvest/divest.
  • ADL Matrix — industry maturity (embryonic, growth, mature, ageing) against competitive position (dominant, strong, favourable, tenable, weak); thrusts of natural development, selective development, prove viability, withdraw.
  • Formulation–implementation matrix — success, roulette, trouble, failure.
  • Strategic levels — network, corporate, business, functional. Network is the one candidates omit.

Attributions to keep straight. Porter owns the five forces, the value chain and the generic strategies. Ansoff owns the product-market matrix. Mendelow owns the stakeholder grid. BCG, GE and ADL are firm-named matrices, not personal ones.

Term pairs the examiner separates. Vision / mission / goals / objectives / values. Strategy formulation / implementation. Concentric / conglomerate diversification. Horizontal / vertical integration. Turnaround / divestiture / liquidation. Strategic control / operational control. Digitisation / digitalisation / digital transformation. Structure (hard) / culture (soft).

The answer-technique rule, worth more than any single framework. Name the framework and say why it fits; apply it to the facts given, naming those facts; conclude with a recommendation. Describing a framework without applying it to the scenario is the single most common way marks are lost here, and an analysis that never decides is unfinished.
