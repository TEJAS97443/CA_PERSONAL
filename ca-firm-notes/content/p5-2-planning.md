## Unit 2 — Audit Strategy, Audit Planning and Audit Program

Planning is examined as three nested things, and mixing them costs marks: the **overall audit
strategy** (scope, timing and direction), the **audit plan** (the nature, timing and extent of
procedures) and the **audit program** (the detailed list of procedures that instructs the team).
SA 300 governs the first two.

### The auditor's responsibility to plan

The auditor shall plan the audit so that the engagement is performed in an effective manner. The
requirement about *who* plans is asked and rarely answered: the **engagement partner and other key
members of the engagement team** shall be involved in planning, including planning and
participating in the **discussion among engagement team members**, because their experience and
insight improve the effectiveness and efficiency of the planning process. Planning is not
delegated wholesale to the assistant who fills in the program.

### Benefits of audit planning

| Benefit | What it prevents |
|---|---|
| Appropriate attention to important areas | The risky balance getting the same effort as the easy one |
| Potential problems identified and resolved on a timely basis | A scope problem surfacing the week before signing |
| The engagement is organised and managed so as to be performed effectively and efficiently | Overrun and rework |
| Team members with appropriate capabilities and competence selected, and work assigned to them | Sending a first-year to audit a derivative |
| Direction and supervision of team members, and review of their work | Unreviewed working papers |
| Coordination of work done by component auditors and experts | Gaps between two teams, each assuming the other did it |

### Planning is continual and iterative

Say this in the Standard's terms. Planning is **not a discrete phase** at the start of the audit; it
is a **continual and iterative process** that often begins shortly after, or in connection with, the
completion of the previous audit and continues until the completion of the current engagement. As the
audit progresses the auditor may need to revise the strategy and the plan — because the results of
risk assessment or of further audit procedures differ from expectations, or the understanding of the
entity changes. Strategy and plan feed each other: a change in the plan can force the strategy to be
revised, and a revised strategy changes the plan.

:::key Planning is a continual and iterative process, not a stage — it starts near the end of the previous audit and ends only when this one does.
Any answer that says planning "is completed before commencement of the audit" is contradicting
SA 300. Say instead that a preliminary strategy and plan are established before substantive work
begins and are **revised as necessary as the audit progresses**, with the revisions documented
together with the reasons.
:::

### Preliminary engagement activities

Before the audit begins, and at the beginning of the current engagement, the auditor shall
undertake three preliminary engagement activities:

1. Perform the procedures required regarding the **continuance of the client relationship and
   the specific audit engagement**.
2. Evaluate compliance with **relevant ethical requirements, including independence**.
3. Establish an understanding of the **terms of the engagement** — the Standard on agreeing
   engagement terms sits at the ethics unit of this desk.

Their purpose is to establish that the auditor has considered events or circumstances that may
adversely affect the ability to plan and perform the audit; that the auditor can remain
independent and able to perform the engagement; and that there is no misunderstanding with the
client about the terms.

**Client continuance** is a judgement, not a formality. The matters considered include the
**integrity of the principal owners, key management and those charged with governance**; whether
the team is **competent** and has the capabilities, time and resources; whether the firm can
**comply with relevant ethical requirements**; and **significant matters arising in the current or
previous engagement** and their implications for continuing.

### Elements of planning discussed with management

The auditor may discuss elements of planning with management to facilitate the conduct of the
engagement — for instance to coordinate some planned procedures with the work of the entity's
personnel. Two limits are the examinable part: the **overall audit strategy and the audit plan remain
the auditor's responsibility**, and care is needed so that discussion does not compromise
effectiveness by making the procedures **too predictable**.

### Establishing the overall audit strategy

The strategy sets the **scope, timing and direction** of the audit and guides the development of
the audit plan. In establishing it the auditor:

- identifies the **characteristics of the engagement** that define its scope, such as the
  financial reporting framework and industry-specific reporting requirements;
- ascertains the **reporting objectives** so as to plan the timing of the audit and the nature
  of the communications required;
- considers the factors that, in the auditor's **professional judgement**, are significant in
  directing the engagement team's efforts;
- considers the results of **preliminary engagement activities** and, where applicable, whether
  knowledge gained on other engagements performed by the engagement partner for the entity is
  relevant;
- ascertains the **nature, timing and extent of resources** necessary to perform the engagement.

The last item is what the syllabus calls the strategy's *assistance for the auditor*. A completed
strategy tells the auditor **what** resources to deploy on specific areas (experienced members on
high-risk balances, an expert on complex matters, enough people to observe inventory counting at
several locations), **how much** to allocate, **when** they are deployed (at an interim stage or
at key cut-off dates), and **how** they are managed, directed, supervised and reviewed — briefing
and debriefing meetings, partner and manager review, and whether an engagement quality control
review is needed.

### Developing the audit plan

The audit plan is more detailed than the strategy and includes:

- the nature, timing and extent of planned **risk assessment procedures**;
- the nature, timing and extent of planned **further audit procedures at the assertion level**;
- **other planned audit procedures** required so that the engagement complies with the SAs.

Planning of these procedures occurs over the course of the audit as the plan develops: the
auditor may complete the planning of risk assessment procedures early, while the further audit
procedures depend on the outcome of that risk assessment.

### Documenting strategy and plan

SA 300 requires documentation of the **overall audit strategy**, the **audit plan**, and any
**significant changes** made to either during the audit **together with the reasons**. The record of
significant changes is the item candidates forget, and it is what demonstrates that planning was
iterative.

> **Verify:** planning and documentation requirements sit in SA 300 and the documentation Standard.
> Where a Standard is replaced, ICAI applies the corresponding provisions from a notified date —
> confirm against the Study Material and Study Guidelines for your attempt.

### The audit program

An **audit program** is a detailed list of the audit procedures to be performed for each area, serving
both as a **set of instructions to the assistants** and as a means of **controlling and recording**
the proper execution of the work. Constructing one requires the auditor to consider the size and
nature of the entity, its accounting system and existing internal control, the deadline and the
availability and experience of staff. Nothing is standard: a program copied from another client or
from last year without review is the failure the examiner is testing for.

| Advantages | Disadvantages, and the answer to each |
|---|---|
| Fixes responsibility, each step being initialled by whoever performed it | Work becomes **mechanical** — met by requiring assistants to report anything unusual |
| Gives the assistant clear, ordered instructions and evidence of coverage | The program turns **rigid** and stops fitting the business — met by revising it as the audit progresses |
| Lets the partner control progress and review completeness | Assistants lose **initiative** and stop at the listed steps — met by inviting additions, and by reviewing the program itself each year |

:::trap Treating "audit strategy", "audit plan" and "audit program" as three names for one document
They differ in level of detail and in what they answer. The **strategy** answers scope, timing,
direction and resources. The **plan** answers the nature, timing and extent of risk assessment
procedures and of further audit procedures at the assertion level. The **program** answers the
individual steps an assistant performs on one area, with a place to sign. A "distinguish" question
wants that ladder of detail, plus the point that the plan develops from the strategy and both are
revised as the audit progresses.
:::

:::think When does audit planning begin?
Often shortly after, or in connection with, the completion of the previous audit — and it
continues until the current engagement is complete. Preliminary engagement activities are
performed at the beginning of the current engagement.
:::

:::attempt Draft the audit program for the purchases of a manufacturing client, and state four benefits of planning it in advance.
Head the answer with the assertion at risk — purchases and trade payables are usually understated, so
**completeness** and **cut-off** drive the program — then follow the transaction flow: test controls
over requisition, purchase order authorisation, goods receipt and three-way matching; reconcile the
purchase register with the ledger; vouch a sample of invoices to the purchase order, goods received
note and inspection report for quantity, rate, approval and accounting head; test cut-off across the
year end; search for unrecorded liabilities through post-year-end invoices, payments and goods
received but not invoiced; perform substantive analytical procedures on purchase price and consumption
ratios; scrutinise debit notes and returns after the year end; check related party purchases and their
disclosure.

For the benefits, take **four** from SA 300's own list and write them in the Standard's words: attention
to important areas; timely identification and resolution of potential problems; organisation and
management of the engagement so that it is performed effectively and efficiently; selection of team
members with appropriate capabilities and competence, and assignment of work to them.

Two further benefits are worth having ready if the mark allocation is larger, or if the question asks
for the full list: direction and supervision of team members and review of their work; and coordination
of the work done by component auditors and by experts.
:::
