## Unit 1 — Financial Management and Financial Analysis

:::dialogue
@P: Profit is up 18%. The bank has refused to renew the working capital limit. Explain that.
@Y: If profit is up, the bank is being unreasonable.
@P: The bank lends against cash and the balance sheet, not against profit. Find where the profit went.
@N: Unit 1 in one exchange. Profit maximisation and wealth maximisation are not the same objective, and ratios say which one the entity is achieving.
:::

### The finance function and its objective

Financial management plans, raises, controls and administers funds. Its scope is **three decisions and
one constraint**: the **financing decision** (how much, from where, in what mix — Unit 2), the
**investment** and **dividend** decisions (Unit 3), and the **liquidity decision** (Unit 4).

| | Profit maximisation | Wealth maximisation |
|---|---|---|
| Measure | Accounting profit or EPS | Market value of the owners' stake |
| Timing | Indifferent to when profit arrives | Discounts for timing |
| Risk | Ignored | Priced in the discount rate |
| Basis | Accrual profit, an accounting figure | Cash flow, an economic figure |
| Vagueness | Which profit — pre-tax or post, short or long run? | One number, quoted daily |
| Verdict | A yardstick, not an objective | The accepted objective |

The three objections to profit maximisation compress to one phrase: **timing, risk and vagueness**.

**Value creation** is wealth maximisation made operational — value is created when return on capital
employed exceeds the cost of that capital. Unit 2 measures that cost, Unit 3 tests projects against
it, Unit 4 asks the same of money tied up in stock and debtors.

:::key Value is created only when return on capital employed exceeds the cost of capital. Growth financed above its own return destroys wealth while still raising profit.
Funds ploughed into projects earning 9% while capital costs 12% raise profit and lower market value.
That asymmetry is the mark when a question contrasts the two objectives.
:::

**Financial manager against financial controller**, examined as a contrast:

| | Financial Manager | Financial Controller |
|---|---|---|
| Orientation | Forward-looking, decision-taking | Backward-looking, reporting |
| Faces | Banks, investors, the capital market | Management, auditors, tax authorities |
| Does | Raises funds, sets structure, appraises investment, manages cash and credit | Records, budgets, prepares statements, runs internal control, reports variances |

**Functions of finance executives**: estimating fund requirements; deciding capital structure;
selecting sources; investment of funds; maintaining liquidity; disposal of surplus and the dividend
decision; financial negotiation; evaluating performance; and tracking the company's own share price.

The **financial management environment** is where those decisions are taken: the **money market** for
short-term instruments, the **capital market** for long-term funds, the intermediaries channelling
savings into investment, and the regulatory, tax and interest-rate framework. The firm is largely a
**price-taker** here — the cost of capital in Unit 2 is set in these markets, not chosen.

### Financial distress and insolvency

**Financial distress** is the condition in which cash flows are insufficient to meet obligations. It
is a matter of degree, running from mild liquidity strain through stretched creditors and breached
covenants to insolvency. **Insolvency** takes two forms and questions turn on the distinction:
**cash-flow (technical) insolvency**, where debts cannot be paid as they fall due even though assets
exceed liabilities; and **balance-sheet insolvency**, where liabilities exceed the realisable value
of assets.

The **costs of distress** split the way marks do. **Direct** — legal, administrative and professional
fees, and management time spent on creditors. **Indirect** — customers and suppliers withdraw, credit
tightens, staff leave, assets are sold below value, new finance costs more. The indirect usually
exceed the direct.

### Financial analysis through ratios

**Users**, each paired with their question: management (control and decisions), investors (return and
risk), lenders (security and servicing capacity), suppliers (short-term solvency), employees
(stability), government (compliance and tax), analysts and rating agencies.

**Sources of financial data**: the balance sheet and statement of profit and loss, the cash flow
statement, notes and accounting policies, the directors' report and management discussion; and
internally, budgets, cost records and management reports, plus industry data to compare against.

| Family | Ratios | Diagnoses |
|---|---|---|
| Liquidity | Current, quick, cash | Can short-term obligations be met |
| Solvency / leverage | Debt–equity, debt to total assets, proprietary, capital gearing, interest coverage, DSCR | How much risk sits with lenders, and can they be serviced |
| Activity / turnover | Inventory, receivables, payables, fixed asset, working capital, total asset | How hard the assets are worked |
| Profitability | Gross, operating, net profit; ROCE, ROE, return on assets | Whether trading and capital both pay |
| Market / valuation | EPS, price–earnings, earnings yield, dividend yield, payout | What the market pays for the earnings |

:::formula Return on Capital Employed
@what: The return earned on all long-term funds in the business, before deciding who gets it.
@formula: ROCE = (EBIT / Capital employed) x 100
Capital employed = Shareholders' funds + Long-term debt = Total assets - Current liabilities
@terms: EBIT = earnings before interest and tax. Shareholders' funds = equity + preference capital
+ reserves, less accumulated losses.
@watch: The numerator is before interest, because interest is a return to one of the suppliers of the
capital in the denominator. PAT over capital employed mixes a post-interest return with a pre-interest
base — the commonest error here.
:::

:::formula Return on equity, decomposed (DuPont)
@what: Splits the owners' return into a trading, an efficiency and a gearing effect.
@formula: ROE = PAT / Shareholders' funds
        = (PAT / Sales) x (Sales / Total assets) x (Total assets / Shareholders' funds)
        = Net profit margin x Total asset turnover x Equity multiplier
@terms: Where preference shares exist, return on *equity* funds uses PAT less preference dividend
over equity shareholders' funds only.
@watch: The three factors must multiply back to the direct ROE; if not, you have mixed average and
closing bases. An ROE rising only through the third factor is a gearing story, not an improvement.
:::

:::formula Interest coverage and debt service coverage
@formula: Interest coverage = EBIT / Interest
DSCR = (PAT + Depreciation + Interest on term loan) / (Interest on term loan + Principal instalment)
@watch: Both are "times", never percentages. DSCR adds principal repayment, which is not an expense
and so is absent from profit, and adds back non-cash depreciation. Comfortable interest coverage with
a DSCR below one is the company in the dialogue.
:::

:::estimate A complete ratio pass over one small set of accounts
@given: All figures ₹ lakh. Equity share capital (face ₹10) 200; reserves 100; 12% debentures 200;
trade payables 90; other current liabilities 10. Net fixed assets 350; inventory 100; receivables
80; cash 70. Sales (all credit) 1,000; cost of goods sold 700; operating expenses 150; purchases
720. Tax 30%. Use closing figures throughout.
@ask: Compute the profit line to PAT, then current and quick ratios, debt–equity, interest coverage,
inventory and debtor velocity in days, creditors' days, ROCE, net profit ratio, ROE and EPS.
Reconcile ROE through the DuPont decomposition.
@work: Balance the sheet first: shareholders' funds ₹300; capital employed 300 + 200 = ₹500; current
liabilities ₹100; current assets ₹250; total 600 = 350 + 250. It balances.

Gross profit = 1,000 − 700 = ₹300. EBIT = 300 − 150 = ₹150. Interest = 12% x 200 = ₹24.
PBT = ₹126. Tax at 30% = ₹37.80. PAT = ₹88.20.

Current ratio = 250 / 100 = **2.5 : 1**; quick = (250 − 100) / 100 = **1.5 : 1**.

Debt–equity = 200 / 300 = **0.67 : 1**; interest coverage = 150 / 24 = **6.25 times**.

Inventory turnover = 700 / 100 = 7 times → **52 days** (365 / 7).

Debtor turnover = 1,000 / 80 = 12.5 times → **29 days**. Creditors = 90 / 720 x 365 = **46 days**.

ROCE = 150 / 500 = **30%**; net profit ratio = 88.20 / 1,000 = **8.82%**.

ROE = 88.20 / 300 = **29.4%**. Shares = 20 lakh, so EPS = **₹4.41**.

DuPont: 8.82% x (1,000 / 600 = 1.6667) x (600 / 300 = 2.0) = 0.0882 x 1.6667 x 2 = 0.2940 =
**29.4%**, equal to the direct ROE.
@sanity: Three checks. The DuPont product must return the direct ROE to the decimal — it does.
ROCE (30%) exceeds the pre-tax cost of debt (12%), so gearing works for the owners, consistent with
ROE landing close to ROCE despite tax. And collection (29 days) is faster than payment (46 days),
so suppliers finance the firm — if yours reversed, check which turnover you divided by which.
:::

:::trap Mixing closing balances with averages inside one answer
Turnover ratios are a flow over an average stock, so averages are preferable and questions often say
"average inventory". Given only a closing balance sheet, closing figures are the only honest basis —
state the assumption once and hold it across every ratio. The mark is for the disclosure and the
consistency. Same for 365 against 360 days: pick one, say so, never switch.
:::

:::think Debtor velocity is 29 days but the stated credit policy is 60 days. Is the ratio wrong?
No, and the finding is favourable. Always compare a ratio against **policy, prior year and
industry** — alone it is a number, not a diagnosis. The reverse case is the examinable one:
collection beyond policy points to weak credit control and possibly unrecognised bad debts.
:::

**Limitations of ratio analysis** is a stock question: ratios are historical and say nothing about
the future; no single correct standard exists to compare against; accounting policy differences on
depreciation and inventory destroy comparability; price-level changes distort trends; window
dressing flatters liquidity; a balance sheet ratio is a single-date snapshot hiding seasonality;
aggregation conceals the mix inside a total; qualitative factors are invisible; and a ratio shows a
**symptom**, never a cause.

:::attempt Net profit ratio has fallen from 10% to 8% while ROE has risen from 24% to 27%. Explain how both can be true, and state which ratios you would compute next.
Write the identity first — **ROE = net profit margin x total asset turnover x equity multiplier** — and
the rest is mechanical: with margin down, ROE still rises if assets turn faster, or if the equity
multiplier has risen through more debt or a return of capital. Then name the next computations:

- **Total asset turnover** and the individual turnover ratios test the efficiency story; a genuine
  improvement shows as higher inventory and receivable turnover.
- **Debt–equity, interest coverage and DSCR** test the gearing story. If the multiplier did the
  work, ROE rose because risk rose.
- **ROCE** is the referee, being unaffected by financing mix. Rising ROCE supports efficiency;
  ROCE flat while ROE rises is gearing.

Close with the sentence examiners look for: an ROE gain achieved purely through leverage is not
value creation, because the equity holder's required return rises with gearing.
:::
