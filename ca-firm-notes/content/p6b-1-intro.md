## Unit 1 — Introduction to Strategic Management

Section B is 50 marks and the shortest syllabus in Group II. It is also the easiest half-paper
in this group to score well on, for one reason: almost every answer is a **named framework
applied to the facts of a scenario**. Learn to draw the frameworks, learn the three-move answer
shape below, and the marks follow.

### How this paper is marked — apply this to every unit

1. **Name the framework and say why it fits.** One sentence. "The company is choosing between
   existing and new products in existing and new markets, so Ansoff's Matrix is the tool."
2. **Apply it to the facts you were given.** Take the specifics — the growth rate, the market
   share, the loss-making unit, the promoter's remark — and place each inside the framework. Quote
   the fact, then say what the framework makes of it. Most of the marks live here.
3. **Conclude with a recommendation.** Which option does the analysis point to, and why. An answer
   that analyses and never decides is unfinished.

:::trap Describing the framework instead of applying it to the scenario
The single largest source of lost marks in Section B. A question gives a company with four
business units and asks you to advise; the answer explains what the BCG Matrix is, names the four
cells, and stops. The examiner has no fact of the case to award against. Write the framework in a
line or two, spend the rest on the company in front of you — naming units, figures and stages —
and finish with the recommendation.
:::

### Meaning and nature of strategic management

Strategic management is the process by which management decides the organisation's long-term
direction and scope, chooses the businesses and markets it will compete in, allocates resources
to that choice, and puts the structures and controls in place to make it happen — in an
environment it does not control.

Four questions define the process, and they are the spine of the section:

| Question | Where it is answered |
|---|---|
| Where are we now? | Units 2 and 3 — external and internal analysis |
| Where do we want to be? | Unit 1 — strategic intent |
| What are our options, and which do we take? | Unit 4 — strategic choices |
| How do we make it happen, and how do we know it is working? | Unit 5 — implementation, control, measures |

Its **nature**, as an answer should state it:

- It concerns the **whole organisation** and the long term, not a department and not a year.
- It is about **choice**: what to do, and equally what to give up.
- It is **continuous and iterative**, not an annual document. Strategy is partly deliberate and
  partly emergent — what a firm does is rarely exactly what it planned.
- It is done under **uncertainty**, so it deals in judgement, not computation.
- It is both **analytical** (frameworks, data) and **behavioural** (leadership, culture,
  resistance), which is why Unit 5 exists.
- It is **top management's** responsibility, but needs the whole organisation to execute.

**Importance.** It gives a direction against which smaller decisions can be tested; makes the firm
proactive rather than reactive; provides the basis for allocating scarce resources; improves
coordination, because units pull the same way; anticipates change instead of improvising; gives
management something to control against; and builds advantage that lasts.

**Limitations.** The environment is uncertain, so the forecasts it rests on can be wrong; it costs
money and senior management time; over-formalised, it produces plans nobody uses; a plan treated as
sacred creates rigidity in a market that has moved; good strategies still fail in execution; and the
benefits are long-term and hard to attribute, so management under-invests in them.

:::key Strategy is a choice about direction and scope made under uncertainty — never a forecast, and never a plan that binds.
Describing strategic management as "long-term planning" understates it and loses the mark. The
distinguishing features are **choice** (options were rejected), **scope** (the whole organisation,
and which businesses it is in) and **environment fit** (internal capability matched to external
conditions).
:::

### Strategic intent — vision, mission, goals, objectives, values

**Strategic intent** is the umbrella term for what the organisation stands for and stretches
towards. It is expressed through five things, and the exam separates them relentlessly.

| Element | Question it answers | Horizon | Test of a good one |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Vision** | What do we want to become? | Long, aspirational | Short, ambitious, future-tense, memorable, stretches the firm |
| **Mission** | Why do we exist, what business are we in, for whom? | Present | States the customer group, the need served and the means used |
| **Goals** | What broad outcomes must we achieve to get there? | Medium, open-ended | Derived from the mission; qualitative direction |
| **Objectives** | What exactly, by how much, by when? | Specific and dated | Measurable, time-bound, assigned to someone |
| **Values** | How will we behave while doing it? | Permanent | Few, non-negotiable, actually constrain decisions |

The hierarchy runs **vision → mission → goals → objectives**, with values cutting across all four
as a constraint on means. A mission that does not narrow the business is useless: "to serve
humanity" tells nobody which products to launch. An objective with no measure in it is a goal
wearing the wrong label.

> **Verify:** the goals-versus-objectives boundary is drawn slightly differently across editions
> of the Study Material — some treat goals as open-ended and objectives as close-ended and
> time-bound, others use the words more loosely. Use your attempt's Study Material wording.

:::ladder A promoter says: "Our mission is to become the most admired logistics company in India." Comment.
@weak: The mission statement is good because it is short, ambitious and sets a clear target to work
towards.
@good: The statement is a **vision**, not a mission. It describes an aspirational future state and
says nothing about what business the firm is in, whom it serves, or how. A mission must define the
business: customer group, need met, means used.
@great: The same identification, plus the correction and its consequence. As a mission it would read
closer to "to move time-critical freight for Indian manufacturers reliably at the lowest cost per
tonne-kilometre, using an owned fleet and a digital tracking platform". The distinction is
operational, not semantic: objectives and resource allocation are derived from the mission, so a
firm with only a vision has nothing to translate into targets and each unit fills the gap its own
way. **Recommendation:** keep the sentence as the vision and write a separate mission defining
customer, need and means, from which annual objectives follow.
:::

### Strategic levels in organisations

Four levels, each with its own question. **Network** is the level candidates omit, and it is
named first in the syllabus.

```board
   NETWORK LEVEL        the firm plus its alliances, joint ventures, franchisees,
        |               outsourcing partners, platform and supply-chain partners
        |               Q: with whom do we cooperate, and what stays inside the firm?
        v
   CORPORATE LEVEL      the whole group / the multi-business firm
        |               Q: which businesses should we be in, and how do we allocate
        |                  resources between them?
        v
   BUSINESS LEVEL       one strategic business unit, one product-market
        |               Q: how do we compete and win in this particular business?
        v
   FUNCTIONAL LEVEL     marketing, operations, finance, HR, IT, procurement
                        Q: how does each function deliver the business strategy?
```

- **Network** strategy operates across organisational boundaries. The firm is one node in a web of
  partners, so the decisions are which partners to hold, what to keep in-house and what to source,
  how value and risk are shared, and how control works without ownership. Franchising, joint
  ventures, contract manufacturing and platform ecosystems are network-level questions.
- **Corporate** strategy is the portfolio: which businesses, entry and exit, diversification,
  resources shared across units. Unit 4's BCG and GE matrices are corporate tools.
- **Business** strategy is about competing in one business; Porter's generic strategies are the
  business-level tool.
- **Functional** strategy converts that into functional decisions and targets, and is where
  implementation bites.

:::think Which level answers "should we exit cement and put the money into logistics?"
Corporate — a portfolio question about which businesses the group should be in. "How do we beat the
two rivals in cement?" is business level; "should we lease the fleet from a partner rather than own
it?" is network level.
:::

:::dialogue
@P: They have four levels on their strategy slide. Name them.
@Y: Corporate, business and functional.
@P: You have just dropped the level that decides whether they own the warehouses or rent them
from their partner.
@N: Four levels are expected, and Network needs a defining line about strategy across
organisational boundaries — alliances, joint ventures, franchisees, outsourced partners.
:::

:::attempt Distinguish vision, mission, goals, objectives and values, giving a one-line illustration of each for a city bus operator.
Separate all five on **question answered, horizon and testability**, and illustrate each rather than
defining it twice.

- **Vision** — what we want to become. "To be the city's first choice for daily travel." No measure.
- **Mission** — why we exist and for whom. "To move commuters across the city safely, on schedule
  and at a fare they can afford, on a clean fleet." Customer, need, means.
- **Goals** — broad outcomes required. "Improve schedule reliability." Direction, no number.
- **Objectives** — the same goal made specific. "Raise on-time departures from 82% to 95% within
  eighteen months." Measurable, dated, owned.
- **Values** — how we behave regardless. "Passenger safety is never traded against schedule."

Two marks sit on the relationships: objectives come from goals, goals from the mission, the mission
serves the vision, values constrain the means at every level. Marks are lost by writing a vision in
the mission row, or an objective with no number in it.
:::
