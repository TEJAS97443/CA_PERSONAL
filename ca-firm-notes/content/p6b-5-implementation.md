## Unit 5 — Strategy Implementation and Evaluation

A strategy that exists only on paper has produced nothing. This unit turns the Unit 4 choice into
structure, behaviour and measurement — and asks, while it runs, whether it is still the right
strategy.

### Formulation versus implementation

| | Strategy formulation | Strategy implementation |
|---|---|---|
| The work | Positioning the firm against its environment | Managing forces inside the firm while it operates |
| Character | Entrepreneurial, analytical, judgemental | Administrative, operational, behavioural |
| Who | A few people at the top | Many people at every level |
| Skills | Analysis, conceptual thinking, intuition | Leadership, motivation, coordination, control |
| Timing | Before resources are committed | After, and continuously |
| Emphasis | Effectiveness — the right things | Efficiency — things done right |
| Failure | The wrong direction chosen | The right direction not delivered |

**Linkages and issues.** The **forward linkage** runs from formulation to implementation: the strategy
chosen dictates the structure, systems, resource allocation and skills to be put in place, so a
diversification and a cost-leadership strategy need different organisations. The **backward linkage**
runs the other way: existing structure, culture and capability constrain which strategies are
realistic. The recurring **issues** are resource allocation to the new priorities rather than the old,
fit of structure and systems to the strategy, leadership, culture and resistance, sequence and timing,
and rewards still pointing at the previous objectives.

:::key Formulation asks what to do; implementation asks how, by whom, with what and by when. Most strategies fail in the second question.
Where a question asks you to discuss implementation issues, the marks are on internal mechanics —
structure, systems, resources, people, culture, timing, control — not on re-justifying the strategy.
Re-arguing the choice is the commonest way to answer the wrong question.
:::

### Strategic change through digital transformation

Three words are not interchangeable. **Digitisation** converts information to digital form.
**Digitalisation** uses digital technology to run existing processes better. **Digital
transformation** changes the **business model, the value proposition and the customer experience** —
what the firm sells, to whom, and how it captures value. That is why it sits here: it is strategic
change, managed as change.

1. Establish the case and the urgency — what makes the current model unsustainable.
2. Set the vision for the changed business, in customer outcomes, not technology.
3. Assess the capability gap: data, skills, systems, processes, partners.
4. Pilot narrow, learn, then scale — evidence beats resistance more cheaply than argument.
5. Realign the hard elements: structure, roles, decision rights, targets, reward.
6. Build the soft side until the new way survives without sponsorship.

**Enablers:** leadership sponsorship, usable data and integrated systems, skills, iterative working,
and controls over privacy and cyber risk. **Barriers:** legacy systems and hard-wired processes, skill
gaps, culture and resistance, cost against an uncertain return, unclear ownership, security exposure,
and change fatigue.

### Organisation structure (hard) and culture (soft)

| | Structure — the **hard** side | Culture — the **soft** side |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Reporting lines, departments, decision rights, systems, procedures, targets | Shared beliefs, values, assumptions, habits, informal networks |
| Visibility | Visible; can be drawn | Invisible; inferred from behaviour |
| How it changes | By management decision, quickly | Slowly, by consistent behaviour and example; cannot be mandated |
| Imitability | Easily copied | Very hard to copy, so a source of advantage |
| Failure mode | A structure that no longer fits the strategy | A culture that quietly defeats the structure |

The structural forms, one line each: **functional** (by activity — efficient, weak across products);
**divisional** (by product, market or geography — accountable, duplicative); **strategic business
unit** (related divisions under one head, to make a large portfolio governable); **matrix** (dual
reporting — flexible, conflicting authority); **network** (a small core coordinating outsourced
partners — flexible, dependent, hard to control).

Both sides must move together. Redrawing the chart while leaving culture untouched produces a new
structure operated by old behaviour; announcing values while targets and rewards still point at the old
strategy produces cynicism. Where the two conflict, culture usually wins, because it decides what
people do when nobody is checking.

:::trap Treating culture as a detail that "will follow" once the structure changes
Culture is the slowest element to change and the one that decides whether the structure functions. An
implementation answer that lists structure, systems and resources but never reaches culture, resistance
and reward alignment leaves marks on the table — and this is precisely where the hard/soft distinction
is examined. Say which changes are hard (fast, by decision) and which are soft (slow, by behaviour and
example), and that the soft work must start earliest.
:::

### Strategic leadership

Strategic leadership is the capacity to set direction and get an organisation to move in it. Its
tasks: **communicating the direction**, **building the capability** the strategy needs, **allocating
resources** consistently with the choice made, **shaping culture** by example and by what gets
rewarded, **sustaining the change** past the point where enthusiasm fades, and **developing
successors**. Two styles are contrasted: **transformational**, which changes what people aspire to and
suits major strategic change, and **transactional**, which works through targets, monitoring and
reward and suits executing an agreed strategy efficiently. Strategy and situation decide which fits.

### Strategic control

Strategic control steers the strategy **while it is being executed**, because the assumptions it
rested on can change before it is finished. Four types:

| Type | Question it asks | Trigger and pace |
|---|---|---|
| **Premise control** | Are the assumptions the strategy was built on still true? | Continuous, focused on those specific premises |
| **Implementation control** | Is execution on track, and do the results so far justify continuing? | At milestones; can conclude the strategy should be abandoned |
| **Strategic surveillance** | Is anything happening anywhere that could affect the strategy? | Broad, deliberately unfocused scanning |
| **Special alert control** | A sudden unexpected event has occurred — does the strategy still hold? | Reactive, immediate |

| | Strategic control | Operational control |
|---|---|---|
| Object | The strategy and its premises | Current operations against plan or standard |
| Horizon | The whole period of the strategy | A month, a quarter |
| Question | Are we doing the right things? | Are we doing things right, within budget? |
| Data | Largely external, qualitative | Largely internal, quantitative |
| Correction | May change the strategy itself | Brings performance back to the existing plan |
| Who | Top management | Line and functional management |

The distinction that earns the mark: operational control corrects performance **against** the plan;
strategic control is willing to conclude that the **plan itself** is wrong.

### Strategic performance measures

Measures exist so that control has something to work with. A good set is derived from the objectives,
**few** enough to focus attention, a mix of **financial** and **non-financial** and of **lagging** and
**leading** indicators, within someone's control, and comparable over time and against competitors.
Financial: profitability, revenue growth, return on capital employed, margins, cash generation.
Non-financial: market share, customer satisfaction and retention, quality and defect rates, delivery
reliability, employee retention and skills, innovation, sustainability. The non-financial ones usually
lead — satisfaction falls before revenue does, which is why a purely financial set reports problems too
late to act on. When the strategy changes the measures must change with it.

:::think Sales are ahead of budget, but the strategy assumed the market would grow 15% and it grew 4%. Which control has been breached?
Premise control. The assumption the strategy was built on has failed even though implementation is
running ahead of plan — beating budget in a 4% market may mean share is being won, but the strategy's
basis has changed. Operational control would report a favourable variance and miss it entirely.
:::

:::ladder A manufacturer restructured into three product divisions eighteen months ago to support a differentiation strategy. Divisional heads still refer decisions to the managing director, launches are late, and staff say "nothing has really changed." Advise.
@weak: The company should implement the new structure properly and instruct staff to follow it, and
the managing director should stop interfering in divisional matters.
@good: Identifies a hard change without a matching soft change. The structure was altered but
culture, decision rights and rewards were not, so behaviour stayed centralised. Recommends
delegating authority formally, aligning divisional targets and rewards to divisional results, and
communicating consistently.
@great: The same diagnosis in the unit's own terms, decided on the facts given. This is a **forward
linkage** failure: differentiation required fast decentralised decisions and the structure was changed
to provide them, but the hard change was left incomplete — decision rights, budgets and rewards still
sit centrally — and the soft change was never begun. The evidence is in the facts: referral upward is a
decision-rights problem, late launches are the consequence, and "nothing has really changed" is culture
reporting that behaviour has not moved. **Recommendation:** complete the hard side first, because it is
fast and visible — write down what each divisional head may decide unaided, move budget authority down,
reset targets and incentives to divisional outcomes. Then work the slow soft side: the managing
director must visibly stop accepting referred decisions, since one reversal teaches more than any
announcement. Add implementation control with a six-month milestone review — and if divisional results
show the differentiation strategy itself is failing, the answer is premise control, not more
restructuring.
:::

:::attempt Distinguish strategic control from operational control, name the four types of strategic control, and give one situation each type would detect that the others would miss.
Separate the two on **object, horizon, question, data and the kind of correction**, and state the core
difference: operational control corrects performance against the existing plan, while strategic control
is prepared to conclude that the plan is wrong.

- **Premise control** — the strategy assumed a tariff would stay and it has been withdrawn. No internal
  report shows this; only a check of the premises does.
- **Implementation control** — two years into a five-year capacity programme the first two plants run
  at half the assumed utilisation, which raises whether to continue at all, not how to catch up.
- **Strategic surveillance** — an unrelated industry's technology becomes a substitute. No premise
  mentioned it and no milestone catches it; broad scanning does.
- **Special alert control** — a fire at the sole supplier's plant, or a sudden regulatory ban, needing
  immediate reassessment rather than a scheduled review.

The last mark is for the point that these are complementary: a firm running only implementation control
will execute an obsolete strategy competently.
:::

:::checkpoint Revision checklist — Strategic Management
**Frameworks you must be able to draw and label from memory.** For each, the axes are the mark:

- **PESTLE** — six heads: political, economic, social, technological, legal, environmental. Run
  country by country for the international environment.
- **Porter's Five Forces** — new entrants, buyers, suppliers, substitutes, rivalry in the centre;
  gives the profit available in the industry.
- **Value chain (Porter)** — five primary activities (inbound logistics, operations, outbound
  logistics, marketing and sales, service), four support activities (firm infrastructure, HRM,
  technology development, procurement), plus margin.
- **Product life cycle** — introduction, growth, maturity, decline, with the strategy for each stage.
- **Mendelow's model** — power against level of interest: minimal effort, keep informed, keep
  satisfied, key players.
- **SWOT** — internal/external against helpful/harmful, and the four pairings SO, ST, WO, WT.
- **Porter's generic strategies** — advantage (cost, differentiation) against scope (broad, narrow):
  cost leadership, differentiation, cost focus, differentiation focus, and stuck in the middle.
- **Ansoff's Matrix** — products against markets: market penetration, product development, market
  development, diversification.
- **BCG Matrix** — relative market share against market growth: stars, question marks, cash cows,
  dogs; build, hold, harvest, divest.
- **GE Matrix** — industry attractiveness against business strength, nine cells, three zones:
  invest/grow, selectivity/earnings, harvest/divest.
- **ADL Matrix** — industry maturity (embryonic, growth, mature, ageing) against competitive
  position (dominant, strong, favourable, tenable, weak); thrusts of natural development, selective
  development, prove viability, withdraw.
- **Formulation–implementation matrix** — success, roulette, trouble, failure.
- **Strategic levels** — network, corporate, business, functional. Network is the one candidates
  omit.

**Attributions to keep straight.** Porter owns the five forces, the value chain and the generic
strategies. Ansoff owns the product-market matrix. Mendelow owns the stakeholder grid. BCG, GE and
ADL are firm-named matrices, not personal ones.

**Term pairs the examiner separates.** Vision / mission / goals / objectives / values. Strategy
formulation / implementation. Concentric / conglomerate diversification. Horizontal / vertical
integration. Turnaround / divestiture / liquidation. Strategic control / operational control.
Digitisation / digitalisation / digital transformation. Structure (hard) / culture (soft).

**The answer-technique rule, worth more than any single framework.** Name the framework and say why it
fits; apply it to the facts given, naming those facts; conclude with a recommendation. Describing a
framework without applying it to the scenario is the single most common way marks are lost here, and an
analysis that never decides is unfinished.

**What to verify against the Study Guidelines and current Study Material for your attempt.** The
goals-versus-objectives wording; the labelling of the two off-diagonal cells of the
formulation–implementation matrix; whether your edition's ADL matrix uses a sixth "non-viable"
position, and its cell-by-cell allocation of thrusts; the digital transformation terminology and
stage lists; and the named set of strategic performance measures. ICAI resets inclusions and
exclusions every year through the Study Guidelines, and these notes cannot substitute for them.
:::
