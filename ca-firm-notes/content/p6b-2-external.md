## Unit 2 — Strategic Analysis: External Environment

External analysis answers one question: what is happening outside the firm that it does not control
but must respond to. It works in three layers, and the tool changes with the layer.

```board
  +-------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |  MACRO / INTERNATIONAL ENVIRONMENT          tool: PESTLE          |
  |  affects every industry; the firm cannot influence it             |
  |   +-----------------------------------------------------------+   |
  |   |  INDUSTRY ENVIRONMENT        tool: Porter's Five Forces    |   |
  |   |  decides how much profit there is to be made at all        |   |
  |   |   +---------------------------------------------------+   |   |
  |   |   |  MARKET                                            |   |   |
  |   |   |  customers, segments, channels, named competitors  |   |   |
  |   |   +---------------------------------------------------+   |   |
  |   +-----------------------------------------------------------+   |
  +-------------------------------------------------------------------+
   Boundary-setting tools that say WHICH industry you are analysing:
   the value chain (which activities are ours, which belong to others)
   and the product life cycle (which stage the industry has reached).
```

### PESTLE — the macro and international environment

Six heads. Name them, then attach the question's facts to each; an empty head is better left out than
padded.

| Head | What it covers | Generic illustration |
|---|---|---|
| **Political** | Government stability and stance, policy direction, taxation and subsidy, trade and industrial policy | A state announcing an incentive scheme for electric fleets |
| **Economic** | Growth, inflation, interest and exchange rates, incomes, employment, credit, commodity prices | Rates rising for a business that sells on consumer finance |
| **Social** | Demographics, urbanisation, education, lifestyle, health awareness, attitudes to brands | Smaller households shifting demand to single-serve packs |
| **Technological** | New products and processes, automation, digital platforms, obsolescence, R&D intensity | A distributor facing platform aggregators |
| **Legal** | Enacted law and compliance — company, labour, competition, consumer, data protection, licensing | New product-labelling requirements |
| **Environmental** | Climate and weather, resource scarcity, emissions and waste rules, sustainability expectations | A monsoon-dependent input for an agri-processor |

**Political against Legal** is the distinction examiners test: political is the *stance and
direction* of government, legal is *enacted law you must comply with*. A proposed policy is
political; a notified statute is legal.

For the **international environment**, PESTLE is run **country by country**, plus the factors that
exist only across borders: trade policy and tariffs, exchange-rate exposure, limits on repatriating
profits, local-content and ownership rules, cultural distance, and differences in infrastructure and
enforcement.

**What the manager does with the output.** Classify each factor as an opportunity or a threat, rank
it by impact and probability, and carry only the material ones into the O and T of the SWOT in Unit
3. A PESTLE that ends as a list of headings has done nothing.

:::key PESTLE describes the environment; Five Forces explains the profit in it. They are not substitutes and the exam expects the right one.
Government policy, demographics, technology or exchange rates: PESTLE. Why margins in this industry
are thin, who captures the value, whether to enter: Five Forces. An industry facing a new regulation
may need both — PESTLE to identify the change, Five Forces to work out what it does to industry
profitability.
:::

### Defining the industry for analysis — the value chain

The **value chain** (Michael Porter) splits the firm's activities into those that create the
product and those that support them, so that value added and cost can be traced activity by
activity.

```board
  SUPPORT      Firm infrastructure  (planning, finance, legal, MIS)        \
  ACTIVITIES   Human resource management                                    |
               Technology development                                       |  M
               Procurement                                                  |  A
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------   |  R
  PRIMARY      Inbound  ->  Operations  ->  Outbound  ->  Marketing  ->     |  G
  ACTIVITIES   logistics                    logistics     and sales  ->     |  I
                                                          Service          /   N
```

Five primary activities — inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales,
service. Four support activities — firm infrastructure, human resource management, technology
development, procurement. What the buyer's price leaves after the cost of all of them is the
**margin**.

Used in *external* analysis it sets the industry boundary: extend the chain back into suppliers and
forward into channels and buyers and you can see which activities the industry actually owns, where
value is added, and therefore whose profit pool you are analysing. Two firms that claim the same
industry may occupy different spans of the same chain. The internal use of the tool — cost and
differentiation drivers inside the firm — belongs with resources and capabilities in Unit 3.

**What the manager does with the output.** Decide which activities to own and which to outsource,
where to attack cost, and which activity is the real source of differentiation.

### Defining the industry for analysis — the product life cycle

```board
  Sales
    ^                                  ______
    |                             ____/      \____
    |                        ____/                \___
    |                  _____/                         \____
    |         ________/                                    \___
    +----------------------------------------------------------------> Time
      INTRODUCTION   |   GROWTH      |   MATURITY      |   DECLINE
      sales low      | sales rise    | sales peak,     | sales fall
      profit nil/neg | profit rises  | growth slows    | profit falls
      few rivals     | entrants rush | many rivals,    | exits, shake-out
      high unit cost | cost falls    | price pressure  | overcapacity
      build awareness| build share   | defend share,   | harvest, or
      and channels   | and capacity  | cut cost, extend| withdraw
```

**What the manager does with the output.** The stage predicts the intensity of competition, whether
the business absorbs or releases cash, and which strategies are even available — share-building in
growth, cost and differentiation defence in maturity, harvest or exit in decline. It is also one axis
of the ADL Matrix and sits behind BCG's growth axis in Unit 4.

:::trap Reading the life cycle off the company's own sales instead of the industry's
The PLC describes a product or industry, not a firm. A company whose sales are falling in a growing
industry is losing share, not facing decline, and the two need opposite strategies — a competitive
response, not harvest or exit. Check the industry growth figure the question gives before naming a
stage.
:::

### Porter's Five Forces — industry environment analysis

```board
                        THREAT OF NEW ENTRANTS
                    (entry barriers, scale economies,
                     capital need, brands, regulation,
                     access to channels, retaliation)
                                  |
                                  v
   BARGAINING POWER  ------>  RIVALRY AMONG   <------  BARGAINING POWER
   OF SUPPLIERS               EXISTING FIRMS            OF BUYERS
   (few suppliers,          (number and size of        (few large buyers,
    unique input,            rivals, industry           standard product,
    switching cost,          growth, fixed cost,        low switching cost,
    forward integration      exit barriers,             price sensitivity,
    threat)                  differentiation)           backward integration
                                  ^                     threat)
                                  |
                        THREAT OF SUBSTITUTES
                    (a different product meeting the
                     same need; relative price and
                     performance; buyer's willingness
                     to switch)
```

The five forces collectively determine **how much profit is available in the industry**, not how well
one firm is doing. Strong forces compress margins for everyone; weak forces leave a pool worth
entering.

**What the manager does with the output.** Three decisions: whether to enter or exit; where to
position so as to face the weakest forces; and which force to weaken — switching costs against buyer
power, long-term contracts or backward integration against supplier power, brand and scale to raise
entry barriers.

:::attempt Draw Porter's Five Forces and give two determinants of each force. Then say what the model tells a firm that the PESTLE analysis cannot.
Draw the five with rivalry in the centre, give two credible determinants each, and close on purpose
rather than repeating the diagram.

- **New entrants** — height of entry barriers (capital, scale, brand, licences); expected retaliation.
- **Suppliers** — supplier concentration relative to the industry; uniqueness of the input and the cost
  of switching.
- **Buyers** — buyer concentration and volume per buyer; a standardised product with low switching
  cost, making price the only variable.
- **Substitutes** — relative price and performance; the buyer's propensity to switch.
- **Rivalry** — number and size of competitors; industry growth rate.

The closing point earns the last mark: PESTLE tells you what is changing; Five Forces tells you
**what that change does to this industry's profitability and who captures the value**. Only the
second answers "should we be in this business, and how do we position?"
:::

:::think A firm says its industry has "only two competitors, so competition is low." What has it missed?
Four of the five forces. Buyers and suppliers can compress its margin without any competitor acting,
a substitute product can take the demand away entirely, and a concentrated, profitable industry
invites entrants. Rivalry is one force of five.
:::

### Understanding customers and markets

The industry sets the profit pool; market analysis says whose money it is.

- **Segment** on a basis that changes buying behaviour — geography, demographics, income, usage rate,
  benefit sought, industrial buyer size or application.
- **Separate the roles**: who decides, who uses and who pays are often three parties, and the
  marketing follows whoever decides.
- **Establish the buying criteria** per segment, and which of them wins the order rather than merely
  qualifying the firm — price, availability, quality, service, brand, credit.
- **Size the segment** and **check the channel**: size, growth and profitability; who controls the
  route to the customer and what it takes of the price.

### Competition in the industry

Competitor analysis is specific, not general. For each significant rival establish four things: its
**objectives** (share, margin, survival), its **assumptions** about the industry, its **current
strategy** and the **capabilities** behind it. Together they predict how it will respond to what you
plan to do, which is the point.

Then place the rivals: **direct** competitors serving the same segment with the same product,
**substitute** competitors meeting the same need differently, and **potential** entrants. Firms with
similar strategies and resources form a **strategic group**, and rivalry inside a group is sharper than
between groups, so the group constrains a firm's options more than the industry average. Note the
**basis of competition** too: where rivals compete on price, margins fall; on differentiation and
service, margins can hold.

:::ladder A packaged-snacks company reports that industry sales are growing 12% a year, its four rivals are all expanding capacity, retailers are consolidating into three large chains, and a low-cost imported substitute has appeared. Analyse the industry.
@weak: The industry is attractive because it is growing at 12% a year, though there is
competition from four rivals and imports.
@good: Applies Five Forces to the facts. Rivalry is high — four rivals adding capacity in an industry
with high fixed costs. Buyer power is rising because three chains now control retail access. The
substitute threat is real and price-based. Growth of 12% places the industry in the growth stage, which
explains the capacity additions and the entrants. Concludes that profitability will be squeezed despite
the growth.
@great: The same, force by force with the fact named against each — and only the facts given. Note what
the passing answer quietly added: **high fixed costs** and **entrants** appear nowhere in the question,
and the marker cannot award an invented determinant, however plausible. Rivalry is high on the stated
facts alone: four rivals, all adding capacity. Supplier power cannot be assessed on these facts at all
and should be flagged, not filled in. The 12% growth is what makes rivalry survivable for now, because
demand is absorbing the new capacity — so the danger point is the moment growth slows and that capacity
turns into a price war.
**Recommendation:** use the growth phase to build a differentiated position and shelf relationships
with the three chains before retail consolidation completes, rather than matching capacity; and
answer the imported substitute on quality and freshness, not price, which is the substitute's own
ground.
:::
