## Unit 4 — Strategic Choices

Units 2 and 3 tell the firm where it stands. This unit is the menu of what it can do about it and the
four matrices that generate and screen the options. Each matrix is drawable in a minute, and in a
scenario question the drawing plus correct placement of the facts is most of the mark.

### The menu of strategic choices

| Choice | What it is | Appropriate when |
|---|---|---|
| **Market development** | Existing products into new markets — geography, segment or channel | The home market is saturating |
| **Product development** | New or modified products for existing customers | The customer relationship is strong, the range dated |
| **Innovation** | Something materially new — product, process or business model | A shift extension will not hold |
| **Concentric diversification** | A **related** new business sharing technology, market, channel or skill | There is a real link to exploit |
| **Conglomerate diversification** | An **unrelated** new business; the logic is financial | Risk spreading, or surplus cash |
| **Horizontal integration** | A firm at the **same stage** of the same industry | Scale, share or capacity |
| **Vertical integration** | A **different stage of the same chain** — back to suppliers, forward to channels | Supply or access is unreliable, or that margin is worth capturing |
| **Turnaround** | Keep it and fix it — cost cuts, asset reduction, refocus, new management | Decline is recent and reversible |
| **Divestiture** | Sell or spin off a unit; the rest continues | Worth more to another owner |
| **Liquidation** | Close and sell the assets; exit | No buyer and no viable fix |

Four pairs get conflated, and the marks are in the distinction:

- **Concentric versus conglomerate** — relatedness. Concentric shares something operational
  (technology, customers, channel, brand, skill), so synergy can be claimed; conglomerate shares
  nothing operational, so its only case is risk spreading or surplus funds, and its typical failure
  is management that does not understand the new business.
- **Horizontal versus vertical** — direction. Horizontal moves **sideways** at the same stage, so
  the firm does more of what it already does; vertical moves **along** the chain into work it has
  never done. A biscuit maker buying another biscuit maker is horizontal; buying its flour mill is
  backward vertical; buying its distributor is forward vertical.
- **Turnaround, divestiture, liquidation** — escalating severity, each with a test. Viable if fixed?
  Worth more to someone else? Any buyer or fix at all? The answer follows from the test.
- **Market development, product development, diversification** — which of market and product is new.
  That is Ansoff's entire point.

:::key Every strategic-choice question reduces to "what is new here — the product, the market, both, or the stage of the value chain?"
Classify the situation first; the label follows. Then give the condition that makes the choice
appropriate and the risk that makes it dangerous. The examiner is testing classification and
judgement, not ten definitions.
:::

### Ansoff's Matrix

Ansoff's product-market matrix crosses **products** (existing, new) with **markets** (existing,
new). Risk rises as you move away from the top-left corner.

```board
                             PRODUCTS
                   EXISTING              NEW
              +--------------------+--------------------+
   EXISTING   |  MARKET            |  PRODUCT           |
              |  PENETRATION       |  DEVELOPMENT       |
   M          |  sell more of the  |  new products to   |
   A          |  same to the same  |  the same          |
   R          |  buyers            |  customers         |
   K          |  lowest risk       |  moderate risk     |
   E          +--------------------+--------------------+
   T          |  MARKET            |  DIVERSIFICATION   |
   S   NEW    |  DEVELOPMENT       |  new products to   |
              |  same products to  |  new markets       |
              |  new markets or    |  highest risk;     |
              |  segments          |  concentric or     |
              |  moderate risk     |  conglomerate      |
              +--------------------+--------------------+
```

**What the manager does with the output.** Generate all four options before choosing, then price the
risk: penetration uses known products in known markets and is cheapest to test, diversification
changes both variables at once and needs capability the firm may not have.

### BCG Matrix

Relative market share (the unit's share against the largest competitor's) against market growth
rate. A portfolio tool, so it is used at corporate level across units or products.

```board
                        RELATIVE MARKET SHARE
                   HIGH                    LOW
              +----------------------+----------------------+
              |  STARS               |  QUESTION MARKS      |
      HIGH    |  high growth,        |  (problem children)  |
              |  high share          |  high growth,        |
   M          |  cash use ~ cash gen |  low share           |
   A          |  STRATEGY: build /   |  cash absorbing      |
   R          |  invest to hold      |  STRATEGY: build     |
   K          |  leadership          |  selectively, or     |
   E          |                      |  divest              |
   T          +----------------------+----------------------+
              |  CASH COWS           |  DOGS                |
   G  LOW     |  low growth,         |  low growth,         |
   R          |  high share          |  low share           |
   O          |  strong net cash     |  little cash either  |
   W          |  generator           |  way                 |
   T          |  STRATEGY: hold and  |  STRATEGY: divest or |
   H          |  harvest; fund the   |  liquidate; retain   |
              |  rest of the group   |  only for a reason   |
              +----------------------+----------------------+

   Cash logic: cows fund stars and the selected question marks.
   Desired path: question mark -> star -> cash cow.  Failure path:
   question mark -> dog, or a starved star sliding into a dog.
```

**What the manager does with the output.** Allocate the group's cash: **build** (stars and question
marks worth backing), **hold** (strong cows), **harvest** (weak cows and question marks not worth
backing), **divest** (dogs) — then check balance: all cows means no future, all question marks means
no cash.

:::trap Using absolute market share, or the company's own growth rate, on the BCG axes
Both axes are specific. The horizontal axis is **relative** share — the unit's divided by the
largest rival's — so 20% is high against a leader at 12% and low against a leader at 45%. The
vertical axis is the **market's** growth, not the unit's sales growth. Get either wrong and every
unit lands in the wrong cell, which costs the question rather than a mark. And a low-share unit in a
*growing* market is a question mark, not a dog.
:::

### GE Matrix — nine cells

The GE matrix answers the criticism that BCG uses one measure per axis. Both axes are **composites**:
**industry attractiveness** (size, growth, margins, competitive intensity, regulation) and **business
strength** (relative share, margins, brand, technology, cost position, management).

```board
                        INDUSTRY ATTRACTIVENESS
                  HIGH            MEDIUM            LOW
             +---------------+---------------+---------------+
   STRONG    | INVEST /      | INVEST /      | SELECTIVITY / |
             | GROW          | GROW          | EARNINGS      |
   B         | protect and   | invest to     | manage for    |
   U         | build         | build         | earnings      |
   S         +---------------+---------------+---------------+
   I AVERAGE | INVEST /      | SELECTIVITY / | HARVEST /     |
   N         | GROW          | EARNINGS      | DIVEST        |
   E         | build         | protect and   | limited       |
   S         | selectively   | refocus       | expansion     |
   S         +---------------+---------------+---------------+
   .  WEAK   | SELECTIVITY / | HARVEST /     | HARVEST /     |
             | EARNINGS      | DIVEST        | DIVEST        |
   STRENGTH  | build on      | manage for    | exit; cut     |
             | strengths     | cash          | fixed cost    |
             +---------------+---------------+---------------+

   Three zones, read on the diagonal:
     top-left triangle      -> invest and grow
     the diagonal band      -> selectivity, manage for earnings
     bottom-right triangle  -> harvest or divest
```

**What the manager does with the output.** Rank investment by zone — and, because the axes are
composites, argue about the weights, which is the point. Circles on a GE grid are conventionally sized
by market size with a slice for the unit's share, so one diagram carries position, prize and the
firm's share of it.

### ADL Matrix

Stage of industry maturity against competitive position. Its contribution is putting the life cycle
on an axis, so a weak player in an embryonic industry is treated differently from one in an ageing
industry.

```board
                         STAGE OF INDUSTRY MATURITY
                  EMBRYONIC    GROWTH      MATURE      AGEING
                +-----------+-----------+-----------+-----------+
   DOMINANT     |     N     |     N     |     N     |    N/S    |
                +-----------+-----------+-----------+-----------+
   STRONG       |     N     |     N     |     N     |     S     |
   C            +-----------+-----------+-----------+-----------+
   O FAVOURABLE |    N/S    |     S     |     S     |    S/P    |
   M            +-----------+-----------+-----------+-----------+
   P TENABLE    |     S     |     S     |     P     |     W     |
   .            +-----------+-----------+-----------+-----------+
   POS.  WEAK   |     P     |     P     |     W     |     W     |
                +-----------+-----------+-----------+-----------+

   The four generic strategic thrusts:
     N = natural development   -- fund it fully; the position deserves it
     S = selective development -- back only the segments where it can win
     P = prove viability       -- limited, conditional investment; earn the right
     W = withdraw              -- divest or liquidate
```

Learn the **two axes with their four and five labels** and the **four thrusts**, plus the pattern:
natural development top-left, withdrawal bottom-right, freedom narrowing as maturity rises and
position weakens. The thrust, not the cell, is the answer.

> **Verify:** presentations of the ADL matrix differ — some editions add a sixth competitive position
> ("non-viable"), and the cell-by-cell allocation of thrusts is not identical everywhere. Confirm the
> version in your attempt's Study Material.

:::think Two units both hold a weak position — one in an embryonic industry, one in an ageing one. Why does ADL separate them and BCG does not?
Because ADL's horizontal axis is maturity, which distinguishes "too early to tell" from "too late to
fix". The embryonic unit is asked to **prove viability** — the industry has not settled and a
position is still winnable; the ageing one is told to **withdraw**, since neither industry nor
position will improve. BCG separates them only by market growth.
:::

:::ladder A mid-sized paint company sells to retail customers in three states. Volume growth has fallen to 2% as those states saturate; the firm has cash, a recognised brand and a strong dealer network. Recommend a growth strategy.
@weak: Ansoff's Matrix has four cells — market penetration, market development, product development
and diversification — of which penetration is least risky and diversification most risky. The company
should choose whichever suits it best.
@good: Applies the matrix to the firm. Penetration is nearly exhausted, since the three states are
saturating and growth has fallen to 2%. Market development means the existing paints in new states;
product development means new products for the existing dealers and customers; diversification would
be riskiest. Both middle options look feasible given the cash and the brand.
@great: The same, decided on the firm's own assets and closed with the risk. Penetration is ruled out
by the facts — 2% growth in saturating states. The transferable strengths are the **brand** and the
**dealer network**; a dealer network is state-specific and must be rebuilt in a new state, whereas new
products travel through the network the firm already controls, so product development exploits the
existing asset and market development recreates it. **Recommendation:** lead with **product
development** — adjacent lines such as waterproofing for the same buyers through the same dealers —
and phase market development into one contiguous state as a funded pilot rather than three at once.
Reject diversification: cash without capability in an unrelated business is the standard reason
conglomerate diversification destroys value. **Risk:** a wider range dilutes management attention and
a quality failure damages the brand carrying the whole portfolio, so each new line needs a stage-gate,
not an open commitment.
:::

:::incident The unit that pays for everything
@symptom: A listed group has three businesses. **Packaging** earns 70% of group profit and holds 34%
share, with its largest competitor at 30%, in a market growing 3% a year. **Speciality chemicals** has
6% share against a leader at 28%, in a market growing 22%, and has consumed cash for four years.
**Printing consumables** has 4% share against a leader at 26%, in a market shrinking 5% a year, and has
broken even for three years.
@signal: The board proposes to fund the chemicals expansion by cutting packaging's maintenance and
marketing budget, and to keep printing consumables because "it covers its own costs and the plant is
written down."
@ask: Place the three units on the BCG matrix. Is the board's funding proposal sound, and what would
you recommend for each unit?
@diagnosis: **Packaging** is a cash cow — relative share 34/30 exceeds one, so it is the market leader,
and growth of 3% is low. **Speciality chemicals** is a question mark — relative share 6/28 is well below
one, growth of 22% is high, and the cash absorption is what the cell predicts. **Printing consumables**
is a dog — relative share 4/26 is far below one and market growth is negative.

The board is half right. Funding a question mark out of a cash cow is the cash logic the matrix
prescribes, so the direction of the money is correct; the **source** is not. Cutting a cow's
maintenance and marketing does not release free cash — it converts the cow into a dog over a few
years, because share in a low-growth market is held by exactly that spending. A cow is harvested by
taking its surplus, not by starving the asset that generates it. The unexamined "keep" on printing
consumables is the second flaw: breaking even in a shrinking market still consumes attention and
working capital while terminal value falls, and the written-down plant is irrelevant to a decision
that turns on what the assets and customer list would realise now.

**Recommendation.** Hold and harvest packaging, funding chemicals from its surplus rather than its
cost base. On chemicals, set a share and margin milestone at which further investment is justified
and treat divestiture as the live alternative while the market is growing and buyers exist — "build
selectively or divest", since the group cannot fund a share war against a rival nearly five times its
size.
Divest or liquidate printing consumables. Finally, state what the matrix cannot see: whether the
units share customers, channels or technology, since a dog carrying a cow's customer relationship may
be worth keeping for a reason the grid does not capture.
:::

:::attempt Draw Ansoff's Matrix and the BCG Matrix from memory, label all eight cells, and state what a manager decides with each matrix's output.
**Ansoff** — products (existing, new) against markets (existing, new): **market penetration**,
**product development**, **market development**, **diversification**. Output: growth options ranked by
risk, rising away from penetration.

**BCG** — relative market share against market growth: **stars**, **question marks**, **cash cows**,
**dogs**. Output: cash allocated through build, hold, harvest and divest, plus a balance check between
cash generators and future earners.

The axis definitions are where marks go: Ansoff's axes are about **newness**, BCG's are **relative**
share and the **market's** growth. In a scenario, place each named unit in a cell, justify it from the
figures given, and only then recommend.
:::
