## Unit 1 — Overview of Cost and Management Accounting

:::scene Day one, costing desk
@env: A client's factory office, third week of April. Two years of files on the table and a
kettle nobody has descaled. Through the glass, the shop floor is running.
@cast: You, with a blank cost sheet. The Principal, who has ten minutes.
@cam: Over the blank sheet, the shop floor soft behind the glass.
@light: Fluorescent, flat. Nothing dramatic.
@beat: He puts a single invoice on the table and waits.
@a11y: The plainness of the setting is deliberate — nothing has gone wrong; this is ordinary
first-day work. The Principal's silence is a prompt, not disapproval.
:::

:::dialogue
@P: This factory made 40,000 units last month and sold them for ₹4,50,000. Did it make money?
@Y: Their profit and loss account says yes, ₹60,000.
@P: That tells you the factory made money. I asked whether the *units* did. Which of them, and
how much each?
@N: That gap is the entire reason cost accounting exists. Financial accounting answers "how did
the entity perform"; cost accounting answers "what did this thing cost, and why". Every
definitional question in this unit is really about that distinction.
:::

### What cost and management accounting is for

The syllabus objective is worth reading as a list of verbs: **establish** the cost of products
and services, **apply** it to determine prices, **understand** cost statements, and **apply**
information for cost ascertainment, planning, control and decision making.

Four disciplines get confused in exam answers, so fix them now:

| Discipline | Question it answers | Audience | Governed by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Financial Accounting | How did the entity perform and what does it own and owe? | External — shareholders, lenders, regulators | Statute and accounting standards |
| Cost Accounting | What did this product, service, job or process cost? | Internal management | Management's own needs |
| Management Accounting | What should we do? | Internal management | Nothing mandatory |
| Financial Management | Where do funds come from and where should they go? | Internal, and the capital market | Judgement plus market discipline |

**Users of cost and management accounting information** are internal: management at every level,
plus employees, and in a regulated or cost-audited environment, outside parties. The
**functions of management accounting** run from collecting and classifying data through
planning, control, performance measurement and decision support.

The **cost accounting department** does not sit alone. It draws from stores, personnel and
payroll, production, and financial accounting, and it feeds back to all of them. Exam questions
on "relation with other departments" want that two-way flow, not a list.

**Installation of a costing system** is examined as a set of considerations rather than a
procedure: the objective and the entity's own needs, the nature of the product and process,
the organisational structure, the level of detail worth its cost, and the cooperation of the
people who will feed it. The recurring theme is that the system must be **worth more than it
costs to run** and must fit the business it is installed in.

:::key Cost accounting is not a smaller financial accounting. It has a different question, a different audience, and no statutory format to hide behind.
Answers that describe cost accounting as "accounting for costs in more detail" lose the mark.
The distinction the examiner wants is the *purpose*: ascertainment, control and decision making
for internal management, against a chosen cost object.
:::

### Cost terms you must be exact about

- **Cost** — the amount of expenditure incurred on, or attributable to, a specified thing.
- **Cost object** — the thing being costed: a product, service, job, process, batch, customer,
  department.
- **Cost centre** — a location, person, or item of equipment for which cost is ascertained and
  used for control. Subdivided into **personal and impersonal**, and into **production and
  service** cost centres.
- **Cost unit** — the unit of quantity in which cost is expressed. Get the unit right and the
  answer follows: per tonne-kilometre for transport, per patient-day for a hospital, per
  kilowatt-hour for power, per student-year for education.
- **Cost driver** — the factor that causes a change in the cost of an activity. This is the term
  Activity Based Costing is built on, later in Unit 2.

**Cost control versus cost reduction** is asked almost every attempt, and the contrast is clean:

| | Cost control | Cost reduction |
|---|---|---|
| Aim | Keep cost within a predetermined standard or budget | Reduce cost permanently below the present level |
| Assumes | The standard is acceptable | No standard is sacred |
| Ends when | The target is met | Never; it is continuous |
| Nature | Preventive and comparative | Corrective and innovative |
| Effect on quality/utility | Maintains it | Must not sacrifice it |

That last row is the mark most people drop: **cost reduction must be real and permanent, and
must not reduce the utility or quality of the product.** A saving obtained by cheapening the
product is not cost reduction.

### Elements of cost, and how cost behaves

The three **elements** are material, labour (employee cost) and expenses, and each splits into
**direct** and **indirect**. The three indirect elements together are **overheads**.

```text
                       Material        Labour          Expenses
   Direct           Direct material  Direct wages   Direct expenses   -> PRIME COST
   Indirect         Indirect mat.    Indirect lab.  Indirect exp.     -> OVERHEADS
```

Everything in Paper 4 hangs off that grid. Prime cost is the total of the direct row; overheads
are the total of the indirect row, then classified functionally as factory, administration,
selling, distribution, and research and development.

**Behavioural classification** is the other axis, and Unit 4's marginal costing depends on it:

- **Fixed cost** — unchanged in total over a period within a relevant range, so it falls per unit
  as volume rises. Factory rent.
- **Variable cost** — changes in total in direct proportion to volume, so it is constant per
  unit. Direct material.
- **Semi-variable cost** — has both a fixed and a variable component. A telephone line rental
  plus call charges; supervisory salary plus overtime.
- **Step cost** — fixed over a range, then jumps to a new level. One supervisor per shift: add a
  shift and the cost steps up, it does not slope.

Separating the fixed and variable halves of a semi-variable cost is examined numerically, usually
by the **high-low method**: take the highest and lowest activity levels, divide the change in
cost by the change in volume to get the variable rate per unit, then substitute back to get the
fixed portion.

:::trap Calling a step cost a variable cost because it rises with volume
A variable cost rises *proportionately* and is constant per unit. A step cost is fixed within
each range and jumps between ranges, so it is neither constant in total nor constant per unit.
The exam tests this with supervision, inspection and delivery-vehicle costs. If the cost only
changes when you cross a threshold, it steps.
:::

**Methods of costing** answer "what kind of thing are we costing" — unit, job, batch, contract,
process, operation, service — and are the whole of Unit 3. **Techniques of costing** answer "how
are we using the cost" — standard costing, marginal costing, absorption costing, budgetary
control — and are the whole of Unit 4. Do not mix the two words in an answer; the syllabus keeps
them separate and so does the marking.

**Digital costing** is explicitly in the syllabus. Treat it as the use of integrated digital
systems — ERP, real-time data capture, analytics, and in Unit 2 the Government e-Marketplace
(GeM) and digital inventory control — to collect and analyse cost data continuously rather than
periodically. The benefits examined are accuracy, timeliness, traceability and lower cost of
collection; the concerns are data quality, implementation cost and control over access.

### The cost sheet

A cost sheet is a statement showing the build-up from material to sales for a cost object and
period. Learn the order of the totals, because the order *is* the marks.

```text
COST SHEET for the period ended ...                        ₹            ₹
  Opening stock of raw material                        20,000
  Add: Purchases of raw material                    1,80,000
  Add: Carriage inward / freight on purchases           8,000
  Less: Purchase returns                              (5,000)
  Less: Closing stock of raw material                (25,000)
Raw material consumed                                             1,78,000
Direct wages                                                        90,000
Direct expenses (chargeable expenses)                               12,000
PRIME COST                                                        2,80,000
  Add: Factory / works overheads                       60,000
  Add: Opening work-in-progress                        15,000
  Less: Closing work-in-progress                     (18,000)
  Less: Sale of scrap                                 (3,000)
WORKS / FACTORY COST                                              3,34,000
  Add: Quality control and R&D cost, and administration
       overheads relating to production                 6,000
COST OF PRODUCTION                                                3,40,000
  Add: Opening stock of finished goods                 22,000
  Less: Closing stock of finished goods               (30,000)
COST OF GOODS SOLD                                                3,32,000
  Add: Administration overheads (general)              25,000
  Add: Selling and distribution overheads              33,000
COST OF SALES                                                     3,90,000
Profit                                                              60,000
SALES                                                             4,50,000
```

Four things about that statement are where marks are actually won:

1. **Freight inward is part of material cost; freight outward is distribution overhead.** The
   words are nearly identical and the treatments are opposite.
2. **Work-in-progress is adjusted at works cost stage**, not at prime cost, because WIP has
   already absorbed factory overheads.
3. **Finished goods stock is adjusted after cost of production**, because that is the value at
   which finished goods are carried.
4. **Sale of scrap is deducted from factory overheads or works cost**, not treated as income.

> **Verify:** the treatment of administration overheads — how much is absorbed into cost of
> production versus shown after cost of goods sold — is a convention that ICAI states in the
> Study Material. Confirm the layout your attempt's material uses before the exam.

For the **service sector** the same logic applies with a different cost unit and no material or
WIP to speak of. A service cost statement builds up operating cost by element, then divides by
the composite cost unit. The examinable skill is choosing that unit: a **tonne-kilometre** for
goods transport, a **passenger-kilometre** for passenger transport, a **bed-day** or
**patient-day** for a hospital, a **room-day** for a hotel, a **kilowatt-hour** for power.

:::estimate Build the cost sheet and find the profit percentages
@given: Opening raw material ₹20,000; purchases ₹1,80,000; carriage inward ₹8,000; purchase
returns ₹5,000; closing raw material ₹25,000. Direct wages ₹90,000; direct expenses ₹12,000.
Factory overheads ₹60,000; opening WIP ₹15,000; closing WIP ₹18,000; sale of scrap ₹3,000.
Quality control cost ₹6,000. Opening finished goods ₹22,000; closing finished goods ₹30,000.
General administration overheads ₹25,000; selling and distribution overheads ₹33,000. Sales
₹4,50,000.
@ask: Compute raw material consumed, prime cost, works cost, cost of production, cost of goods
sold, cost of sales and profit. Then express profit both as a percentage of sales and as a
percentage of cost, and say which one the question is more likely to want.
@work: Raw material consumed = 20,000 + 1,80,000 + 8,000 − 5,000 − 25,000 = **₹1,78,000**.

Prime cost = 1,78,000 + 90,000 + 12,000 = **₹2,80,000**.

Works cost = 2,80,000 + 60,000 + 15,000 − 18,000 − 3,000 = **₹3,34,000**.

Cost of production = 3,34,000 + 6,000 = **₹3,40,000**.

Cost of goods sold = 3,40,000 + 22,000 − 30,000 = **₹3,32,000**.

Cost of sales = 3,32,000 + 25,000 + 33,000 = **₹3,90,000**.

Profit = 4,50,000 − 3,90,000 = **₹60,000**.

As a percentage of sales: 60,000 / 4,50,000 = **13.33%**.
As a percentage of cost: 60,000 / 3,90,000 = **15.38%**.
@sanity: Two checks. First, the two percentages must differ and the cost-based one must be the
larger, because the denominator is smaller — if yours came out equal or the other way round,
you have divided by the wrong figure. Second, read the question's wording: "profit on cost" and
"profit on sales" are different questions, and a question that gives you a margin and asks for
sales is testing exactly this. When the question says the entity earns a profit of 20% **on
cost**, sales are 120% of cost; when it says 20% **on sales**, cost is 80% of sales.
:::

:::attempt Without looking above, write the cost sheet skeleton from opening raw material down to sales, marking at which line WIP is adjusted and at which line finished goods stock is adjusted.
A full-mark skeleton has the six named totals in this order — **raw material consumed, prime
cost, works or factory cost, cost of production, cost of goods sold, cost of sales** — with
sales reached by adding profit.

The two adjustment points are the whole test:

- **Work-in-progress is adjusted between prime cost and works cost.** Opening WIP added,
  closing WIP deducted. It sits there because WIP already carries factory overheads, so
  adjusting it before overheads are added would understate it.
- **Finished goods stock is adjusted between cost of production and cost of goods sold.**
  Opening added, closing deducted. It sits there because finished goods are valued at cost of
  production.

If you wrote the two adjustments in the wrong places, the totals still add up and every
subsequent figure is wrong — which is exactly why examiners like this question. Also check that
you deducted sale of scrap rather than adding it as income, and that carriage inward went into
material rather than into distribution.
:::

:::transfer Cost behaviour
@to: Unit 4's marginal costing and CVP analysis is built entirely on splitting cost into fixed
and variable, so the high-low method you learn here is a prerequisite there rather than a
separate topic.
@stops: Marginal costing treats the fixed/variable split as a decision tool and deliberately
excludes fixed cost from product cost. The cost sheet in this unit is an **absorption** costing
statement and includes fixed factory overheads in cost of production. The same classification
serves two methods that give different product costs and different profits — reconciling them
is itself an examinable topic.
:::
