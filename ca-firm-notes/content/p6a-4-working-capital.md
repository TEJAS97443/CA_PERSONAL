## Unit 4 — Management of Working Capital

:::dialogue
@P: The client is profitable, has a full order book, and could not pay salaries on the 7th. How?
@Y: Their debtors are slow.
@P: Debtors are slow, stock is high and the suppliers have stopped waiting. Put a number on it.
@N: The number is the cash conversion cycle. This unit is examined as arithmetic, not opinion.
:::

**Gross working capital** is total current assets; **net working capital** is current assets less
current liabilities. Split it another way and the financing question appears: **permanent (core)
working capital** is the minimum level always tied up, and **temporary (fluctuating) working
capital** rises and falls with the season or the order book.

**Liquidity against profitability** is the trade-off the unit turns on. Holding more current assets, and
financing them long term, makes the firm safe and less profitable: idle cash earns little, generous
credit buys sales at a cost, large stocks avoid stock-outs and tie up funds. Holding less does the
opposite. Three financing strategies name the positions:

| Strategy | How it finances | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Conservative | Long-term funds cover permanent needs and part of the fluctuating need | Low risk, low return; surplus funds in slack season |
| Aggressive | Short-term funds cover the fluctuating need and part of the permanent need | High return, high refinancing and interest-rate risk |
| Moderate (matching or hedging) | Long-term funds match permanent needs, short-term funds match fluctuating needs | Maturity of the finance matches the life of the asset |

**Primary and secondary sources of liquidity** must be kept apart. **Primary sources** are the
ordinary, low-cost, day-to-day ones: cash and near-cash balances, cash generated by trading, trade
credit taken, and undrawn bank limits. **Secondary sources** are reached for when the primary ones are
exhausted: renegotiating or rescheduling debt, factoring or securitising receivables, liquidating
inventory at a discount, selling long-term assets, and ultimately insolvency proceedings. The
distinction is not merely descriptive — **using a secondary source changes the firm's operations or
capital structure and signals distress**; using a primary source does not.

### The operating cycle and the cash conversion cycle

:::formula The operating cycle, the cash conversion cycle and the funds they need
@formula: Raw material period  = (Average raw material stock / Annual RM consumption) x 365
Work-in-progress period = (Average WIP / Annual cost of production) x 365
Finished goods period   = (Average finished goods / Annual cost of goods sold) x 365
Receivables period      = (Average debtors / Annual credit sales) x 365
Payables period         = (Average creditors / Annual credit purchases) x 365

Gross operating cycle = RM period + WIP period + FG period + Receivables period
Net operating cycle (cash conversion cycle) = Gross operating cycle - Payables period
Number of operating cycles a year = 365 / Net operating cycle
Working capital required = Total operating cost / Number of operating cycles a year
@terms: Each numerator is a stock (a balance), each denominator a flow (a year's turnover). Use averages
where opening and closing figures are given, closing balances otherwise.
@watch: Every ratio must pair a stock with **its own** flow — finished goods against cost of goods sold,
not sales; debtors against **credit** sales; creditors against **credit purchases**, not consumption.
Pairing debtors with cost of sales, or stock with sales, loses the whole answer.
:::

> **Verify:** conventions differ. Many texts define the operating cycle as inventory period plus
> receivables period and reserve "cash conversion cycle" for that figure minus payables — the same
> arithmetic under different names, as above. Some questions use 360 days rather than 365. Follow the
> question's basis, state it, and check the Study Material for your attempt.

:::attempt Average raw material ₹40,000 against annual consumption ₹3,00,000; average WIP ₹30,000 against cost of production ₹4,50,000; average finished goods ₹50,000 against cost of goods sold ₹5,00,000; average debtors ₹80,000 against credit sales ₹6,00,000; average creditors ₹45,000 against credit purchases ₹3,20,000. Total operating cost ₹5,50,000. Compute the operating cycle, the cash conversion cycle and the working capital requirement.
Take a 365-day year and pair each stock with its own flow.

```text
  Raw material period   (40,000 / 3,00,000) x 365   =   48.67 days
  Work-in-progress      (30,000 / 4,50,000) x 365   =   24.33 days
  Finished goods        (50,000 / 5,00,000) x 365   =   36.50 days
  Receivables           (80,000 / 6,00,000) x 365   =   48.67 days
                                                       -----------
  GROSS OPERATING CYCLE                                158.17 days
  Less: Payables period (45,000 / 3,20,000) x 365   =   51.33 days
                                                       -----------
  NET OPERATING CYCLE (CASH CONVERSION CYCLE)          106.84 days
```

The net cycle is 106.8385 days unrounded, so operating cycles a year = 365 / 106.8385 =
**3.4164 cycles**.

Working capital required = 5,50,000 / 3.4164 = **₹1,60,990**, call it ₹1.61 lakh.

Cross-check the other way, since either may be asked: 5,50,000 x 106.8385/365 = **₹1,60,990**. The
two routes are algebraically the same statement, so if they disagree you have made an arithmetic
slip, not a methodological choice.

**Carry the unrounded cycle.** Rounding the cycle count to 3.42 gives ₹1,60,819 — about **₹171
out**. Rounding the *days* to 106.84 first gives ₹1,60,992, which is ₹2 out. Neither error is large
enough to matter commercially and both are large enough to lose a tick, so keep full precision in
the working and round only the final answer. If you must show a rounded cycle figure, show it and
then say you divided by the unrounded one.

The sentence that earns the interpretation mark: cash is locked up for roughly 107 days, so a
**shorter** cycle releases cash with no new finance — collect faster, hold less stock, pay later — and
each day removed cuts the funds needed by about ₹1,507 (5,50,000/365).
:::

### Assessment of the working capital requirement

Three approaches are examined. The **estimation of components** method builds current assets and
liabilities up line by line, valuing stock and debtors at cost and adding a stated safety margin —
noting that debtors may be taken at **cost** or at **selling price** depending on the question, and
that cash cost excludes depreciation. The **percentage of sales** method applies a historical ratio of
working capital to sales: quick and crude. The **operating cycle** method is the one just worked.
Whichever is used, state whether you worked on a **total cost** or a **cash cost** basis.

### Receivables, payables and credit management

**Credit granting** assesses the applicant — character, capacity, capital, collateral, conditions —
then sets the credit period, any early-payment discount and the credit limit. **Monitoring** uses the
average collection period, an **ageing schedule** of receivables by days outstanding, and receivables
as a proportion of sales. **Collection** escalates from statements and reminders to telephone
follow-up, withdrawal of credit, an agency, and legal action. The governing rule: **relax credit only
while the incremental contribution exceeds the incremental cost of bad debts, discounts,
administration and the funds tied up.**

:::formula The cost of forgoing a cash discount
@formula: Cost (% per annum) = [ d / (100 - d) ] x [ 365 / (Credit period - Discount period) ] x 100
@terms: d = discount percentage offered for early payment.
@watch: This is the implicit annual interest in *not* taking a discount, usually far above any borrowing
rate — on 2/10, net 45 it is (2/98) x (365/35) = **21.28%** a year, so a firm that can borrow more
cheaply should always take the discount. Read the same formula the other way to price the discount you
offer your own customers.
:::

**Managing payables** is the mirror image: trade credit is free while no discount is forgone, so take
the full period offered — but stretching beyond it costs goodwill, invites supply interruption and
eventually cash-on-delivery terms. Compare the cost of forgoing the discount with the firm's own cost
of short-term finance and choose the cheaper.

**Factoring and forfaiting** are both ways of turning receivables into cash, and the comparison is
examinable almost every attempt:

| | Factoring | Forfaiting |
|---|---|---|
| Receivables covered | Ordinary trade receivables, domestic or export | Export receivables only |
| Instrument | Invoices; no negotiable instrument needed | Bills of exchange or promissory notes, usually bank-guaranteed |
| Tenor | Short-term | Medium to long-term |
| Recourse | With or without recourse | Always without recourse |
| Amount advanced | Part of invoice value, the balance on collection | Effectively the whole value, discounted upfront |
| Services | Finance plus sales ledger administration, collection and credit protection | Financing only |

:::estimate Should the credit period be extended from 30 days to 60?
@given: Present sales ₹10,00,000, 30-day collection period, bad debts 1% of sales. Variable cost 70% of
sales; fixed cost ₹1,50,000. Extending credit to 60 days raises sales to ₹12,00,000 with bad debts of
2%; fixed cost unchanged. Required return on funds in receivables 20% a year. Use 365 days and value
debtors at total cost.
@ask: Evaluate the proposal, showing incremental contribution, incremental bad debts and the
opportunity cost of the extra investment in receivables.
@work:
```text
                                        Present (30 d)    Proposed (60 d)   Incremental
  Sales                                    10,00,000         12,00,000
  Less: Variable cost at 70% of sales        7,00,000          8,40,000
                                           ---------         ---------
  CONTRIBUTION                              3,00,000          3,60,000        60,000

  Cost build-up for valuing debtors:
    Variable cost at 70% of sales            7,00,000          8,40,000
    Add: Fixed cost                          1,50,000          1,50,000
                                           ---------         ---------
    TOTAL COST                               8,50,000          9,90,000
  Debtors at total cost x days/365             69,863          1,62,740        92,877
  Bad debts (1% / 2% of sales)                 10,000            24,000        14,000
```
Opportunity cost of the extra investment = 92,877 x 20% = **₹18,575**.

Net benefit = incremental contribution 60,000 − incremental bad debts 14,000 − opportunity cost
18,575 = **₹27,425**. The proposal is worth accepting.
@sanity: Check each term's direction before trusting the total. Fixed cost appears in both columns and
cancels out of the *incremental* contribution, which is why 30% of the ₹2,00,000 extra sales (₹60,000)
is the right figure. Bad debts rise on both counts — bigger base, worse rate. And the investment in
debtors roughly doubles because both the cost base and the period rise; a small incremental investment
means you applied 30/365 rather than 60/365 to the proposed column.
:::

:::trap Valuing debtors at sales value when the question says cost
Money is tied up in debtors only to the extent of the **cost** incurred — the profit element was never
spent, so no funds are locked in it. ICAI questions therefore say "total cost" or "cash cost", and the
opportunity cost is sensitive to which: debtors at sales value here would be 12,00,000 x 60/365 =
₹1,97,260 instead of ₹1,62,740. State your basis in one line, and remember cash cost excludes
depreciation.
:::

### Cash and treasury management

Three motives for holding cash: **transaction** (routine payments), **precautionary** (a buffer against
uncertainty) and **speculative** (to exploit an opportunity). The control tool is the **cash budget**,
projecting receipts and payments period by period and exposing deficits before they arrive. Cash is
then managed by **speeding collections** (prompt billing, decentralised collection, concentration
banking, lock boxes) and **controlling disbursements** (centralised payment, use of the cheque float,
paying on the last permissible day), with genuine surpluses invested short term.

**Treasury management** owns all of this: liquidity and cash-flow management, banking relationships and
the negotiation of facilities, arranging short-term funding, investing surpluses, monitoring the firm's
financial exposures and reporting on them. Its aim is the right money in the right place at the right
time — no idle surplus, no missed payment.

### Banking norms of working capital finance

A bank does not finance the whole working capital gap. The borrower contributes a **margin** from its
own long-term funds and the bank funds the balance. Against the security charged — usually stocks and
book debts — the bank computes a **drawing power**: eligible security value, less the stipulated
margin, less unpaid creditors for those stocks. Drawings are allowed up to the **lower** of drawing
power and the sanctioned limit, which is why a borrower with an adequate limit can still be unable to
draw. The underlying idea is a **maximum permissible bank finance** — a ceiling derived from the
working capital gap after the borrower's own contribution — policed by periodic stock and book-debt
statements and an annual review.

> **Verify:** the specific margin percentages, the method by which permissible bank finance is
> computed, and the eligibility rules for security are matters of banking regulation and lender
> policy, and they change. This note deliberately states no percentage. Confirm the treatment
> expected in the Study Material and Study Guidelines for your attempt.

:::key A shorter cash conversion cycle is free finance. Every day removed releases funds permanently and costs no interest, which is why the cycle is the first thing to compute and the last thing to report.
On the figures above one day is about ₹1,507, so cutting the 48.67-day receivables period to 40 days
releases roughly ₹13,000 of the ₹1,60,992 requirement with no new borrowing. Benefit against the cost
of achieving it is the form every working capital answer should take.
:::

:::think Current ratio is 2.5 and rising, and the cash conversion cycle is lengthening. Is liquidity improving?
No — it is deteriorating while the ratio flatters it. A current ratio rising on slower stock and
slower-paying debtors means more funds locked up for longer, not more cash available. The ratio is a
snapshot of balances; the cycle measures how fast they turn into cash. Read them together.
:::

  Each period = (Average balance / its own annual flow) x 365

**Decision rules, and where they fight each other.**

- Accept a project if **NPV > 0**, **IRR > cost of capital**, **PI > 1**, payback is within the
  cut-off, or ARR beats the target. For one conventional project the discounted measures agree.
- On **ranking**, prefer **NPV** — it maximises absolute wealth. Prefer **PI** only when capital is
  rationed and projects are divisible; with indivisible projects, test feasible combinations, because
  PI ranking can leave funds idle and money on the table.
- Prefer **MIRR to IRR** where the reinvestment assumption matters, and abandon IRR altogether when
  cash flows change sign more than once, because **multiple IRRs** exist.
- Never rank on **payback** (ignores everything after the cut-off and, undiscounted, the time value
  of money) or **ARR** (uses profit, not cash, and ignores timing) — quote them as supplementary.
- In financing, accept the geared plan only **above the indifference EBIT**; in dividends, follow
  Walter and Gordon's single rule — retain when r > Ke, distribute when r < Ke.

**Ratios and what each one diagnoses.** Liquidity — current, quick, cash: can short-term obligations
be met. Solvency — debt–equity, proprietary, capital gearing, interest coverage, DSCR: how much risk
sits with lenders and whether they can be serviced. Activity — inventory, receivables, payables,
fixed asset, working capital turnover: how hard the assets are worked and how long cash is locked up.
Profitability — gross, operating and net profit ratios, ROCE, ROE, return on assets: whether the
trading and the capital both pay. Market — EPS, price–earnings, earnings and dividend yield, payout:
what the market will pay for the earnings. Remember that ROCE is the referee between an efficiency
story and a gearing story, and that every ratio is a symptom, not a cause.

**Traps that cost marks.** Multiplying Ke or Kp by (1 − t). Adding leverages instead of multiplying
them. Using EBIT/PBT for DFL when preference shares exist. Giving retained earnings a separate market
value. Comparing raw present values of assets with unequal lives instead of EAC. Valuing debtors at
sales value when the question says cost. Pairing a stock with the wrong flow in a cycle computation.
Treating a bonus issue or a stock split as a gain to shareholders.
