### Origin, and what an audit is

The word is from the Latin *audire*, to hear: accounts were once read aloud to an official who
heard and passed them. The listening became a documented examination; the logic did not change.

An **audit** is the independent examination of financial information of any entity, whether
profit-oriented or not and irrespective of its size or legal form, carried out with a view to
expressing an opinion on that information. Three words carry it: *independent*, *examination*,
*opinion*. Management prepares the financial statements; the auditor opines on them.

An **external audit engagement** is one where the auditor is independent of the entity, appointed
by or on behalf of the owners rather than by management, and reports to those owners. That
separates it from internal audit, a function inside the entity reporting to management and those
charged with governance.

### Need for audit, and its benefits

| Benefit | Who it serves |
|---|---|
| Independent credibility for the financial statements | Shareholders, lenders, regulators, revenue authorities |
| A moral check; material error and fraud are likelier to be found | Owners not in management |
| Basis for settling accounts on retirement, death or admission of a partner | Firms |
| Evidence for valuation, claims, disputes, finance applications | Any entity |

### The objective, in the Standard's own words (SA 200)

SA 200 states the overall objectives of the independent auditor as: to obtain **reasonable
assurance** about whether the financial statements as a whole are free from **material
misstatement**, whether due to **fraud or error**, thereby enabling the auditor to express an
opinion on whether the financial statements are prepared, **in all material respects**, in
accordance with an applicable financial reporting framework; and to report on the financial
statements, and communicate as required by the Standards on Auditing, in accordance with the
auditor's findings.

Reasonable assurance is a **high** level of assurance, obtained when the auditor has obtained
**sufficient appropriate audit evidence** to reduce **audit risk** to an acceptably low level.
It is not absolute assurance, and no synonym for it earns the mark.

SA 200 also carries the ideas the rest of the paper is built on: compliance with relevant ethical
requirements; **professional skepticism**, a questioning mind alert to conditions that may indicate
misstatement and a critical assessment of audit evidence; **professional judgement**; and conduct of
the audit in accordance with all SAs relevant to it. Where an objective in a relevant SA cannot be
achieved, the auditor evaluates whether that requires modification of the opinion or withdrawal.

The audit rests on a **premise**: that management, and where appropriate those charged with
governance, are responsible for preparing the financial statements and for internal control, and
will give the auditor the information and access the audit needs.

:::key An audit gives reasonable assurance on whether the financial statements as a whole are free from material misstatement — nothing wider, nothing narrower.
It is not a certificate of correctness, not assurance about future viability, and not an opinion on
the efficiency with which management has conducted the entity's affairs. State all three and there is
little left to deduct.
:::

### Scope of audit

Scope is the audit procedures the auditor judges necessary in the circumstances to achieve the
objective, determined by the terms of the engagement, relevant legislation, and the pronouncements of
the Institute. Two consequences are examined. Where law fixes the scope of a statutory audit, an
agreement with the client **cannot cut it down**, and equally the auditor does not take on work
outside that scope unless agreed. And the audit covers the financial statements **as a whole**,
through selective testing, so the auditor looks for misstatements material individually or in the
aggregate rather than for every misstatement.

### Inherent limitations of an audit

The most reliably examined topic in the unit. The auditor cannot obtain absolute assurance, and the
reason is structural, not a failure of effort.

1. **The nature of financial reporting.** Preparation involves management judgement and
   estimates about which a range of reasonable interpretations exists; some items carry
   irreducible estimation uncertainty.
2. **The nature of audit procedures.** Management may not provide complete information,
   intentionally or otherwise. Fraud may involve sophisticated, carefully organised schemes,
   collusion and forgery. An audit is not an official investigation into alleged wrongdoing and
   the auditor has no legal powers of search. Audit evidence is therefore ordinarily
   **persuasive rather than conclusive**.
3. **Timeliness, and the balance of benefit against cost.** Financial information loses value
   if it is late, which limits the time and cost that can be justified.
4. **Other matters.** Fraud involving senior management or collusion; the existence and
   completeness of related party relationships and transactions; non-compliance with laws and
   regulations; and future events or conditions bearing on the entity's ability to continue as a
   going concern.

Add one supporting proposition: internal control has its own inherent limitations, so the
auditor's reliance on it is never complete.

:::trap Writing that the auditor "detects all frauds and errors"
Detection is not the objective; reasonable assurance that the financial statements as a whole are
free from material misstatement, whether caused by fraud or error, is. Immaterial fraud, and fraud
concealed by senior-level collusion, may not be found — saying so earns a mark rather than losing
one.
:::

### Qualities of an auditor

Technical competence in accounting, auditing and law is assumed. Examined beyond it: integrity,
objectivity and independence; professional skepticism; professional judgement; knowledge of the
client's business; tact with firmness; clear writing; methodical documentation; confidentiality. The
fundamental principles and the threats to independence belong to the ethics unit of this desk.

### Relationship of auditing with other disciplines

Auditing borrows from everything and is subordinate to none. **Accountancy** — know how the number
was made before judging it; auditing begins where accounting ends. **Law** — company law, contract,
tax and sector regulation fix what the statements must contain and what must be reported.
**Economics** — industry conditions and the concepts of value and income. **Behavioural science** —
an audit is conducted on people, and judging what you are told is behavioural work. **Statistics and
mathematics** — sampling and analytical procedures. **Information technology** — electronic records
change both the controls and the approach. **Financial management and production** — ratios, funding
structure and the processes generating transactions.

:::think Why does the opinion say "in all material respects" rather than "in every respect"?
Because materiality sits inside the objective. Only misstatements that could influence users'
economic decisions change the opinion, so promising every respect would promise work no audit
performs.
:::
