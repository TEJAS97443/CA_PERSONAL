## Unit 3 — Strategic Analysis: Internal Environment

Internal analysis asks what the firm has, whom it must satisfy, and what it can do better than anyone
else. It ends by joining Unit 2's external findings to the internal ones in a SWOT, then converting
that into a competitive position.

### Understanding key stakeholders — Mendelow's model

A **stakeholder** is any party with an interest in what the organisation does: shareholders, lenders,
employees, customers, suppliers, government and regulators, communities. Their expectations conflict,
so the firm must know whose bind. Mendelow's model maps them on two dimensions — **power** (ability to
influence the strategy) and **interest** (how much they care about it).

```board
                        LEVEL OF INTEREST
                    LOW                    HIGH
              +----------------------+----------------------+
              |                      |                      |
        LOW   |   A                  |   B                  |
              |   MINIMAL EFFORT     |   KEEP INFORMED      |
   P          |   monitor only       |   explain, consult;  |
   O          |                      |   they lobby, they   |
   W          |                      |   cannot compel      |
   E          +----------------------+----------------------+
   R          |                      |                      |
              |   C                  |   D                  |
        HIGH  |   KEEP SATISFIED     |   KEY PLAYERS        |
              |   do not provoke;    |   involve in the     |
              |   they can act if    |   decision; strategy |
              |   roused             |   must be acceptable |
              |                      |   to them            |
              +----------------------+----------------------+
```

- **A — low power, low interest:** minimal effort; monitor in case they move.
- **B — low power, high interest:** keep informed. They cannot stop the strategy but they can make
  noise, and an informed supporter is useful — a community group, a small shareholder body.
- **C — high power, low interest:** keep satisfied. Dangerous precisely because they are currently
  indifferent: one decision touching their interest moves them to D. A large lender is the standard
  case.
- **D — high power, high interest:** key players. The strategy must be acceptable to them, so they
  are involved before it is settled, not informed after.

**What the manager does with the output.** Choose an involvement strategy per quadrant and test any
proposed strategy for **acceptability** to the key players before committing. Positions are not
permanent — a decision can raise a stakeholder's interest, and management can move a stakeholder
deliberately by sharing information or granting representation.

:::trap Mapping stakeholders by how important they feel rather than by power and interest
Employees are always "very important", so they land in the key-player box by default. Power is the
ability to influence or block the strategy; interest is how much this decision affects them.
Unorganised employees facing a plant closure have high interest and low power — "keep informed" is
the answer, and saying so earns the mark. The axes are power and interest, not power and *influence*.
:::

### Strategic drivers

Five drivers determine where a strategy can go. Treat each as a question about the firm's position:

- **Industry and markets** — which industries and segments is the firm in, how attractive are they,
  and are they growing or consolidating?
- **Customers** — which groups does it serve, how concentrated and loyal are they, and how
  profitable is each?
- **Channels** — how does it reach them, who controls the channel, what does the channel cost, and
  can the firm reach the customer directly?
- **Products and services** — what is the range, where is each item in its life cycle, and how
  differentiated is it?
- **Competitive advantage** — what does the firm do better than rivals, and will that hold?

Their use is diagnostic: changing one driver silently changes the others. Moving to direct online
distribution changes the channel, and with it the customer relationship, the viable product range
and the basis of advantage.

### Resources and capabilities

Resources are what the firm **has**; capabilities are what it can **do** with them.

| Layer | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Resources | Stock of assets, tangible and intangible | Plant, cash, patents, brand, licences, data, people |
| Competences | Activities the firm performs reliably | Running a plant efficiently; settling a claim in a day |
| Core competences | The few competences central to the strategy that distinguish the firm | An engineering skill rivals cannot match; unreplicable distribution reach |

Resources are classified as **tangible** (physical, financial), **intangible** (brand, reputation,
intellectual property, data, culture) and **human** (skills, experience, relationships). The
intangible ones are usually the durable source of advantage, being hardest to buy.

A resource or capability produces a **sustainable** advantage only if it is **valuable** to
customers, **rare** among competitors, **hard to imitate** and **hard to substitute** — and the firm
is organised to exploit it. A superb capability the firm is not structured to use is a wasted asset,
which is the Unit 5 problem.

:::think A firm's advantage is that it owns the only cold-storage network in a region. Is that sustainable?
Only until someone builds another one. It is valuable and currently rare, but imitability decides
sustainability — if a rival can fund and construct the same network, the advantage lasts as long
as the construction period. A capability built on accumulated skill, relationships or data lasts
longer than one built on an asset that money can buy.
:::

### Combining external and internal analysis — SWOT

```board
                       HELPFUL                 HARMFUL
                to the objective          to the objective
            +------------------------+------------------------+
   INTERNAL |  S  STRENGTHS          |  W  WEAKNESSES         |
   origin   |  what we have and do   |  what we lack or do    |
   (Unit 3) |  better than rivals    |  worse than rivals     |
            +------------------------+------------------------+
   EXTERNAL |  O  OPPORTUNITIES      |  T  THREATS            |
   origin   |  favourable conditions |  adverse conditions    |
   (Unit 2) |  we could exploit      |  we must defend against|
            +------------------------+------------------------+

   Generating strategy from the grid — pair the cells:
     S + O  use strengths to seize opportunities   (the aggressive option)
     S + T  use strengths to defend against threats
     W + O  fix or fund a weakness to reach an opportunity
     W + T  the survival quadrant: retrench, exit or partner
```

Three disciplines separate a SWOT that scores from a list that does not. **Strengths and weaknesses
are internal and comparative** — measured against competitors, not in the abstract; a 20% margin is a
strength only if rivals earn less. **Opportunities and threats are external and outside the firm's
control**; "we should launch a new product" is a strategy, not an opportunity. And the same fact can
appear twice: reliance on one large customer is a strength in volume and a weakness in dependence.

**What the manager does with the output.** Pair the cells to generate candidate strategies, then
evaluate them with the Unit 4 tools. A SWOT that stops at four lists earns half the marks.

:::key A SWOT is a bridge, not a list. Its output is the pairs — SO, ST, WO, WT — and each pair is a candidate strategy.
In a scenario the four lists earn the first half of the marks; the strategies drawn from pairing them,
and the recommendation, earn the second. Tie every item to a fact in the question.
:::

### Gaining competitive advantage — Michael Porter's generic strategies

Porter argues advantage has only two sources — being the **lowest cost** producer or being
**differentiated** — pursued either across a **broad market** or in a **narrow segment**. Two
dimensions, four boxes.

```board
                          SOURCE OF ADVANTAGE
                   LOWER COST          DIFFERENTIATION
             +---------------------+---------------------+
   BROAD     |  COST LEADERSHIP    |  DIFFERENTIATION    |
   target    |  win on price and   |  win on features,   |
   (whole    |  volume; margin     |  brand, quality,    |
   industry) |  from lowest cost   |  service; margin    |
             |  base               |  from a premium     |
   SCOPE     +---------------------+---------------------+
   NARROW    |  COST FOCUS         |  DIFFERENTIATION    |
   target    |  lowest cost within |  FOCUS              |
   (one      |  one segment only   |  tailored offer to  |
   segment)  |                     |  one segment only   |
             +---------------------+---------------------+

   Failing to choose  ->  "stuck in the middle": no cost advantage
                          and no reason for the customer to pay more.
```

| Strategy | Needs | Fails when |
|---|---|---|
| Cost leadership | Scale, high utilisation, tight overhead control, process efficiency, cheap inputs | A rival finds a lower cost base, technology resets the cost curve, or cost cuts damage the product |
| Differentiation | A feature customers will pay for; brand, quality or service capability | The premium exceeds what buyers will pay, imitation erodes the difference, or it stops mattering |
| Cost focus | Deep knowledge of one segment and a cost structure suited only to it | The segment is too small, or a broad cost leader serves it well enough |
| Differentiation focus | A segment with distinct needs, willing to pay to have them met | Its needs converge with the mainstream, or a broad player targets it |

**What the manager does with the output.** Commit. The model's value is that it forces a choice and
rules out being mediocre on both dimensions; the firm then aligns its value chain to the cell it
picked, since a cost leader and a differentiator do not need the same activities.

:::ladder A regional garment maker sells mid-priced clothing through its own stores. It has no scale advantage against national brands, and its prices are above the local unbranded tailors. Sales are flat. Advise on competitive positioning.
@weak: The company should reduce its costs and also improve its product quality so that it can
compete with both the national brands and the local tailors.
@good: Identifies the firm as stuck in the middle under Porter's generic strategies — neither lowest
cost, since it lacks scale against the national brands, nor differentiated, since it sells mid-priced
clothing with no distinguishing feature. It must choose one of the four cells rather than sit between
them.
@great: The same diagnosis with the facts named, then a reasoned choice. Cost leadership is
unavailable: the national brands hold the scale and the unbranded tailors the lower overhead, so a
firm this size cannot win on cost across the industry, which rules out both broad-market cells. Its
real assets — its own stores and its regional presence — are segment assets, so the viable cells are
the focused ones, most plausibly **differentiation focus**: a defined regional segment served with
fit, local styling, alteration and exchange service that neither a national brand nor a tailor can
match at that price. This is also why "cut cost and raise quality" is wrong; it deepens the stuck
position by spending on both dimensions at once. **Recommendation:** adopt differentiation focus on
the regional segment, realign the value chain to service and fit rather than volume, and accept a
smaller addressable market as the price of a defensible position.
:::

:::attempt Draw Mendelow's matrix, label the four quadrants with the strategy for each, and explain what management should do when a high-power stakeholder's interest suddenly rises.
Draw the grid with **power** on one axis and **level of interest** on the other, and name the
quadrants correctly: low/low **minimal effort**, low power–high interest **keep informed**, high
power–low interest **keep satisfied**, high/high **key players**.

The second half is the discriminating part. A high-power, low-interest stakeholder who becomes
interested moves from "keep satisfied" to "key player", so the treatment must change with the
position: that stakeholder must now be involved in the decision and the strategy must be acceptable
to them, not explained afterwards. Hence the model is dynamic — stakeholders are mapped **for a
specific decision**, not once for the organisation, and a strategy touching a lender's security or a
regulator's remit itself moves stakeholders into the key-player box. If a strategy is unacceptable to
a key player it must be modified, dropped, or the stakeholder brought along before commitment.
:::
